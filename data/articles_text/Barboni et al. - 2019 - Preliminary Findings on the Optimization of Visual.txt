Abstract
Biofeedback training has been used to improve fixation stability in subjects with central vision loss, but the psychophysiological mechanisms underlying the functional improvements resulted was not reported. The aim of this study was to investigate
the effects of microperimetric biofeedback training on different visual functions and self-reported quality of vision in subjects
with age-related macular degeneration. This case-control study included six subjects (72.0 ± 6.1 years of age) diagnosed
with age-related macular degeneration (wet or dry) with low vision (best corrected visual acuity ranging from 0.5 to 0.1 in
the study eye) and five healthy volunteers (64.2 ± 3.7 years of age). Ophthalmological and functional examinations were
obtained from all subjects twice with an approximately 3-month interval. Subjects with central vision loss performed 12 sessions (10 min each) of biofeedback training between the two examinations. Functional evaluation included: microperimetry,
spatial luminance contrast sensitivities, color vision thresholds, visual acuity, and reading speed. Visual performance during
daily activities was also assessed using a standardized questionnaire. The ratio (2nd/1st examination) of the spatial luminance
contrast sensitivity at lower spatial frequencies were much higher for the training subjects compared with the controls. In
addition, self-reported quality of vision improved after the training. The significant improvement of the visual function such
as spatial luminance contrast sensitivity may explain the better self-reported quality of vision. Possible structural and physiological mechanisms underlying this neuromodulation are discussed.
Keywords Biofeedback training · Psychophysiology · Visual system · Low vision · Macular degeneration

In normal observers, the central area of vision, or the
main fixation locus, is not exactly on the anatomical center
of the retina considering the center of the fixation (functional
fovea). Putnam et al. (2005) showed that the center of the
fixation locus is displaced approximately 50 µm (~ 0.16°),
usually to the bottom of the foveal pit, from the location
of the structural central retina—the point of highest cone
density. Besides the “random choice” of the visual system
that naturally occurs even in normal observers, there is usually a search for an alternative central fixation locus, known
as preferred retinal locus (PRL), in pathological conditions
such as those caused by macular impairment (Crossland
et al. 2005, 2011).
Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) causes macular
impairment due to a degenerative process of retinal neurons.
AMD is the most common cause of severe and irreversible
visual impairment in the elderly population (Chuang 1987).
The retinal lesions caused by AMD are responsible for the
perception of central dark spots (scotomas), resulting, very
often, in the involuntary replacement of the original PRL
to another unaffected (or less affected) area (for review see
Cheung and Legge) (Cheung and Legge 2005). However,
the new PRL is not always the secondary point with highest
sensitivity (Guez et al. 1993; Fletcher and Schuchard 1997).
Because of this, optimizing the visual abilities of AMD subjects by stimulating a retinal area with relatively better visual
sensitivity is a possibility, and we can precisely choose a
new PRL to be stimulated using microperimetry associated
with the biofeedback training (Nilsson et al. 2003).
The aim of the present study was to investigate the effects
of biofeedback training on different visual tasks (visual function and visual performance) in subjects with AMD. To
reach this objective, we selected a large battery of tests that
allowed us to assess specific aspects of vision. The battery
included the following: high contrast near VA using multiple
white optotypes presented on a black background (high contrast near VA), spatial luminance contrast sensitivity, color
vision test, reading speed, and a questionnaire addressing
the ability to perform daily activities to investigate the self reported quality of vision.

losses similarly in both dry and wet AMD eyes (Landa et al.

Methods

Microperimetry, or fundus-related perimetry, is a fundus tracking controlled visual field examination that allows to
project each stimulus on an exact preplanned retinal area in
order to provide a retinal sensitivity map even in subjects
without the ability to fixate a central target (Midena et al.
2010). The Macular Integrity Assessment System (MAIA;
CenterVue, Padova, Italy) was used for the microperimetric
measurements and for the biofeedback training. The system
is equipped with a scanning laser ophthalmoscope that provides real-time eye-tracking and eye movement compensation (Sunness et al. 1995).

All subjects underwent microperimetric examinations.
The Expert Protocol, applied monocularly, was evaluated
before and after the training period or within approximately 3 months in the healthy subjects. The results of the
Expert Protocol allow evaluating light sensitivity thresholds at different macular locations. The test grid consisted
of 37 points inside a concentric area of 5° from the central
point, meaning that the central area of 10° was examined,
with 12 points within each concentric ring (2°, 6°, and
10°) plus the measurement of one central point. Besides
evaluating sensitivity thresholds, the system allows recording fixation locations 25 times per second. For a description of the fixation recording and analysis in patients with
macular diseases, see (Mandelcorn et al. 2013).
Goldman-based standard light stimuli parameters
(size III white stimuli) and strategy (threshold 4–2) were
used. The maximum luminance of the stimulus (318 cd/
m2) allows a stimulus presentation ranging from 0 to 36
decibels (dB). The observer’s task was to press a button
to indicate the presence of the light spot whenever it was
detected, always keeping central fixation. Visual field
locations that required brighter stimuli to reach threshold showed reduced sensitivity, thus, lower dB sensitivity
values indicate lower sensitivity. Conversely, higher dB
values correspond to dimmer stimuli and represent higher
sensitivity.

Biofeedback Training
Biofeedback training is a relatively new method that has
been developed based on the brain plasticity (Tarita-Nistor
et al. 2009) and recently clinically established as a therapeutic alternative for patients with central vision loss. After
obtaining a retinal sensitivity map provided by the microperimetry, it is possible to find small retinal areas, usually more
eccentric areas, where visual sensitivity is more preserved
than central areas affected by diseases. This area with better
sensitivity is selected by the examiner and during the training sessions the subject is asked to move his/her fixation to
that area. In the present study, the biofeedback training was
planned to stimulate a new, previously chosen, retinal locus
with higher sensitivity compared with the original PRL.
The new retinal locus was chosen as the closest upper point
(in terms of retinal location) in which a higher sensitivity
threshold, compared with the original PRL, was detected
during the Expert Protocol. The aim of the biofeedback
training was to replace the original fixation locus with this
relatively better retinal point.
All subjects underwent 12 training sessions (each 10 min)
using the aforementioned MAIA system (Centervue, Padova,
Italy). During the training sessions, the task of the examiner
was to guide and to monitor the observer’s fixation. The
instruction was to move the eyes up, down, right, or left. The

observer moved his/her (non-covered) eye to the position

In addition to the examiner’s instructions, the audio (beep)

to fixate the desired location, the equipment changed from

to keep the fixation at that point as much as possible. This
procedure was repeated several times during the training
sessions.

Functional Evaluation
The functional evaluation of vision always was performed
monocularly before and after the training period for the
AMD subjects who underwent the biofeedback training and
twice with an approximately 3-month interval for the healthy
subjects. High contrast near VA, spatial luminance contrast
sensitivity, and color discrimination were measured using
self-written computerized programs installed on a portable
tablet device (Galaxy Tab S2 8″; Samsung, Seoul, South
Korea). The screen of the tablet was always placed 30 cm
from the observer’s eye.
For the high contrast near VA task, the stimuli were multiple white optotypes on a black background, as previously
described by González et al. (2007). The task of the subject
was to inform the direction (right, left, up, or down) of the
letter E at each stimulus presentation. From the size of the
letters (largest = 5 mm and smallest = 0.2 mm at each part
of the letter) and the distance of the test (30 cm), it was possible to calculate the respective visual angles ranging from
0.955° to 0.038°, the last representing the equivalent Snellen
of 20/46 or 0.4 LogMAR.
The spatial luminance contrast sensitivity function
was obtained using static achromatic stimuli (black and
white). The stimuli were checkerboards superimposed with
a Gabor and a Gaussian luminance function to “mask”
the outer and the inner edges of the stimulus. Six spatial
frequencies were tested: 0.25, 0.6, 1.2, 2.0, 4.7, and 9.3
cycles per degree (cpd). For all spatial frequencies, the
test started with a high luminance contrast (99%). The
mean luminance of the stimulus (75 cd/m²) was constant
and identical to the mean luminance of the homogeneous gray background. At each stimulus presentation, the
target appeared randomly either on the right side or on
the left side of the screen. The response was correct when
the observer touched the screen on the side where the
stimulus appeared. If the observer touched the opposite
side, the response was considered incorrect. At each correct response, the stimulus contrast decreased 5%, and
the stimulus contrast increased 6% with each incorrect

13

response. The goal of the test was to determine the lowest
contrast that the observer needed to identify the stimulus
based on the last five reversals of the stimulus.
The luminance contrast was calculated using the Michelson equation (Imax−Imin)/(Imax + Imin), which considers
the maximum (max) and minimum (min) luminance intensity (I) of the target. Contrast sensitivity, calculated as the
reciprocal of the contrast threshold, was therefore plotted
against the respective spatial frequency. The higher the values of the function, the better the contrast sensitivity.
The color discrimination test was developed based on
well-established classical procedures (Mollon and Reffin
1989; Rodriguez-Carmona et al. 2005). The visual stimuli
were pseudo-isoluminant mosaics of small spots in which
the target and background provided only chromatic differences without borders and with a random variation of the
luminance (mean luminance = 115 ± 60 cd/m²). The stimulus was presented dynamically, meaning that the chromatic
target was never on one specific place on the screen, but
constantly moving on the screen. The features of the stimulation ensured that the observer could only identify the target
if there was chromatic discrimination and not due to contour
clues, luminance differences, or visual adaptive processes.
The task of the observer was to identify the position of the
dynamic chromatic target against a background of constant
chromaticity (x = 0.3159, y = 0.3353; CIE 1931) on the
screen of a portable tablet device (Galaxy Tab S2 8″; Samsung, Seoul, South Korea). The response was given by the
observer using the touchscreen system of the portable device
to point to the exact location of the moving target. When
the response of the observer was correct, the chromaticity
of the target approached 10% of the background chromaticity, making it more difficult to identify the target’s position.
And, when the response was incorrect (or no response was
recorded after 3 s), the chromaticity of the target was moved
10% away from the background chromaticity, every 3 s, until
it could be identified again by the observer.
There are three types of cones in the human retina;
each type contains a specific visual photopigment that is
highly sensitive to a particular visible spectrum range:
long (L-cone: peak at ~ 560 nm), middle (M-cone: peak
at ~ 530 nm), and short (S-cone: peak at ~ 420 nm) wavelengths (Nathans et al. 1986). For each of the three color
discrimination test applied here, consecutively in a random
order, targets differ from the background along one specific
significant line in the color space: protan (L-cone sensitivity related), deutan (M-cone sensitivity related), or tritan
(S-cone sensitivity related). After six reversals of the stimulus, the threshold was calculated. The results were given in
chromaticity coordinates based on the color space diagram
CIE 1931. The result is the distance between the threshold
chromaticity and the chromaticity of the background. Lower
values correspond to better color discrimination.

Reading speed was assessed using a standardized Hungarian reading table (Radner Olvasótábla 1; Novartis Hungaria).
Two sentences of black letters on a white background, each
including three lines containing 24 characters, were used to
test reading speed. The letters were 0.06 and 0.08 equivalent
Snellen at a distance of 40 cm. The illumination of the reading page (215 lx) provided high luminance contrast between
the letters and the background. Subjects were instructed
to read the sentences as fast as possible without skipping
words. The time was recorded and words per minute was
calculated. If an error occurred, the subject was asked to
re-read the word.

Self‑reported Quality of Vision
The Hungarian version of VF-14, (Steinberg et al. 1994)
a translated and validated subjective brief questionnaire,
(Bausz and Németh 2006) was applied to evaluate the ability of performing daily activities. Questions regarded the difficulty of performing tasks such as reading, face recognition,
walking, driving, and home activities. Five response options
were available for each question: not difficult (4 points), a
little bit difficult (3 points), difficult (2 points), very difficult (1 point), and impossible (0 point). The scores were
calculated by summing the points of the first 12 responses.
The maximum possible score, which represented the best
possible performance of daily activities, was 48 points. The
lower the score, the worse the performance. The questionnaire was applied before and after the training period. In
addition, the subjects were asked to describe any significant
change in their visual performance perceived during the
training period.

Analysis
The results obtained before the training period were compared between the groups. Afterward, the ratio (2nd/1st
examination) between the results obtained in the first and
the second examinations was calculated for each variable
of each subject. The ratios were then compared between
AMD subjects who underwent the microperimetric biofeedback training and healthy subjects who did not perform the
training.

Results
AMD and Control Visual Performance Prior
to Training
All healthy subjects had a BCVA of 0.00 (LogMAR) in both
eyes, whereas AMD subjects had low BCVA in both eyes
(see Table 1). In the first examination, all microperimetric

parameters were worse for the AMD subjects (N = 6 eyes)
compared with the healthy subjects (N = 5 eyes). The average threshold (Fig. 1a) was, on average, 29.4 ± 0.7 for
healthy subjects and 17.9 ± 7.8 for AMD subjects. The
macular integrity (Fig. 1b) was 7.7 ± 3.0 for healthy subjects
and 99.0 ± 2.3 for AMD subjects. In addition, all healthy
subjects had stable fixation in both eyes according to the
Expert Protocol. Two subjects from the AMD group had
unstable fixation, and four subjects had relatively unstable
fixation in the study (better) eyes prior to the training period.
Parameters providing information about the fixation stability, such as P1, which considers the fixation points within
1° of eccentricity, and P2, which considers the fixation
points within 2° of eccentricity, were different. The healthy
subjects showed, on average, 99.4% ± 1.3% and 100% (all
subjects) for P1 and P2, respectively; whereas, the AMD
subjects showed, on average, 51.3% ± 29.9% and 85.0% ±
15.9%, for P1 and P2, respectively.
Figure 1 shows that, in accordance with the microperimetric sensitivity losses found in the AMD subjects, other
visual functions were significantly impaired. High contrast near VA (Fig. 1a) and reading speed (Fig. 1b) to both
0.06 eq. Snellen and to 0.08 eq. Snellen were lower for AMD
subjects compared with healthy subjects.
AMD subjects showed lower sensitivity to luminance
contrast compared with healthy subjects at all spatial frequencies (Fig. 1c). Color discrimination thresholds (Fig. 1d)
were impaired in AMD subjects at all axes tested: protan
(healthy = 74.0 ± 16.8 and AMD = 326.6 ± 337.2), deutan
(healthy = 66.8 ± 18.3 and AMD = 245.5 ± 174.5), and tritan
(healthy = 97.1 ± 42.8 and AMD = 356.5 ± 199.3).
Finally, the self-reported quality of vision to perform daily activities was lower for the AMD subjects
(average = 15.4 ± 7.8) compared with healthy subjects
(average = 46.4 ± 0.9).

Discussion
The present results confirm previous reports on visual
improvement after biofeedback training in AMD patients
(Vingolo et al. 2007, 2013; Amore et al. 2013) and further
show that biofeedback training may modulate specific visual
functions differently. The previous studies showing the positive effects of biofeedback training have mainly considered
clinical methods that are quite used to measure visual function and to classify patient as being normal or abnormal
from the ophthalmological point of view. However, clinical
methods such as visual acuity do not consider other visual
attributes such as contrast sensitivity and color discrimination which are known to be affected even in early stages of
AMD (Midena et al. 1997; Vemala et al. 2017). Therefore,

these visual functions may be taken into account not only to
identify markers for prodromal disease stages and/or disease
progression, but to rather investigate therapeutic effects.
Neuroplasticity of the adult human visual system has long
been considered for visual rehabilitation in several ophthalmological conditions (Martins Rosa et al. 2013). Structural
changes caused by AMD results in a change in fixation locus
and, consequently, functional alterations of the central vision
(Cheung and Legge 2005). Therefore, oculomotor and perceptual integration raise the possibility of stimulating a better retinal location to obtain positive functional effects, (Liu
and Kwon 2016) even in advanced macular lesions of elderly
adults (Nilsson et al. 1998; Vingolo et al. 2009; Ueda-Consolvo et al. 2015).
Although BCVA is usually well correlated with the quality of vision, this visual function alone does not always represent the general ability for certain daily activities. High
contrast near VA improved for all subjects performing the
biofeedback training. The present data corroborate results
from González et al. (2007) and further suggest this technique to be considered while following up the effects of the
biofeedback training in AMD subjects together with the
standardized clinical examinations.
Spatial contrast sensitivity function provides a more
detailed assessment of the spatial vision, and it may better predict the subject’s ability to perform daily activities
(Owsley and Sloane 1987). For instance, contrast sensitivity dysfunctions are present in AMD subjects, even in the
early stages of the disease (Midena et al. 1997). Peyrin et al.
(2017) have recently reported that lower spatial frequency
processing is more preserved than higher spatial frequency
processing in AMD subjects. The present data show that
luminance contrast at low spatial frequencies may be better modulated by the biofeedback training than the higher
spatial frequencies. We hypothesize that the displacement
of the preferred retinal locus to a more eccentric retinal area
with higher sensitivity would favor the luminance contrast
processing.
The AMD subjects tested in the present study, with
advanced AMD, showed reduced color discrimination
thresholds affecting the three axes tested: protan, deutan, and
tritan. Therefore, the present study corroborates the results
of a previous report (Vemala et al. 2017) showing color
discrimination impairment even in the very early stages
of AMD. We hypothesize that, initially, it may be directly
caused by the central impairment of the degenerated macula;
later, however, it may be worsened by the adoption of an
eccentric fixation point. There were two aims to investigate
the effects of the biofeedback training on color discrimination: first to verify a possible improvement of this visual
function, and second to make sure that with modulating the
fixation point to a more eccentric retinal area, the color discrimination would not become more altered. Although color

13

discrimination thresholds did not improve significantly after
the biofeedback training, they did not decrease. Most AMD
subjects showed relatively constant color discrimination
thresholds. The present data show, therefore, that the stimulation of a new preferred retinal locus changing the fixation
point to a more eccentric retinal area does not disturb color
discrimination thresholds for protan, deutan, and tritan axes
in AMD subjects.
Reading speed has been previously shown to be improved
after biofeedback training to replace the fixation to a more
eccentric area in patients with macular diseases (Nilsson et al. 2003; Vingolo et al. 2009). In the present study,
improvement of reading speed after the biofeedback training
was not statistically significant. We hypothesize that reading performance is a more complex visual function, and it
might be more influenced by personal experience than other
basic visual functions such as visual acuity or spatial contrast sensitivity. In this context, subjects more exposed to
reading situations might improve their ability to read more
than subjects who do not use this visual function very often.
Moreover, AMD subjects with better vision and, therefore,
better reading ability, were certainly exercising this visual
function more than the subjects with poorer reading ability. Further investigations might address this question by
considering a more homogeneous group to be evaluated.
Perhaps subjects showing more similar visual acuity and
similar reading habits would better show the effects of the
biofeedback training on the reading speed.
The scores of the questionnaire to assess visual performance in daily activities were significantly improved after
the biofeedback training in AMD subjects. Moreover, most
AMD subjects reported personal experiences in which they
perceived improvement of their visual ability to perform
daily activities. For instance, one subject who works at the
family’s bakery reported that after training she could better
see the numbers on the paper money, which helped her as a
cashier. Another subject who had complained about reading
difficulties reported in the last weeks of the training period
that the dark spot in the center of her vision was moving a
little bit to the side, and she was now able to fixate the letters of interest.
One limitation of this study is that the training was only
applied to AMD subjects. The healthy subjects studied
were all normal observers with very stable foveal fixation; therefore, it was not possible to apply the biofeedback
training in these subjects to compare with the results of
AMD subjects. On the other hand, the functional evaluation may be performed on a group of AMD subjects, without training, within an approximately 3-month interval.
This would clarify whether the improvements shown in
the present study were real effects of the training or just
a functional fluctuation of AMD eyes. Although in the
present study only a control group of healthy volunteers


[page 9]
Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback

was considered, it may not discredit the present findings,
since visual changes in AMD patients undergoing biofeedback training were found by comparing results before
and after the training. Now that evidence for improvement
of specific visual function is provided, next phase of the
study will focus on the visual functions modulated by the
biofeedback training, shown here, including AMD patients
with relatively stable fixation in order to stimulate fixation stability without changing the fixation point as well
as AMD patients not receiving the biofeedback training.
Another limitation is the low number of participants.
We were not able to include several subjects in the study
because of the long-term protocol. To take part in the
study, the subject had to agree to coming to the eye clinic
every week without intervals. Due to difficulties such as
advanced age and the fact that they required the help of
someone to get to the clinic, several subjects were not
able to take part in the study or to complete the training protocol. The possibility of having a higher number
of participants would allow us to study different groups
according to the type of AMD (dry or wet) and stage of
the disease. Further investigations may consider including more subjects to study a homogeneous group and to
be able to correlate the results with the severity as well as
the type of AMD.
In summary, we found improvement in the test results
of AMD subjects after the biofeedback training when
their pre- and post-training assessments were compared.
Thanks to a more detailed assessment of visual abilities,
it was possible to assess this improvement. The significant
improvement of visual function such as spatial contrast
sensitivity may explain the better self-reported quality of
vision.
We are aware that the preliminary data reported here
does not allow the generalization of the findings. At the
same time we assume that the clear perceptual as well
as self-reported improvements found in this small sample show important indications for researchers that might
worth the consideration in further studies.
