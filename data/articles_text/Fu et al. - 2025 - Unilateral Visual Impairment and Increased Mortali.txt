Purpose: The purpose of this study was to investigate the association between the
full spectrum of visual acuity categories and risks of all-cause, cardiovascular disease
(CVD)-related, cancer-related mortality in the UK Biobank study.
Methods: A total of 131,468 participants (aged 40–79 years) with visual acuity measurement from the UK Biobank prospective cohort were included. Visual acuities of both eyes
were used to assign each participant into one of the following groups: bilateral normal,
unilateral near normal, bilateral near normal, unilateral visual impairment (VI), mild VI,
and low vision. Cox proportional hazards multivariable regression models were used to
estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the risks of all cause, CVD-related, and cancer-related mortality.
Results: Over a median follow-up of 13.49 years, a total of 9372 deaths were recorded,
including 4682 cancer-related deaths and 1926 CVD-related deaths. Compared with the
bilateral normal group, participants with decreased visual acuity had increased risks of
all-cause and CVD-related mortality with dose-response-type gradients, but no elevated
risk of cancer-related mortality was observed. Decreased unilateral visual acuity but not
bilateral near normal vision was associated with increased risks for all-cause and CVD related mortality. For all-cause and CVD-related mortality, the adjusted HRs were 1.17
(95% CI = 1.09–1.25) and 1.24 (95% CI = 1.07–1.44) for the unilateral near normal group,
and 1.20 (95% CI = 1.14–1.27) and 1.26 (95% CI = 1.12–1.42) for the unilateral VI group.
Conclusions: Decreased unilateral visual acuity was associated with increased all-cause
and CVD-related mortality risks among participants without VI in the UK Biobank.
Translational Relevance: Achieving bilateral vision balance may contribute to reducing
the mortality risk of individuals with decreased unilateral visual acuity.

Introduction
Visual impairment (VI), encompassing conditions
from mild visual difficulties to complete blindness,
has a profound impact on individuals, their families,
and society.1,2 VI is associated with increased risks of
cognitive impairment, dementia, depression, psychosis,
and Parkinson’s disease, and with reduced quality

of life and loss of independence, posing a substantial public health challenge.3–12 Studies across diverse
populations and countries investigated the relationship
between VI and increased mortality risks.13–15 Some
studies also reported the association between VI and
cause-specific mortality risks, including cardiovascular disease (CVD)-related, cancer-related, and injury related mortality risks.16–18 However, most existing
studies focused primarily on individuals with severely

near-normal visual acuity. The gap may be partly

VI as visual acuity in the better eye worse than 6/12

Individuals with mildly reduced visual acuity
faced elevated risks of lacking educational qualifications, possessing higher deprivation scores, being
unemployed, holding lower-status jobs, living alone,
and experiencing mental health issues.19,20 These
challenges contribute significantly to societal burdens,
especially considering the large proportion of individuals with mildly reduced visual acuity worldwide. A
recent study involving 581,526 Korean adults found
that individuals with mildly reduced visual acuity
faced a 17% increased risk of all-cause mortality
among young to middle-aged population (average
age = 39.7 years), but not in the overall population or
among older adults.17 Thus, it underscored the need
for comprehensive studies that consider the entire
spectrum of visual acuity categories to better understand and address the broader impacts of visual acuity
on mortality.
On the other hand, in previous studies exploring
the association between VI and mortality risk, VI
was defined using various measures: presenting visual
acuity of the better eye,17 binocular presenting visual
acuity, or best-corrected visual acuity of the better or
worse eye.13 For each individual, monocular or binocular visual acuity was used to identify the vision level,
which, however, could not reflect the combined visual
acuities of both eyes. Individuals with visual acuity
above the VI threshold in one eye and below the threshold in the other eye were overlooked and classified into
the reference group along with those who had good
visual acuities in both eyes.
Fortunately, the UK Biobank Eye and Vision
Consortium offers a comprehensive classification
system that covers the full spectrum of visual acuity
categories by assessing the visual acuities of both eyes.
This system classified visual acuity levels above the
WHO VI threshold (equal to or better than 6/12) into
normal (6/6 to 6/9.5) and near-normal vision (worse
than 6/9.5 to 6/12), encompassing a spectrum from
bilateral normal vision to blindness. It further subdivides individuals within the WHO-defined non-VI
group into four categories: bilateral normal vision
(normal vision for both eyes), unilateral near-normal
vision (normal vision for one eye and near-normal
vision for the other), bilateral near-normal vision
(near-normal vision for both eyes), and unilateral VI
(above the WHO VI threshold for one eye and below

the largest prospective adult cohort with visual acuity
measurements and over a decade of follow-up,21 our
study aimed to investigate the associations between
the full spectrum of visual acuity categories and
mortality risks, including all-cause, CVD-related,
and cancer-related mortality risks. Additionally, we
explored these associations across age subgroups
(above and below 60 years) and by sex stratification.
We hypothesized that, even among individuals classified as non-VI according to WHO criteria, variations
in visual acuity levels would be associated with mortality risk, and that these associations would differ by age
and sex.

Methods
Study Design and Population
The datasets used in this study were obtained
from the UK Biobank (Application: 129237). The
UK Biobank is a large, population-based prospective cohort, in which more than 0.5 million participants aged 37 to 73 years attended one of the
22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and
Wales between 2006 and 2010 at baseline examinations (5.5% response rate).22,23 Between 2009 and
2010, approximately 117,252 participants underwent
the initial assessment visit including eye measurements. An additional 16,016 participants, who had not
received eye examinations at their initial visits, underwent eye assessments during the first repeat assessment
visit between 2012 and 2013.24 We defined the visit
with the first available visual acuity examination as the
“baseline”, either between 2009 and 2010 or 2012 and
2013.
The UK Biobank study was granted ethical
approval from the National Health Service (NHS)
North West Centre for Research Ethics, adhering to
the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki
(Ref: 21/NW/0157). This work used data provided
by patients and collected by the NHS as part of
their care and support. All participants gave written
informed consent and signed the informed consent
to be linked to national electronic health-related
datasets.

Classification of Visual Acuity Categories
Habitual distance visual acuity in each eye (i.e.
tested with the individual wearing any prescribed,
presently used optical correction) was measured to
assess real-life visual acuity rather than best-corrected

visual acuity (Field ID 5208 and 5201, measurement details in Resource 100250). Briefly, visual acuity
was measured using a traditional logarithm of the
minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) chart on a
computer screen under standard illumination.25
Each individual was assigned to one of six
mutually exclusive visual acuity categories based on
the LogMAR visual acuity of both eyes. According to the WHO definition, VI was defined as visual
acuity worse than 6/12 (> 0.3 LogMAR) in the better seeing eye. Above the WHO VI threshold, we defined
normal vision as visual acuity between 0 and 0.2 (6/6
to 6/9.5, Snellen equivalent), and near-normal vision
as visual acuity between 0.21 and 0.3 (worse than 6/9.5
to 6/12, Snellen equivalent). Considering the combined
visual acuities of both eyes, individuals not meeting
the WHO criteria for VI (i.e. the WHO non-VI group)
were subdivided into four groups: (1) bilateral normal
vision (visual acuity of 0–0.2 in both eyes); (2) unilateral near-normal vision (0–0.2 in one eye and 0.21–
0.3 in the other eye); (3) bilateral near-normal vision
(0.21–0.3 in both eyes); and (4) unilateral VI (0–0.3
in one eye and 0.31 or worse in the other eye). In
addition, individuals who met the WHO definition
of VI were classified as (5) mild VI (visual acuity
of 0.31–0.49 in the better eye), and (6) low vision
(visual acuity ≥ 0.5 in the better eye). The low vision
group included participants from the WHO moderate
VI (n = 966), WHO severe VI (n = 71), and WHO
blindness categories (n = 4), due to the small sample
sizes within each individual group. Detailed definitions
and the distribution of WHO VI classification and six
visual acuity categories are provided in Supplementary
Table S1.

Mortality and Follow-Up
The date of death (Field ID 40000) was obtained
from NHS Digital for participants in England and
Wales and from the NHS Central Register for those
in Scotland, with a data update period of every 3 or
6 months. It was used to determine the survival status
and follow-up endpoint. Participants not matched with
death certificates were considered alive. Participant
follow-up duration started at the date of baseline and
ended either at the date of death or October 13,
2023. The underlying cause of death (Field ID 40001)
was used to determine the specific cause of death
according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th
revision (ICD-10). CVD-related mortality was identified using ICD-10 codes I00 to I99, whereas cancer related mortality was determined using ICD-10 codes
C00 to D48.

Covariates Ascertainment
In this study, demographic characteristics (age, sex,
ethnicity, income, and education), lifestyle factors
(smoking and drinking status), medical history
(diabetes mellitus, hypertension, stroke, and heart
attack histories), and body mass index (BMI) were
collected. Sex was acquired from the central registry
at recruitment (Field ID 31). The baseline information
was collected during the baseline visit, including age
(Field ID 21003), income (Field ID 738), ethnicity
(Field ID 21000), education (Field ID 6138), smoking
and drinking status (Field ID 20116 and 20117),
medical history (hypertension, stroke, and heart attack
histories from Field ID 61500, and diabetes mellitus
from Field ID 2443) and BMI (kg/m2 , Field ID 61500).
BMI was imputed with mean value (missing percent =
0.50%) and categorized into four groups according to
the WHO BMI classification.21

Statistical Analysis
Participant characteristics were summarized utilizing descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were
presented as mean ± standard deviation or median
(interquartile range [IQR]), depending on the normality of data distribution, which was evaluated using
Q-Q plots. Categorical variables were presented as
frequencies and percentages (n, %). The standardized
mean difference was used to compare variables between
visual acuity categories, with a standardized mean
difference exceeding 0.1 indicating a significant difference in variables.
Cox proportional hazards regression was used to
estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence
intervals (CIs) for all-cause, CVD-related, and cancer related mortality in different visual acuity categories,
with the bilateral normal group as the reference. The
assumption of proportional hazards was evaluated by
statistical tests based on weighted Schoenfeld residuals,26 and the stratification method was used to deal
with the violation of variables sex and age. Model 1
was stratified with sex and age group (age ≥ 60 or <
60 years). Model 2 was adjusted for age and ethnicity based on model 1. Model 3 was further adjusted
for income, education, smoking and drinking status,
histories of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, stroke, and
heart attack, and BMI category based on model 2.
Based on our hypothesis and the violation of the
proportional hazard assumption, subgroup analyses
were performed with stratification by sex and age.
Several sensitivity analyses were carried out to
assess the robustness of our results. First, participants with cancer or CVD at baseline were excluded

Association of Visual Acuity Categories With
Cause-Specific Mortality
A total of 4682 cancer deaths (49.96% of all deaths)
and 1926 CVD deaths (20.55% of all deaths) were
recorded. Similar associations between decreased
visual acuity and increased mortality risk were
observed for CVD-related mortality as for all-cause

mortality (Fig. 2C). Individuals with decreased visual
acuity exhibited an increased risk of CVD-related
mortality, except for the bilateral near-normal vision
group, demonstrating a clear dose-response relationship (P for trend < 0.001). For cancer-related mortality, the strength trend and associations with decreased
visual acuity were observed in model 1 and model 2
(see Supplementary Table S2). However, the associations did not remain significant in the fully adjusted
model (model 3), with one exception of association
with unilateral VI, which remained significant after
adjusting all potential confounders (Fig. 2D).

Sensitivity Analyses
The results among the overall participants remained
largely consistent with the main analyses after excluding those with cancer or CVD at baseline (Supplementary Table S8), excluding participants who died within
the first 2 or 4 years (Supplementary Table S9), and
using Fine and Gray subdistribution hazard models
(Supplementary Table S10). However, the associations were slightly attenuated in some visual acuity
categories.

Discussion
In this prospective cohort of 131,468 participants
from the UK Biobank, we found that decreased visual
acuity, including WHO-defined VI, was associated with
increased risks of all-cause and CVD-related mortality.
Additionally, we believe that our study was the first to
report the association of increased risks for all-cause
and CVD-related mortality with slightly and significantly decreased unilateral visual acuity in the WHO
non-VI groups.

With the variations of visual acuity classification
methods, reference groups, and study population, there
were inconsistencies in findings across studies. In the
UK Biobank cohort, Zhang et al. found that participants with WHO-defined VI (presenting visual acuity
of the better eye worse than 0.3 LogMAR) had a
26% increased risk for all-cause mortality, combining those with mild VI and moderate to severe VI
as one group and setting the participants without
dual sensory impairment (VI and hearing impairment) as the reference group.28 Supporting this, we
also found that participants with WHO-defined VI
exhibited a 28% increased risk for all-cause mortality setting WHO-defined non-VI group as reference.
Additionally, we examined the associations between
WHO-defined VI and different VI levels (mild VI and
moderate to severe VI) with all-cause, CVD-related,
and cancer-related mortality, and compared the results
using different reference groups. For individuals within
the same visual acuity category, outcomes can vary
depending on the choice of reference group. In our
study, when bilateral normal vision was used as the
reference group, participants classified as mild VI
and low vision showed higher adjusted HRs for all cause mortality compared to when the WHO non-VI
group was used as the reference. Moreover, for participants with mild VI, an increased risk for CVD-related
mortality was observed only when bilateral normal
vision served as the reference group, but not when
the WHO non-VI group was used (see Supplementary Table S3). Of note, the impact of VI may be
underestimated when the WHO non-VI group was
used as the reference group because the WHO nonVI group included the bilateral normal vision, unilateral near-normal vision, bilateral near-normal vision,
and unilateral VI groups. In addition, for individuals
in the WHO non-VI group, the vision levels definition
methods may contribute to different results. Among
the young to middle-aged Korean population, Han et
al. used habitual distance visual acuity in the better
eye to categorize vision levels and found that individuals with mildly reduced visual acuity (between 0.5 and
0.8, decimal value) had an increased risk of all-cause
mortality, but not CVD-related mortality, compared
with those with normal vision (better than 0.8, decimal
value).17 In our study, which classified visual acuity
category based on the combined visual acuities of
both eyes, participants under 60 years with unilateral VI had increased risks of both all-cause and
CVD-related mortality, whereas those with bilateral
near-normal vision had an increased risk of CVD related mortality. The method of defining vision levels
based on the visual acuity of the better eye assumes
that individuals with decreased unilateral visual acuity


[page 9]

face the same mortality risk as those with good
visual acuity in both eyes, potentially overlooking the
increased risk among those with unilateral visual acuity
decline.
In our study, participants with decreased unilateral visual acuity (unilateral near normal and unilateral VI) and WHO-defined VI (mild VI and low vision)
faced with increased risks of all-cause and CVD related mortality. The association between VI and
mortality risk was supported in other studies,13–18 with
chronic stress being a potential contributing factor.
Zheng et al. suggested that increased allostatic load
(AL) served as a mediator between VI and elevated
all-cause and CVD-related mortality risks.18 AL is
a biomarker reflecting chronic stress and associated
with an increased risk for CVD incidence.29 Furthermore, high AL is associated with low socioeconomic
status and a lack of social support.30 Cross-sectional
studies using similar visual acuity categories also
showed that decreased unilateral visual acuity was
associated with lower socioeconomic status, lack of
support, and poorer general health.19,20 Individuals
with decreased unilateral visual acuity may experience
more significant chronic stress due to these socioeconomic, mental, and physical challenges, potentially
leading to an elevated AL that increases mortality risk.
Additionally, lower socioeconomic status and limited
support may reduce their accessibility to healthcare,
further contributing to the elevated mortality risk.
Unlike those with decreased unilateral visual acuity,
individuals in the bilateral near-normal vision group
did not exhibit lower socioeconomic status or limited
support compared with those with bilateral normal
vision.19 This may partly explain the comparable
risks of all-cause and CVD-related mortality observed
between the two groups. On the other hand, compared
with the unilateral near-normal vision (7.76%) and
unilateral VI (13.18%) groups, the relatively small
sample size of the bilateral near-normal vision group
(0.87%) may have contributed to the lack of statistically significant associations with mortality risk.
This finding warrants further investigation in other
populations with different distributions of visual acuity
categories.
In our study, older paticipants (aged 60 years and
above) with either unilateral near-normal or unilateral VI demonstrated increased risks for all-cause,
CVD-related and cancer-related mortality. Among
younger participants (under 60 years), those with
unilateral VI showed increased risks for all-cause
and CVD-related mortality. Previous studies have
suggested that limitations in daily living activities,
particularly walking ability, may mediate the relationship between VI and mortality in older adults.18,31,32

Furthermore, decreased unilateral visual acuity may
impair mobility in older adults due to imbalanced
visual acuity between the eyes. In younder adults,
only more severe unilateral impairment may similarly
hinder mobility and contribute to adverse health
outcomes. However, to our knowledge, no studies have
specifically examined the effects of decreased unilateral visual acuity or visual imbalance on daily living
activities. In further subgroup analyses stratified by
sex, unilateral VI was associated with CVD-related
mortality in older female subjects but not in older
male subjects. Human and animal researches suggest
that reproduction is a significant stress source for
female subjects, which would cause oxidative stress
contributing to elevated AL.33 The potential association between reproduction and AL, as well as the sexspecific difference in the cardiovascular biomarkers of
AL might partly explain the sex difference.30,34 The
mechanisms linking decreased unilateral visual acuity
to higher mortality risks are likely multifaceted, involving factors such as social status, access to healthcare,
mobility limitations, and overall physical health. The
UK Biobank Eye and Vision Consortium suggested
these visual acuity categories as a key sensory health
indicator, considering it both as a risk factor and a
health outcome.19 These complexities warrant further
investigation to better understand and address these
associations.
In existing studies focusing on VI, the decreased
visual acuity and the comprehensive assessment of
visual acuity in both eyes among individuals without
VI have been neglected. Our study revealed that the
classification of VI based on the visual acuity of the
better eye not only overlooked the adverse effects of
mildly reduced visual acuity, particularly in cases of
unilateral visual acuity decline, but also underestimated
the impact of VI when the WHO non-VI classification was used as the reference group. To advance
ophthalmology research and policy, a broader perspective on the visual acuity category method needs to
be considered. For instance, in our study, individuals
with unilateral near-normal vision and 82.81% of those
with unilateral VI were classified as having normal
vision when visual acuity category was determined by
the visual acuity of the better eye. As a result, the
increased mortality risk associated with impaired visual
acuity in these groups would have been overlooked.
Although the distribution of visual acuity categories
may vary across populations and countries, addressing the needs of previously neglected groups could
deliver significant public health benefits. In clinical
practice, ophthalmologists and optometrists should
consider not only focusing on enhancing the vision
of patients with severe VI but also addressing unilat-


[page 10]

eral visual acuity decline to achieve bilateral vision
balance, which may contribute to reducing mortality
risks. For example, in individuals with good visual
acuity in one eye but uncorrected refractive error in
the other, prescribing appropriate refractive correction (e.g. glasses or contact lenses) can help achieve
balanced bilateral visual acuity. In older adults with
bilateral cataracts, timely surgery for the fellow eye
after the surgery of the first eye needs to be considered.
To our knowledge, our study novelly reported
the association of the full spectrum of visual acuity
categories, which was assessed using combined visual
acuities of both eyes, with all-cause, CVD-related, and
cancer-related mortality. With the large-scale participants, rich data sources, and long follow-up time of
the UK Biobank, subgroup analysis and sensitivity
analysis were conducted to verify the result’s reliability with multivariable models. The comparison between
the associations of the visual acuity categories and
WHO VI classification with risks of all-cause, CVD related, and cancer-related mortality was investigated.
Nevertheless, several limitations of our study
warrant mention. First, because of the nonrandom
recruitment and low response rate of the UK Biobank
and the fact that the eye examination was performed on
a subset of the population, the distribution of visual
acuity categories in our study may vary among the
populations in the UK and other countries.22 However,
the “healthy volunteer effect” in the UK Biobank had a
limited effect on the investigation of participants with
mildly reduced visual acuity because of the relatively
less impacts caused by impaired vision. Second, the
limited numbers of participants with moderate VI
and severe VI precluded separate association analyses. These participants were considered as the low
vision group, leading to masking group differences.
Third, the specific eye diseases leading to decreased
visual acuity were not investigated in detail. All-cause
impaired habitual visual acuity was an overall visual
acuity outcome of diverse eye conditions and diseases,
which might have a better reflection than specific eye
diseases.
In conclusion, our study found that decreased
unilateral visual acuity was associated with increased
risks of all-cause and CVD-related mortality among
participants without VI in WHO classification in the
UK Biobank. Additionally, the associations between
decreased unilateral visual acuity and mortality risks
varied across different sex and age subgroups. These
findings highlighted the importance of adopting
a broader concept of visual acuity classification
method beyond VI in ophthalmology and visual
sciences. Integrating strategies to achieve bilateral

vision balance in clinical practice may enable ophthalmologists and optometrists to help reduce the mortality risk of individuals with decreased unilateral visual
acuity.
