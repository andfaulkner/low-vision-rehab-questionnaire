Depression screening among older adults attending low-vision
rehabilitation and eye-care services: Characteristics of those
who screen positive and client acceptability of screening
Edith E. Holloway and Bonnie A. Sturrock
Centre for Eye Research Australia, Royal Victorian Eye and Ear
Hospital, University of Melbourne, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia

Ecosse L. Lamoureux
Centre for Eye Research Australia, Royal Victorian Eye and Ear
Hospital, University of Melbourne, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia;
Singapore Eye Research Institute, National University of Singapore;
and Duke–National University of Singapore Graduate Medical
School, Singapore

Jill E. Keeffe and Gwyneth Rees
Centre for Eye Research Australia, Royal Victorian Eye and Ear
Hospital, University of Melbourne, Melbourne, Victoria, Australia

Aim: To investigate characteristics associated with
screening positive for depressive symptoms among older
adults accessing low-vision rehabilitation and eye-care
services and to determine client acceptability of depression
screening using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2
(PHQ-2) in these settings.
Methods: One-hundred and twenty-four older adults
(mean = 77.02 years, SD = 9.12) attending low-vision
rehabilitation and eye-care services across Australia were
screened for depression and invited to complete a
telephone-administered questionnaire to determine
characteristics associated with depressive symptoms and
client acceptability of screening in these settings.
Results: Thirty-seven per cent (n = 46/124) of participants
screened positive for depressive symptoms, and the majority
considered the new depression screening method to be a
‘good idea’ in vision services (85%). Severe vision loss
(<6/60 in the better eye) was associated with an increased
odds of screening positive for depressive symptoms (odds
ratio 2.37; 95% confidence interval 1.08–6.70) even after
adjusting for potential confounders. Participants who
screened positive had a preference for ‘talking’ therapy or a
combination of medication and ‘talking therapy’ delivered
within their own home (73%) or via telephone (67%).
Conclusion: The PHQ-2 appears to be an acceptable
method for depression screening in eye-care settings among
older adults. Targeted interventions that incorporate
home-based or telephone delivered therapy sessions may
improve outcomes for depression in this group.

Introduction
The link between vision impairment and depression in older
adults is well established, and studies have shown that higher
levels of vision impairment may be associated with increased
risk [1]. For a person with vision impairment, depressive
symptoms can lead to increased levels of disability and functional decline and a greater risk of developing major depression, which may act as a barrier to effective vision
rehabilitation outcomes [2,3]. Early identification and management of depressive symptoms in this group is therefore
critical.
The need to identify depression in high-risk groups has been
emphasised in screening guidelines, and the UK’s National
Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE) has recommended
routine depression screening for people with functional
impairment within health-care settings [4]. Studies conducted
in health conditions including epilepsy have yielded support
for depression screening [5] and have generally found a high
rate of patient acceptability, with a preference for brief
screening tools [6,7].
In low-vision rehabilitation services throughout Australia,
staff have undertaken training [8] to screen for depressive
symptoms using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2)
[9], a two-item measure that has been validated in previous
research and is widely used as a first-line screening instrument.
The PHQ-2 has recently demonstrated its clinical utility in
oncology settings [10] and with coronary heart disease
patients [11]. It has also demonstrated good sensitivity, specificity and ease of administration for older adults when compared with ‘gold-standard’ diagnostic interviews [12].
While depression screening is the first step to ensuring
patients gain access to treatment through appropriate referrals, screening alone is not sufficient to improve outcomes
[13], and greater emphasis has been placed in current screening guidelines on the importance of combining screening with
effective treatment and follow-up [14]. Adherence to treatment in older adults is frequently reported to be poor [15];
however, evidence suggests that patients who receive their
preferred treatment show greater improvements and adherence to treatment compared to those who do not [16]. A
better understanding of treatment preferences in this group is
therefore needed.

The primary aims of this exploratory study were the following: (i) to determine the proportion and characteristics of
participants who screened positive for depressive symptoms
in a sample of older adults accessing low-vision rehabilitation
and eye-care services; and (ii) to determine client acceptability of depression screening using the PHQ-2 in these settings.
A secondary aim was to identify preferences for psychological support services for those who screened positive.

Measures

Methods

Background
Age, gender, marital status, country of birth, languages
spoken, education level and general health were recorded via
self-report at the baseline assessment. Participants were also
asked to self-report if they had ever suffered from depression
(‘no’, ‘yes, in the past’, ‘yes, currently’) and if they had ever
received treatment for depression in the form of antidepressant medication, therapy or counselling (‘no’, ‘yes, in the past’,
‘yes, currently’).

Study design and setting
Participants were recruited from Vision Australia centres
across Victoria, New South Wales, Queensland and the Australian Capital Territory and the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear
Hospital (RVEEH) between November 2010 and July 2012
as part of a prospective longitudinal study exploring outcomes of depression screening [17]. This study reports on
findings from the baseline assessment.

Ocular health
The main cause of vision loss and visual acuity were
extracted from Vision Australia and the RVEEH patient files.
Visual acuity was assessed using logarithm of the minimum
angle of resolution (LogMAR) charts at Vision Australia and
the Snellen chart at RVEEH. Visual impairment was categorised as mild (<6/12–6/18), moderate (<6/18–6/60) or severe
(<6/60).

Participants and procedure
To be eligible for the study, participants were required to
have visual acuity <6/12 in the better eye (best corrected), be
aged 60 years or older, be able to converse in English, have
adequate hearing (with or without a hearing aid) and have no
cognitive impairment as determined by a score of ≤7 on the
six-item Cognitive Impairment Test (6CIT) [18].
Potential participants who met visual acuity and age eligibility criteria were identified by Vision Australia staff during the
new intake assessment, in which the PHQ-2 [9] was administered by Vision Australia staff as a standard component of
intake. Following depression screening, consent to be contacted by the research team was obtained by Vision Australia
staff. Consenting clients were contacted by the research team,
who completed a cognitive assessment. At the RVEEH site,
patient records were reviewed by a research assistant who
completed the PHQ-2 with eligible patients waiting for their
clinic appointment. Across all sites, clients who screened
positive for depressive symptoms were advised that their
responses warranted further follow-up and were offered a
referral letter to discuss depression with their GP. Educational materials (e.g. beyondblue resources) about depression
and services available in the community were also offered. All
eligible clients were invited to take part in the study, and a
baseline assessment was scheduled within 1 week of depression screening with consenting clients. A research assistant
completed the baseline assessment via telephone to determine
demographic characteristics, acceptability of depression
screening and preferences for psychological support services.
Ethical approval was received from the Human Research and
Ethics Committee of the RVEEH (project no. 09/923H).
Written consent was obtained from all eligible participants,
including permission to access ophthalmology and low vision rehabilitation reports.

Depressive symptoms
Participants were screened for depression using the PHQ-2
[9]. The PHQ-2 consists of the first two items of the PHQ-9
[19] (see Appendix I), which assess depressed mood and loss
of interest (anhedonia) over the previous 2 weeks. At least
one of these symptoms is required to establish a diagnosis of
major depressive disorder (MDD) or any depressive disorder
listed in the DSM-IV. PHQ-2 scores can range from 0 to 6,
and a score of ≥3 suggests clinically significant depression,
which should prompt either completion of the full PHQ-9 or
a clinical interview to assess for MDD [20]. A score of ≥3 has
been shown to have sensitivity as high as 83% and specificity
of 92% for major depression [9]. The PHQ-2 has been
validated in previous research for use in older adults [12].
Acceptability of depression screening
The Patient Acceptability Scale (PAS) [21] was used to determine the extent to which participants felt ‘annoyed’, ‘embarrassed’, ‘upset’ or ‘uncomfortable’ in answering the twoquestion screening tool. Response options ranged from 1
(very much) to 4 (not at all). Participants were also asked to
indicate if they thought it was a good idea to screen for
depression (‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unsure’).
Perceived need for psychological help
Participants were asked to indicate the extent to which they
felt the need for help on a single item rated on a five-point
Likert scale. A higher score indicated a greater perceived need
for psychological help.
Preferences for psychological treatments
To determine preferences for psychological treatment, participants who screened positive for depressive symptoms
were asked to indicate (i) which of four treatment options
was their first preference, (ii) which was their second preference and (iii) if any were unacceptable choices [22]. All
participants, regardless of their treatment preference, were

Analysis
Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS version 19.0.
The cut-off score for the PHQ-2 was ≥3, indicating depressive symptoms warranting further clinical attention (i.e. a
positive screen). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test of normality
was statistically significant (P < 0.05), indicating that the data
were not normally distributed and non-parametric tests were
warranted. Chi-square and non-parametric tests (e.g. Mann–
Whitney U) were used to compare between-group differences
(positive versus negative depression screen) for each of the
sociodemographic variables. To identify factors associated
with depressive symptoms, exploratory analyses were performed. Variables identified as associated with the outcome
in chi-square and non-parametric tests (P < 0.05) were
entered in univariate regression models. A multivariate logistic regression model was performed to determine if level of
visual acuity remained associated with depressive symptoms
after adjustment for potential confounders. Odds ratios and
95% confidence intervals for a positive depression screen
relative to a negative screen are reported.

Results
Participants and response rates
The files of 234 clients were reviewed for visual acuity, of
whom 33 (14%) did not meet the eligibility criteria. Of the
eligible clients, 42 (21%) declined to be screened for depression, three (2%) did not meet other eligibility criteria and a
further 32 (16%) were screened but declined to take part in
the study; of the latter, 41% (n = 13) screened positive for
depressive symptoms. In this paper, we report on the baseline
data from 124 eligible participants.
The participants ranged in age from 60 to 94 years (mean =
77.02, SD = 9.12); most were female (n = 88, 71%) and
reported having at least one chronic health condition besides
their eye condition (86%; Table 1). The most prevalent
causes of vision loss were age-related macular degeneration
(51%) and glaucoma (18%), and the majority (83%) had
mild or moderate vision loss.
Proportion and characteristics of participants who screened
positive for depressive symptoms
Thirty-seven per cent (n = 46/124) of participants screened
positive for depressive symptoms, and no differences were
found in the proportion who screened positive at Vision
Australia centres (n = 38/106; 36%) and the proportion who
screened positive at the RVEEH (n = 8/18; 44%) (P = 0.33).
Participants who screened positive were more likely to have
severe vision loss (χ2(df = 2, n = 124) = 4.73, P = 0.04), have
current depression (χ2(df = 2, n = 124) = 49.71, P > 0.001)
and currently be receiving treatment for depression (χ2(df = 2,

Univariate logistic regression analyses showed that participants with severe vision loss had an estimated 2.37-fold
increase in the odds of screening positive for depressive symptoms compared to those with mild vision loss (95% CI
1.08–6.70; P = 0.04) (Table 2). This association remained
significant in a multivariate model after controlling for age,
gender, history of depression, treatment status and perceived
need for psychological support (OR 7.41; 95% CI 1.59–
34.62; P < 0.001). History of depression, treatment status
and a perceived need for psychological support were also
associated with a positive depression screen in univariate
analyses (all P < 0.05; Table 2).
Acceptability of depression screening
The majority of participants believed it was a ‘good idea’ to
screen for depression at eye-care and vision rehabilitation
services (85%). Most participants rated the questions of the
PHQ-2 as ‘easy’ (45%) or ‘very easy’ (41%) to answer and
reported not feeling embarrassed (96%), upset (96%),
uncomfortable (96%) or annoyed answering the questions.
Participants who screened positive did not differ in their level
of acceptance towards depression screening compared to
those who screened negative (all P > 0.05).
Preferences for emotional support and
psychological treatments
Participants who screened positive for depressive symptoms
(n = 46) preferred ‘talking therapy’ from a counsellor or
psychologist (29%) or a combination of medication and
‘talking therapy’ (33%). Fewer participants had a preference
for antidepressant medication (20%) or no treatment (18%).
In general, participants were accepting of most settings for
receiving ‘talking therapy’, including at home (73%) and via
telephone (67%). Around half reported they would be happy
to receive sessions in a therapist’s office (56%) or a Vision
Australia centre (46%), while only 7% were accepting of
sessions over the Internet.

Discussion
This is the first study to report on the acceptability of depression screening at low-vision rehabilitation and eye-care services among older adults. On a brief, well-validated screening
tool, administered largely by trained low-vision rehabilitation staff, over a third of participants scored above the clinical cut-off for depressive symptoms, warranting further
clinical attention. Encouragingly, many participants indicated little negative affect following administration of the
screening questions. Over half of those participants who

The frequency of patients who screened positive for depressive symptoms in our sample was slightly higher than that
reported in other low-vision samples [2,23]; however, those
data were often gathered in general community rather than
eye-care settings. In low-vision rehabilitation settings,
seminal work examining the frequency of depression using a
formal diagnosis found that 34% of older adults (versus 37%
who screened positive for depressive symptoms in our study)
met the criteria for major or subthreshold depression [3]. In
the current study, 86% of participants who screened positive
for depressive symptoms also self-reported current depression, while 87% who scored <3 reported no current diagnosis. Taken together, these results provide information about
the plausibility of a positive screen and point to the PHQ-2 as
a useful first-stage screening tool. However, as we did not
include a formal diagnosis of depression, we cannot know
with any certainty how many false positive and false negative
screens are among our data.
We found that poorer visual acuity was associated with a
positive depression screen. It may be the level of disability
associated with the vision loss that increases the risk of
depression rather than visual impairment itself. For example,
a recent study using a probability-based sample showed that
physical limitations and social isolation were the mechanisms
by which older adults developed depression, irrespective of
the degree of vision loss [24].
The majority of participants who screened positive preferred
‘talking’ therapies, rated as the treatment of choice alone or
in combination with antidepressants. This is consistent with
research demonstrating that psychological treatments for
depression are favoured over antidepressant medication in
older adults [22]. Psychological therapies have also been
shown to be a more effective treatment strategy for depressive symptoms that do not meet the full diagnostic criteria,
known as subthreshold depression, levels of which are commonly experienced by vision-impaired adults [3]. Our findings showed that two-thirds of participants who screened
positive would be happy to receive ‘talking’ therapy over the
phone. Due to barriers such as lack of available and accessible services, transportation problems and cost, treatment
adherence to psychological therapies is often reported to be
low in primary care [25]. Telephone-administered therapy in
addition to face-to-face sessions via home visits or video
conferencing, particularly important for those with hearing
difficulties, may provide suitable delivery modes to older
vision-impaired adults for whom travel is a challenge.
Several limitations and caveats exist in interpreting these
findings. Previous studies have shown depression to be a
barrier to seeking low-vision services [3], and as such, the
rates of depression and severity of symptoms may be lower in
this sample, which was largely recruited from low-vision

services. As noted, the percentage of positive screens was also
quite high in those who declined to participate in the
research. This may leave a knowledge gap in terms of the
characteristics of those clients not completing the PHQ-2
(hence not invited to take part in the study) and how these
differed from those of the invited clients. Furthermore, as the
majority of our sample had not yet received low-vision rehabilitation services, we did not assess service use. Future
research in this area should determine the impact of rehabilitation services on depressive symptoms. Due to the small
sample size, we were unable to stratify the data exploring
treatment preference by exposures such as history of depression, current treatment and perceived need for psychological
help, as these factors may also influence treatment preferences. Individuals with a cognitive impairment were excluded
from this study; however, the co-occurrence of depression
and cognitive impairment among older adults is common,
highlighting the importance of identifying depression in this
group. Vision rehabilitation and eye-care providers should
also consider training staff in the use of depression screening
tools that have been validated for use in individuals with
dementia. These instruments generally require more time to
administer than briefer tools such as the PHQ-2 [26].
Despite these limitations, this study provided a novel method
for identifying depression that involved internal screening by
trained staff of eye-care and low-vision rehabilitation providers. This study was not designed to evaluate the psychometric
properties of the PHQ-2, and the current presence of depression was determined via self-report. Future studies could
provide data on the clinical utility of the PHQ-2 compared to
a ‘gold-standard’ instrument for use in older adults with
vision impairment. The positive predictive value of a PHQ-2
score ≥3 has been reported to be around 34% [27]. Therefore, eye-care providers could also consider a two-step
approach adopted by the American Heart Association (AHA)
Prevention Committee, where the PHQ-9 [19] is administered for confirmation of a positive PHQ-2 result [28]. An
important benefit of this two-stage approach is that many
false-positive PHQ-2 screen results could be eliminated, as
has been shown in previous studies [29].
In conclusion, this study provides evidence to support acceptability among older adults of depression screening using a
brief tool, the PHQ-2, administered by staff at eye-care and
low-vision rehabilitation providers. The findings from this
study provide preliminary data on the types (and modes of
delivery) of treatment that would be acceptable to vision impaired older adults. These preferences may be useful in
informing the development of appropriate and effective treatment to be used in conjunction with screening tools. We are
currently integrating an evidence-based treatment for depression (Problem-Solving Therapy for Primary Care, PST-PC)
into low-vision rehabilitation settings [30]. Staff will be
trained to deliver PST-PC via telephone to those clients who
screen positive for depressive symptoms on the PHQ-2 and,
subsequently, the PHQ-9. Integrating such services into low233

Key Points
• Older adults with vision impairment are at
increased risk of depression, and methods to
improve the detection of depression are needed.
• The study provides evidence to support the acceptability of depression screening in low vision rehabilitation and eye-care services among older adults.
• Depression screening should be combined with
appropriate treatment, and future studies should
investigate such services in low vision rehabilitation settings.
