Abstract
Aim: To evaluate the relationship between Preverbal Visual Assessment (PreViAs)
results and cerebral visual impairment (CVI) diagnosis.
Method: This single-center retrospective chart review included children who
completed a CVI interdisciplinary clinic or occupational therapy vision evaluation
between May 2018 and May 2023 and had a completed PreViAs. Data were extracted
from the electronic medical record. Data analysis included sensitivity, specificity,
area under the curve, and Fisher's exact test.
Results: Of the 205 charts screened, 98 met study criteria (81 with CVI, 17
without); ages ranged from 3 to 50 months. The mean age of children with CVI
was 15.41 months (SD = 8.93) and without was 13.24 months (SD = 5.98). Among
the PreViAs domains, visual processing showed the highest frequency of concern
(CVI = 94%; non-­CVI = 76%), while visual attention had the lowest (CVI = 72%; non-CVI = 41%). Visual processing demonstrated the highest sensitivity (93.8%), whereas
visual attention showed the best balance of sensitivity (71.6%) and specificity (58.8%).
Area under the curve ranged from 0.59 to 0.66. All domains demonstrated significant
differences between children with and without CVI (Fisher's exact test, p < 0.05).
Interpretation: Effective screening tools are critical for detection of CVI. The
PreViAs is a sensitive screening tool in children at high risk for CVI. Children with
concerns on the PreViAs should be referred for an interdisciplinary CVI evaluation.

Cerebral visual impairment (CVI) is the most common cause
of bilateral visual impairment in children in high-­income
countries.1 CVI is a neurological condition caused by damage or malformation to the areas of the brain responsible for
processing visual information before, during, or shortly after
birth.2,3 The most common etiologies of CVI include periventricular leukomalacia, congenital brain malformations,
hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, intraventricular hemorrhage, and preterm birth.4–6 CVI can also result from genetic
or metabolic disorders, central nervous system infections,
and traumatic brain injuries. CVI commonly co-­occurs with
other neurological conditions and developmental disorders,

such as cerebral palsy, with as many as 60% to 70% of children
with cerebral palsy also having CVI.7 Additionally, seizures
are a prevalent co-­occurring disorder, affecting up to 50% of
children with CVI.8 This strong evidence linking common
conditions to an increased risk of CVI makes a compelling
argument for proactive CVI screening to enable early intervention and educational accommodations to improve developmental outcomes.9
The presence of CVI can negatively impact development,
including cognition, language, and motor skills.9,10 CVI
is a heterogeneous condition with varying levels of severity and symptoms depending on the degree of neurological

conditions. Children with CVI may experience difficulties
recognizing colors, shapes, and reading, leading to delayed
learning.11,12 They may struggle with visual attention, tracking moving objects, and processing visual stimuli, particularly
in crowded or unfamiliar settings.9,13 This can make certain
environments, like classrooms, feel visually overwhelming.
Children with CVI learn new information differently from
those without CVI. Typically developing children learn by
observing their surroundings, interacting with people, and
exploring visually, much of which happens through incidental
learning, where knowledge is gained unintentionally during
everyday activities and interactions. In contrast, children with
CVI often depend on other senses, such as touch and hearing,
to compensate for their impaired functional vision. Children
with CVI require more targeted instruction and guidance for
most aspects of learning, motor exploration, and social interactions.14,15 Therefore, early screening and detection of CVI is
crucial for timely, targeted interventions that maximize neuroplasticity and improve overall quality of life.15
Although the prevalence of CVI is increasing, CVI is often
not diagnosed until preschool age or later.16 Late diagnosis
leads to missed opportunities for vision-­specific, early intervention services.17 A validated caregiver questionnaire is essential for effective CVI screening to enable earlier detection
and diagnosis. CVI screening helps clinicians understand who
should undergo a more comprehensive, interdisciplinary CVI
evaluation.18,9 The Preverbal Visual Assessment (PreViAs) is
a caregiver screening questionnaire used to describe observations of visual behaviors of infants 24 months developmental
age and under across four domains: visual attention, visual
communication, visual motor coordination, and visual processing.18 Using a sample of 220 infants, the PreViAs developed age-­dependent cut-­off scores to identify infants with
delayed visual maturation. It has been validated in children
aged 2 to 24 months.19 To date, the PreViAs has not been tested
as a screening tool for infants at high risk for CVI. Therefore,
the study aimed to evaluate the relationship between PreViAs
results and CVI diagnosis using retrospective data. The ethics
board at our institution approved this study (IRB 2014–6879).

following criteria: (1) medical history consistent with

acuity, contrast sensitivity) not attributed to anterior visual

For scoring, 1 point is given for each visual behavior demonstrated, with a potential overall score of 30. The maximum
scores for each domain are as follows: visual attention is 11,
visual communication is 5, visual motor coordination is 13,
and visual processing is 20. The PreViAs has validated age-dependent cut-­off scores for each domain, categorizing each
child into one of three concern categories: no concern, continue to monitor (MON), or area of concern (AOC).19

Fisher's exact test with the Freeman–Halton extension was
used to analyse the effect of each of the four PreViAs domain
outcomes on CVI diagnosis. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Area under the curve, sensitivity, and specificity analyses were calculated. Wilcoxon
rank-­sum tests were used to compare PreViAs outcomes between children with and without CVI within each age group
(0–12 months, 13–24 months, and 25+ months). Data analysis was conducted using SAS statistical software, version 9.4
(SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). The two-­sided significance
level for all analyses was set at 0.05.

DISC US SION
Early screening tools with high sensitivity and specificity
are crucial for detecting CVI. To our knowledge, this is
the first study to explore the relationship between PreViAs
results and CVI diagnosis. Our group utilized retrospective
data to gain a preliminary understanding of the utility
of the PreViAs as a screening tool within this population.
Our preliminary data suggest that the PreViAs effectively
identifies children who would benefit from a CVI diagnostic
evaluation, warranting further prospective studies.
As anticipated, our data showed that the PreViAs questionnaire is sensitive in detecting CVI in young children
with concern. We hypothesized that AOC alone would
be the most sensitive model; however, combining levels of
concern (AOC + MON) demonstrated the highest sensitivity. Our results demonstrated that the PreViAs can differentiate young children with CVI from those without CVI.
These findings are similar to previous studies demonstrating that the PreViAs differentiated between delayed visual
maturation and typically developing individuals.19 Our data
indicates that all children screened using the PreViAs and
scoring as AOC or MON in any domain should be referred
for a comprehensive CVI diagnostic evaluation. Based on the
results of this study, our institution has modified its clinical
screening approach to refer all children who exhibit any level
of concern on the PreViAs for a CVI evaluation.
Screening tools are developed to detect conditions such
as CVI and other pediatric eye disorders early in life, before
clinical signs become apparent or are recognized by clinicians. Screening aims to promote early detection and minimize the risk of missed diagnoses. Most screening tools
maximize sensitivity to identify all individuals with a specific condition to refer them for further evaluation. High
sensitivity of the PreViAs questionnaire ensures that fewer
children with CVI are missed, facilitating timely interventions for optimal developmental outcomes. Early evaluations
are preferable to avoid late diagnoses and missed intervention opportunities. Lower specificity of CVI screening tools
can result in over-­referral of individuals who are unlikely to
have CVI. Since the primary aim of CVI screeners is identifying and referring children for additional testing, higher
sensitivity is favored, even at the expense of specificity.
When examining the PreViAs results across all visual
domains for children with CVI, our findings show that the
frequency of concern and highest sensitivity were observed
for the visual processing domain (94%, 93.8%), followed by
visual motor coordination (89%, 88.9%), visual communication (84%, 84%), and visual attention (72%, 71.6%) respectively. A significant portion of our sample exhibit various
brain abnormalities, potentially resulting in impaired visual attention.20,21 Visual attention is an early, foundational
visual skill that underlies participation in daily activities.
For children with CVI, there are multiple factors that can
negatively affect visual attention, including visual fatigue,
sensory environment, activity familiarity, and environmental clutter.10,22 Although we suspected that visual attention

   

| 693

would be the domain with the highest frequency of concern,
we were surprised to find that it had the lowest frequency
and sensitivity; however, it demonstrated the most favorable
balance between sensitivity and specificity, making it a crucial domain for early screening.
For successful, early screening of CVI, a questionnaire
should be short, understandable, and easy to administer in a
busy setting. The PreViAs questionnaire, with 30 simple yes/
no questions, is a clinically useful tool for early CVI detection. It can be completed electronically or on paper, making
it accessible for caregivers. It is feasible to use in high-­risk
settings, such as neonatal intensive care unit follow-­up clinics, and applies to children who are developmentally aged
24 months and under who may be at risk for CVI. Studies
show strong internal consistency across its visual domains,
ensuring its reliability.18,23 Alternative methods for early
screening of vision impairment, primarily used in inpatient
settings and requiring direct interaction with young children, may be more challenging to implement in busy, outpatient clinical settings.24,25 Caregiver questionnaires offer
several advantages over screeners that require direct administration, including convenience, time efficiency, accessibility, and cost-­effectiveness. Future studies should compare the
PreViAs results with direct administration vision screening
tools to understand the correlations between the two.
We divided our participants into three smaller age groups
to explore whether there were group differences (CVI vs non-CVI) in the mean scores of PreViAs. Formal analysis was
completed for all groups (0–12 months, 13–24 months, and
25+ months). Our results showed meaningful differences between groups in all PreViAs domains in the 0-­to 12-­month
group (CVI vs non-­CVI), whereas only two domains (visual
communication and visual attention) were statistically different in the 13-­to 24-­month group and no domains in the
25+ months group. It should be noted that the 25+ months

caution. These findings align with those of Kim et al., who
used the PreViAs to evaluate visual maturation in typically

normative scores by domain.26 They reported that the maturation age of certain visual perceptual skills occurred later
in the second year of life. Specifically, the visual motor coordination domain matured at 24 months and visual processing at 21 months. This might explain why the visual motor
coordination and visual processing domains were not statistically different in our study's 13-­to 24-­month group. The
visual perceptual skills that Kim et al. reported maturing in
the first year of life were visual attention at 6 months and
visual communication at 12 months.26 These data support
our results in that visual attention and visual communication were significant in both the 0-­to 12-­month and 13-­to
24-­month groups. It may be easier for caregivers to notice
delays in visual attention and visual communication during
the first 12 months since these domains mature earlier. Since

It is important to note in this study the PreViAs was
administered as part of a CVI evaluation in children who
were referred because of concern for CVI or who had an
existing diagnosis of CVI. As such, the PreViAs was used
to characterize visual development rather than as a stand-alone screening tool. It was typically completed within 1 to
6 months after a CVI diagnosis or as part of the CVI diagnostic evaluation. However, as shown in our data (Table 1),
many children in our study had identifiable conditions,
putting them at higher risk for CVI early in life. This suggests that the PreViAs could be tested prospectively as a
true CVI screening tool in future studies for children with
high-­r isk conditions. Caregivers of children with diagnoses known to increase the risk for CVI could complete the
PreViAs early in life to ascertain if more comprehensive
diagnostic CVI testing is warranted. This approach would
allow for a more accurate determination of the sensitivity
and specificity of the PreViAs within a broader, high-­r isk
population. Interestingly, the PreViAs was completed for
two children with minimal risk for CVI. These children
were ultimately diagnosed with anterior visual pathway
disease and not brain-­based visual impairment. Of note,
the PreViAs appropriately screened these children as negative for CVI.
The PreViAs screening questionnaire demonstrated clinical usefulness in our study. Limitations of this study include
the retrospective nature of this study and small sample size
at a single institution. Our data exhibited wide 95% confidence intervals for several odds ratios which likely reflect
the small sample size and limited number of non-­CVI cases
(especially in the 25+ months group). Our findings are preliminary and should be confirmed in larger, higher-­powered
studies. Additionally, this study was exploratory in nature
with the primary objective to identify potential associations;
therefore, we did not apply formal multiple testing adjustments (e.g. Bonferroni, Holm) to control for the family-­w ise
error rate. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted with
caution as they may have increased risk of type one error inherent in exploratory analysis and should be confirmed in
larger, prospective studies.
Our sample was derived from children who were referred
for a CVI evaluation; therefore our sample was skewed toward those who received a diagnosis of CVI. Additionally,

C ONC LUSIONS
This study is the first to evaluate the PreViAs screening
questionnaire among children with suspected CVI. Our
results provide preliminary evidence that the PreViAs is a
sensitive screening tool for detecting CVI early in life. Our
data shows that children who score in the AOC or MON
categories on any visual domain of the PreViAs should be
monitored closely and referred for a comprehensive CVI
evaluation for early diagnosis.
Although this study is limited by its retrospective design
and small sample size skewed toward CVI diagnoses, it is the
first study to explore the relationship between the PreViAs
and CVI diagnosis. Future studies should prospectively
utilize the PreViAs as a screening tool in a broader, high-risk group of children with medical conditions commonly
associated with CVI. Future prospective studies should also
examine the correlation between the PreViAs and other
measures of vision function and functional vision.
