PURPOSE. Visual screening is important in public health, but visual acuity (VA) testing
is costly at scale. We used items from a validated VA survey with 100 yes/no questions
to identify screening questions for low vision (VA ≤ 0.3 logMAR or 20/40) and legal
blindness as defined by the VA criterion (VA ≤ 1 logMAR or 20/200).
METHODS. We analyzed data from 385 adults with measured VA who completed the survey.
Participants were classified positive if they answered “no” to selected single, pair, and
triplet items for each threshold. We computed sensitivity, specificity, and area under the
curve (AUC).
RESULTS. For low vision, “In a fast-food restaurant, can you read the posted menus?”
yielded AUC = 0.85 (sensitivity = 0.89; specificity = 0.81). For legal blindness, “When
seated in front of a computer, are you able to read the letters on the keyboard keys?”
produced AUC = 0.80 (sensitivity = 0.89; specificity = 0.71). For both screening thresholds, adding a second item increased AUC by ≤0.05; a third item gave no further improvement.
CONCLUSIONS. One targeted question can screen for low vision or acuity-defined legal
blindness with good discrimination; a two-item pair offers a compact option when higher
specificity is desired. These brief, functional screens are practical when in-person VA
testing is impractical.
Keywords: low vision, legal blindness, visual acuity, screening, public health

V

isual screening is a cornerstone of public health because
early detection of vision impairment not only enables
timely clinical intervention but also facilitates access to critical support services such as Social Security benefits, rehabilitation programs, and community-based resources. Visual
acuity (VA)—measured using standardized tools such as the
Bailey–Lovie, Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study
(ETDRS), or Snellen charts and expressed in Snellen or logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) units—is
the most common method to assess a person’s vision status.
Clinically in the United States, low vision is defined as a
best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of 0.3 logMAR (20/40)
or worse in the better-seeing eye,1 whereas legal blindness
is defined as a BCVA of 1 logMAR (20/200) or worse in the
better-seeing eye or a visual field of 20° or less, as codified
by the U.S. Social Security Administration.2 In the present
study, we operationalized legal blindness exclusively by the
VA criterion (VA ≤ 1.0 logMAR). These definitions form the
basis for determining eligibility for various resources and
interventions that can significantly improve quality of life.
In many settings, particularly in a remote study or large scale public health studies, it is often impractical to perform
comprehensive clinical VA assessments on every participant.

In such cases, researchers often rely on self-reported screening questions to estimate vision status. Some commonly
used vision-related screening questions include the following question: “Are you blind, or do you have serious difficulty
seeing, even when wearing glasses?,” which has been used
in the American Community Survey (ACS) since 2008 and
the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System core module
since 2013. Another question is “Are you blind or unable to
see at all?,” which has been administered by the National
Health Interview Survey since 1999. Although self-reporting
is cost effective, its precision compared to gold-standard clinical VA measurements is frequently questioned. This challenge is underscored by several studies that have directly
compared self-reported vision with objective VA measures
using well-defined survey questions to probe individuals’
perceived vision status or vision abilities.3–6
For example, Wittenborn et al.3 conducted a validation study comparing telephone survey responses from 438
patients who had their VA tested at University of Washington clinics. Their survey included the ACS question, “Are
you blind or do you have serious difficulty seeing, even
when wearing glasses?,” to screen for both low vision and
legal blindness. The results indicated that the ACS ques-

Further illustrating the challenge, Whillans and Nazroo4
examined data from over 5000 participants in the Irish
Longitudinal Study on Ageing. They analyzed responses to
the question, “Is your eyesight (using glasses or corrective
lenses as usual) excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor or
blind?” They categorized “fair,” “poor,” or “blind” as self reported visual impairment. When assessing the ability of
this question to identify individuals with visual impairment,
defined as VA worse than 0.5 logMAR (∼20/60), they found
it performed poorly overall (AUC = 0.58). Despite correctly
identifying most individuals without objective impairment at
this level (specificity = 0.92), the question missed many of
those with impairment (sensitivity = 0.25), highlighting the
limitation of using such general self-ratings for screening at
the better VA threshold.
Other studies have tested self–report visual function
questions—focused on everyday visual tasks—to estimate
vision status. For example, Owen et al.5 evaluated the 25–
item National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire-25
(NEI–VFQ–25) in a sample of 1807 older adults recruited
from 20 U.K. general practices. Visual impairment was
defined as a BCVA worse than 0.3 logMAR. Participants
answered the question, “At the present time, would you
say your eyesight, using both eyes (with glasses or contact
lenses, if you wear them), is excellent, good, fair, poor,
very poor, or are you completely blind?” Those who
responded “poor,” “very poor,” or “completely blind” were
3.64 times more likely to have VA worse than 0.3 logMAR
compared with those who responded “excellent,” “good,” or
“fair.” Reporting problems on the social function subscale—
specifically, difficulty interacting with people or visiting
unfamiliar environments—was associated with 1.84–fold
higher odds of low vision, the largest effect among the eight
subscales.
Potter et al.6 analyzed data from 4999 participants in the
2022 National Health and Aging Trends Study. To detect
low vision (VA ≤ 20/40), they used two yes/no functional
questions targeting distance VA: “Can you recognize someone across the street when wearing glasses or contacts?”
and “Can you watch television across the room when wearing glasses or contacts?” This two-item screen produced a
sensitivity of 0.20, a specificity of 0.97, and an AUC of
0.58, indicating weak performance when using proposed
functional vision questions to determine low vision
status.
The reviewed studies above highlight a significant
challenge: Commonly used self-reported vision questions,
whether focused on general eyesight or broader functional
categories, often demonstrate limited accuracy and variable
performance when compared against clinical VA measurements. This discrepancy underscores the need for improved
screening methods, particularly in settings where clinical
assessments are impractical. Although the reviewed research
above3–5 evaluated existing survey questions against specific

VA thresholds, many of those questions were not initially
constructed to target the functional performance associated
with different acuity levels.
To address this challenge, the current study evaluates
items from our previously developed 100-question visual
acuity survey.7 Unlike general self-report questions, this
survey consists of items purposefully designed based on
everyday tasks requiring different VA levels (e.g., “Would
you be able to see the eye of a needle if you hold it at arm’s
length?"). The survey development and validation employed
item response theory (IRT) to establish a strong correlation between performance on these tasks and measured
VA. Therefore, this study aimed to leverage this task-based,
acuity-linked item bank to determine which specific survey
items, individually or in combination, can effectively identify individuals with low vision (VA ≤ 0.3 logMAR) or legal
blindness (VA ≤ 1 logMAR). We evaluated the diagnostic
performance of each item using sensitivity, specificity, and
receiver operating characteristic (ROC)–derived AUC metrics
to capture classification accuracy.

METHODS
Participants
This study utilized data from a cohort of 385 participants
whose recruitment and detailed characteristics were previously described.7 Participants were recruited from four U.S.
institutions between June 2019 and February 2022. Inclusion
criteria required participants to be 18 years or older, have
available VA data, and be able to communicate in English
to complete the 100-item visual acuity survey. All participants were naïve to the items at enrollment. The recruitment aimed to include individuals across a broad spectrum
of visual function; the measured binocular VA for this cohort
ranged from −0.3 to 2.0 logMAR (mean ± SD, 0.58 ± 0.44
logMAR). VA with habitual correction was assessed using
standardized charts (Bailey–Lovie, ETDRS, or Snellen). When
both monocular and binocular measurements were available, we used binocular VA; when only monocular measurements were available, VA from the better seeing eye was
used. Ocular diagnoses were derived from physician exam
notes and participant self-report entries, which were generally consistent with one another but may still be subject to
recall bias.
Full details regarding the recruitment procedures, specific
inclusion/exclusion criteria, VA measurement protocols,
data collection methods, and comprehensive characteristics
(including demographics and diagnoses) can be found in
the original publication.7 Institutional review board approval
was obtained at all participating sites, and all participants
provided informed consent prior to data collection. This
study utilized the 100-item visual acuity survey, which
assesses a person’s self-reported ability to recognize details
of everyday objects at specific viewing distances (e.g., seeing
the eye of a needle held at arm’s length). The full list of
questions is available in the Supplementary Material and our
previous publication detailing the development and validation of the survey.
Items were generated iteratively by the research team
to span a wide range of acuity demands and emphasize
broadly familiar U.S. scenarios. The item pool was informally piloted with normally sighted and low-vision individuals, and wording and content were refined accordingly. A
key objective for the current analysis is identifying individ-

≤ 1.0 logMAR). To achieve this, we leveraged the items from
the survey validated previously, which used IRT to establish a strong correlation between participants’ measured
VA and their IRT-estimated visual abilities across a broad
acuity spectrum (∼0.15–1.47 logMAR). The wide range of
the item bank offers choices for selecting better items for
different screening thresholds. Five items showed suboptimal IRT model fit in that analysis, but the overall survey
displayed good reliability. We therefore applied the established linear relationship from that prior work to transform
the IRT-derived difficulty of each survey item into a corresponding VA threshold (the 50% probability point for a “yes”
response), enabling us to evaluate the potential of each item
for screening at our target acuity levels.

Evaluation of Item Screening Performance
Three primary evaluation metrics were used to assess screening performance: sensitivity, specificity, and AUC. Sensitivity measures the percentage of participants classified by
the screening questions as likely to have low vision or
legal blindness who truly have VA worse than the corresponding threshold, indicating the ability to correctly identify these conditions. Specificity represents the percentage
classified as likely not having low vision or legal blindness who truly have VA better than the threshold, reflecting
the ability to correctly identify those without these conditions. For a single survey item, a “no” response classified
the participant as likely having low vision or legal blindness, and a “yes” response classified them otherwise. For
pairs or triplets of items, the primary classification rule
used was that only a response pattern of “no” to all items
in the set classified the participant as likely having low
vision or legal blindness; any “yes” response classified them
otherwise.
The overall screening ability was evaluated using the
AUC derived from the ROC curve. Because each screening
has only one decision threshold (classifying a participant
as either meeting or not meeting the VA criterion), the ROC
reduces to a single point, and the AUC simplifies to the average of sensitivity and specificity. This single number represents the chance that, when one participant’s VA is worse
than the threshold and another’s is better, the screening rule
tags the worse VA participant as positive and the better VA
participant as negative. An AUC of 1.0 means it always makes
this distinction, whereas 0.5 indicates chance performance.

Item Selection Steps
To evaluate whether a person’s VA was worse than 0.3
logMAR (low vision) or 1.0 logMAR (legal blindness), we
assessed the performance of individual items, two-item
pairs, and three-item triplets using sensitivity, specificity,
and AUC metrics. The item selection process proceeded as
follows:
1. One-item selection—For each screening threshold
(0.3 and 1.0 logMAR), sensitivity, specificity, and AUC
were computed separately for each of the 100 survey
items. The 20 items with the highest AUC at each
threshold were retained as primary candidates for
subsequent pairing.

2. Two-item pair selection—Each of the top 20 items
was paired with each of the remaining 99 (yielding 1980 unique combinations per threshold). Screening metrics were recalculated for each pair. For
these pairs, a response pattern of “no” to both
items indicated low vision or legal blindness. The
AUC metric, which combines information across all
response patterns, was used to rank the pairs, and
the 20 pairs with the highest AUC at each threshold
were selected for further evaluation.
3. Three-item triplet selection—Finally, potential three item combinations were evaluated. Each of the top 20
two-item pairs was combined with each of the remaining 98 individual items. For these triplets, a response
pattern of “no” to all three items was considered as
low vision or legal blindness. The AUC was calculated
for each triplet based on this classification approach
relative to the participants’ true VA status, and the 20
triplets with the highest AUC at each threshold were
identified.

RESULTS
Participants
This analysis included 385 participants from a cohort
described previously.7 The prior publication summarizes the
overall characteristics, but Table 1 provides specific breakdowns by age category and VA level for the sample. The

DISCUSSION
This study evaluated selected items from the 100-item visual
acuity survey, which was created to capture the functional
impact of VA on daily tasks, as a potential vision-screening
tool. Each item and item combination were assessed with
three standard diagnostic accuracy measures (sensitivity,
specificity, and AUC) to identify the best sets for detecting
low vision (VA ≤ 0.3 logMAR) and legal blindness (VA ≤
1.0 logMAR). A major strength of our approach is that the
survey items were specifically tailored to capture real-world
visual challenges related to VA, thereby enhancing clinical
relevance and potential utility in public health settings.
Our visual acuity survey offers flexible options for various
purposes, including low vision and legal blindness screening. To determine the best strategy for item selection, a stepwise method was employed in the current study. Initially,
we identified the top 20 individual items based on the
AUC at each screening threshold. Subsequently, we examined all possible two-item combinations by pairing each of
these primary items with every remaining item and then
selected the top 20 pairs based on their improved diagnostic performance. For both low vision and legal blindness
screening, the 10 items with the highest AUCs demonstrated
performance better than past studies. The top 10 pairs
further increased overall screening performance. The results
provide a bank of items for further selection for different
future applied scenarios. For example, if a population-based
public health study aims for the best categorization results
by asking just one question, then “Q97. In a fast-food restaurant, can you usually read the posted menus?” could provide
the most effective outcome for low vision screening, and
“Q30. When seated in front of a computer, are you able to
read the letters on the keyboard keys?” for legal blindness
screening. If a dedicated health-screening tool has space for
two questions, the best-performing pair can be selected to
enhance overall screening accuracy.
The selection of screening items and metrics should be
guided by the specific application context and goals. For
example, if minimizing missed cases (false negatives) is
paramount, such as in initial community screenings, prioritizing items or pairs with the highest sensitivity is crucial,
even at the cost of potentially more false positives as represented by a lower specificity. Conversely, situations with
limited follow-up resources or high costs associated with
false positives may warrant prioritizing items with the highest specificity. Although AUC offers a summary of overall discriminatory ability, practical implementation often
requires balancing these specific trade-offs. To examine the
marginal effect of adding items beyond sensitivity and specificity, we also examined positive predictive value (PPV), the
probability that a positive screen truly meets the VA threshold, and negative predictive value (NPV), the probability that
a negative screen truly does not meet the VA threshold, for
the top 10 items, pairs, and triplets. For low-vision screening,
PPV was already high with one item (0.93) and increased
only slightly with two (0.96) and three items (0.98), indicating minimal marginal gain. For legal blindness screening, the
mean PPV increased from 0.40 (one item) to 0.48 (two items)

to 0.54 (three items), again with smaller improvement from
the third item than from the second. NPV remained high and
stable for legal blindness (∼0.95 across one to three items)
and decreased modestly for low vision as item sets lengthened (from 0.582 to 0.576 to 0.537). Overall, a two-item
screen appears to offer the best balance between brevity and
predictive performance, with one item often being sufficient
for short screening and three items providing only marginal
additional benefit.
A notable observation from our single-item screening
results for low vision is the prominence of reading-related
tasks. Specifically, six out of the top 10 performing individual screening questions (Q97, Q46, Q15, Q12, Q21, Q17)
directly involved reading in various contexts, such as reading
menus, license plates, newspapers, street signs, credit card
numbers, and time on watches. This finding aligns with past
literature highlighting that reading is commonly and significantly affected by vision loss, often from an early stage,8 and
is frequently cited as the primary functional complaint in low
vision rehabilitation.9,10 The strong performance of these
reading-centric questions in our study further underscores
their relevance and potential utility in effectively screening
for low vision.
When compared to prior studies using general self–report
questions or functional questions to screen for visual impairment, our targeted functional items appeared to demonstrate better diagnostic accuracy, although direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to inherent differences in study samples and designs. Nonetheless, examining similar metrics provides context for our findings. Earlier
studies reported AUC values ranging from 0.56 to 0.80 for
screening for low vision or legal blindness. In contrast, our
highest performing single item achieved AUC values up to
0.85 for low vision and 0.80 for legal blindness screening,
whereas our best–performing pairs achieved AUC values
up to 0.87 and 0.82, respectively. Furthermore, comparing our best single item (Q97) for low-vision screening
with findings from the NEI-VFQ-25 study further highlights
the potential strength of targeted functional questions. Our
calculated odds ratio of 3.28 (95% confidence interval, 2.6–
4.17), representing 3.28 times increased odds of having low
vision given a “no” response to Q97, indicates a substantially stronger association with low vision status than the
1.84 odds ratio reported by Owen et al.5 for their social functioning subscale.
Even with promising, although imperfect, improvements
in screening accuracy from our functional items, it is also
important to consider the comparison between self-reported
visual function and in-person VA assessment. Although VA
measurement with an eye chart is the gold standard for
classification, electronic health record VA data quality and
usability have been shown to vary across documented VA
measures, observations, and eyecare subspecialties.11 Additionally, self-report measures provide an opportunity to
capture an individual’s perception of their ability in daily
life beyond the VA. One of the trade-offs when relying
on self-report vision disability is less accurate categorization results and overestimated prevalence of visual impairment.12 However, the subjective dimension is critical, as
past research indicates that self-perceived visual function
often predicts vision-related impacts on different aspects
of daily life, such as falls,13 mental health, and well-being,
more strongly than clinical measurements alone.14,15 Thus,
even alongside improvements in screening accuracy, self report functional data remain valuable for understanding the

broader personal and public health consequences of vision
impairment that extend beyond acuity numbers.
Despite these promising results, we acknowledge several
limitations. First, our screening questions target low vision
(VA ≤ 0.3 logMAR) and legal blindness as defined by VA
(VA ≤ 1.0 logMAR) and do not detect legal blindness due
to visual-field loss alone (e.g., ≤20°). As in many studies that estimate blindness prevalence using VA thresholds,16,17 our VA-based items may misclassify individuals
with preserved acuity but severely constricted visual fields.
In addition, diagnosis-specific visual deficits may also influence responses to particular items (e.g., central vision loss
in AMD vs. peripheral field loss in glaucoma), potentially
producing item-level differences even at similar VA. Future
work should incorporate items designed to reflect functional consequences of field loss (e.g., navigation in crowds,
bumping into obstacles outside central vision) and validate combined VA- and field-oriented item sets against gold standard perimetry.
Second, our study sample of 385 participants is relatively
small compared to population studies, and its composition,
with fewer normally sighted individuals than the general
population, may limit the generalizability of our findings to
broader screening applications. Further studies are needed
to validate these items in larger, more diverse cohorts.
Third, the performance metrics reported here are based
on responses collected within the context of the full 100item survey. It is possible that the performance of individual items or short combinations might differ if administered
independently, outside the context of the longer survey. The
current study provides a list of items that can be tested for
their performance in future studies.
Fourth, some of the best-performing items identified for
screening rely on familiarity with specific objects or settings.
Examples include question Q97, which refers to reading
fast-food menus, and question Q30, involving letters on
computer keyboards. This reliance might limit their applicability or necessitate alternative item selection for public
health screening in populations with widely varying environmental or technological exposure. The need for such
culturally or environmentally specific items underscores
the difficulty in creating universally applicable functional
screening tools. Accordingly, external validation in other
countries, with cultural/linguistic adaptation and empirical
item assessment, is warranted. At the same time, several
items from our item bank may be more context agnostic
(e.g., recognizing a facial expression across a residential
street, seeing one’s unpainted toenails), which can guide
localization. Careful cross-national item selection or adaptation may be necessary when screening diverse populations.
In conclusion, our study demonstrated that a targeted set
of functional vision screening items can effectively identify
low vision and acuity-defined legal blindness, outperforming
past self-report questions. The survey provides a variety of
items that can be employed in different research or clinical
settings for efficient screening.
