Janet P. Szlyk, Joan Stelmack, Robert W Massof,

Abstract: The purpose of this study was to develop an instrument that would be
sensitive to the visual difficulties of persons with low vision and that could be
used to evaluate outcomes and plan vision rehabilitation services. Toward that
end, the Veterans Affairs Low Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire was administered to 117 participants in rehabilitation programs in Veterans Affairs
medical centers and in the private sector. The results demonstrate that the questionnaire is an effective instrument for measuring vision difficulties in persons
who receive low-vision rehabilitation.

Approximately 240,000 new cases of visual impairments (that is, low vision and
blindness) occur each year in the United
States, and this number is expected to
double over the next 25 years, according
to estimates cited in an epidemiological
review by Massof (2002). Given that
most of the diseases and conditions that
result in visual impairments are more
prevalent in older people, the aging of

A number of questionnaires have been
developed to assess the quality of life of
or the ability to perform daily tasks by individuals who have vision problems (for a
review, see Massof & Rubin, 2001). However, the main emphasis of the design of
the items and their scales has been on
evaluating visual function in clinical triJournal of Visual Impairment & Blindness, May 2004

such as cataract (Mangione et aI., 1992).
Most measures have been validated
against traditional measures of visual
function, including visual acuity and visual fields, using correlation analyses that
have yielded acceptable but not high correlations (Haymes, Johnston, & Heyes,
2000). The correlations are not expected to
be high. As has been pointed out (Massof
& Rubin, 2001), redundancy should be
avoided, and the participants' self-report
measures should provide wider profiles of
functional performance than can be gained
from laboratory tests of letter recognition
and spot-luminance detection.
The aim of the research reported here
was to use this effective means of obtaining more information from people with
visual impairments and apply it to persons with low vision who are undergoing
vision rehabilitation. The intent was to
build a scale of items that are directly addressed by, and affected positively by, rehabilitation strategies. The methods used
in this study were different from those
used in previous work with low-vision
participants in that we maintained a
strong clinical orientation to vision rehabilitation. We were interested in developing a scale with clearly written questions
about the performance of daily tasks that
participants could easily understand. Another goal was to incorporate subcomponents of the items related to (1) whether
any difficulty was the result of visual
problems, (2) whether the participant was
interested in receiving training for that
activity, and (3) how the participant performed the activity. Even if an activity or
task is difficult to perform, if the participant is not interested in training in it,
then the activity or task becomes much

less important for developing the participant's rehabilitation curriculum.
In addition, we used the Rasch rating scale model (Wright & Masters, 1982) to
construct a yardstick from the ordinal
data provided by the questionnaire. The
Rasch technique is optimal for developing
an understandable ranking of tasks (for
example, from the least to the most difficult). It has been used successfully in the
development of interval scales, such as
difficulty in assessing the activity levels of
people who are blind (Becker, Lambert,
Schulz, Wright, & Burnet, 1985; Schulz,
Lambert, & Becker, 1985), the importance
of accomplishing daily goals by persons
with low vision (Massof, 1998), and the
self-perceived ability for independent mobility in those with vision loss that is due
to the inherited retinal degenerative disease retinitis pigmentosa (RP) (Turano,
Geruschat, Stahl, & Massof, 1999). The
ranking represents a useful yardstick to
gauge a participant's functional growth
after a rehabilitation program.
A pilot study (Stelmack, Szlyk, Joslin,
Swetland, & Myers, 2000), in which the
authors applied the National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire-25
(NEI VFQ-25, 1996) in a Veterans Affairs
(VA) setting, demonstrated some limitations in its use with persons who are undergoing vision rehabilitation. In this earlier work, Stelmack et aI. found that 77
legally blind veterans from the Edward
Hines VA Blind Rehabilitation Center, an
inpatient program, reported statistically
significant improvement on 30 of 39 items.
The 9 items on which there was no significant improvement dealt with overall
health, ocular pain, driving, socializing,
and grooming. Stelmack et al. concluded
that because the inpatient program does

[page 3]
not focus on the rehabilitation of overall
health or ocular pain, a change on these
indexes would not be expected. In addition, the severity of the participants' vision losses precluded any training in driving. Another previous study in the senior
author's laboratory showed no relationship between vision and either socializing
or grooming (Szlyk et aI., 2001).
Furthermore, Stelmack et aI. (2000)
found that almost one-fourth of the questions on the NEI VFQ-25 werenot relevant
to the study group. The also found that the
older participants were confused about
the directions and the wording of some
of the items and whether the questions
should be answered according to their
corrected (eyeglass or contact lens) visual acuity or their visual acuity with
low-vision devices. The goal became to
develop a visual functioning questionnaire that would be appropriate for the
aging participant of a low-vision rehabilitation program that could be used to
design the participant's program and
could provide a standard to measure
progress.
This article reports the results from the
baseline testing of participants who were
enrolled in vision rehabilitation programs
within the VA or in private-sector clinics.
It includes the psychometric properties
associated with each scale.

Methods
PARTICIPANTS

The Veterans Affairs Low Vision Visual
Functioning Questionnaire was administered to 117 participants (97 men and 20
women), who ranged in age from 29 to 93
(mean age = 72 years). The participants
were recruited from four rehabilitation
©2004 AFB, All Rights Reserved

programs: (1) the Visual Impairment
Center to Optimize Remaining Sight
(VICTORS) Program at the Chicago VA
Health Care System, West Side Division, a
short-term inpatient regional program that
serves veterans who have less-severe vision
loss than legal blindness (that is, a visual
acuity of 20/200 or worse and/or a visual
field diameter of 20 degrees or less) (20 participants), with visual acuity typically ranging from 20/40 to 20/200; (2) the Hines
Blind Rehabilitation Center, described earlier (49 participants), with participants
whose visual acuities are typically 20/200
and worse; (3) the Edwin and Lois Deicke
Eye Center, a nonprofit agency providing
low-vision services in the private sector (32
participants), serving participants with a
spectrum of visual acuities from 20/40 and
worse; and (4) the Massachusetts Eye and
Ear Infirmary, a hospital-based rehabilitation program in Boston (16 participants),
serving participants with a range of visual
acuities that is similar to the Deicke Eye
Center program. All the participants gave
their informed consent to participate. The
protocol was approved by the VAand University of Illinois at Chicago Institutional
Review Boards.

There were four (a-d) subcomponents to
each question. The first subcomponent
(a) asked the participant to rate his or her
difficulty with a particular task. The rating
scale consisted of six response categories:
(1) "not difficult," (2) "slightly difficult,"
(3) "moderately difficult," (4) "extremelydif-

ficult," (5) "impossible," and (6) "do not do
it for nonvisual reasons." If the participant
responded "1," the interviewer would skip
to the d subcomponent of the question. If
the participant responded "6," the interviewer would skip to the next question.
The second subcomponent (b) asked the
participant if any difficulty expressed in
subcomponent a was because of her or his
vision; the participant responded yes or no.
If the participant responded no, the interviewer would skip to the next question.

The third subcomponent (c) asked the participant if he or she wanted training in the
activity; the participant responded yes or no.
The fourth subcomponent (d) asked the participant how he or she usually performed the
task. There were five response categories for
subcomponent d: (1) "with my own eyes,"
(2)"with vision devices/techniques" (a magnifier), (3) "with my other senses or non vision
devices" (for example, a cane), (4) "someone
helpsme,"and (5) "not applicable."
The questionnaire was administered to
participants by telephone before their admission to the rehabilitation programs.
The interviewer received consent from the
participants by telephone, and the participants signed the consent form when they
were admitted to the rehabilitation programs. As was stated earlier, data were
analyzed with a Rasch rating-scale
model (Wright & Masters, 1982), using
WINSTEPS Version 3.16 Analysis Software. Correlational analyses were then used
to relate the transformed Rasch measures.

The item "reading street signs and

for reading signs (see Figure 1). Perhaps

Another seeming discrepancy is that although the participants most commonly
rated reading small print on labels as extremely difficult and wanted rehabilitation
training for it, they scored identifying
medicine as only slightly difficult and did
not want training for it (see Figure 5). The
two items may seem to be similar, in that
medicines likely have small-print labels;
however, medicines come in different formulations, shapes, and sizes and sometimes in potentially identifiable containers.
Tactile variation may explain the difference in responses between these two items.
IMPLICATIONS FOR PRACTICE

The results show that this questionnaire is an effective instrument for strati Journal of Visual Impairment & Blindness, May 2004

[page 14]
fying and measuring visual difficulties in
individuals who receive low vision rehabilitation. The Rasch analytic technique
was critical in the development of a reliable yardstick with which to measure a
participant's progress after participation
in a program. Consistent with Massof's
(1998) findings that recreational reading
requires the most visual ability and self care requires the least, we found that reading small print on package labels and reading newspapers were the most difficult
tasks for the participants in our study and
that keeping clean and getting dressed
were among the least difficult tasks. The
aim of Massof's study was to develop a
hierarchy of importance, before persons
undergo low-vision rehabilitation, for
achieving daily goals independently. Our
study showed that getting around in familiar places, such as the home, was not difficult for the participants. This finding is
consistent with the finding by Turano et al.
(1999), which used Rasch analysis, and that
"moving about in the home" required the
least visual ability for participants with RP.
Use of the Veterans Affairs Low Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire and tracking whether a participant prefers reading or
mobility training (see Figure 7) would provide a clearer direction for planning a customized rehabilitation program.
IMPLICATIONS FOR RESEARCH

In future research, we will assess the reliability of the rank ordering of items after
rehabilitation. This will be a further test of
the integrity of the difficulty scale. The
work by Turano et al. (1999) to define a
mobility scale for participants with peripheral vision loss and the two dimensions of reading and mobility defined in
our study emphasize the need to develop

274

Journal of Visual Impairment & Blindness, May 2004

interval scales that are based on the predominant visual profiles in study groups.
In' this study, the participants' interest in
receiving training was differentiated on the
basis of their needs. Some participants
preferred to receive training in reading related items, while others tended to prefer
training on items that focused on mobility
(see Figure 7). These preferences were related to the loss of central vision versus the
loss of peripheral vision, respectively. However, this is a point that is beyond the scope
of this article and needs to be explored in
greater depth in subsequent articles dealing
with this measure. Future rehabilitation
programs may want to consider including a
choice of either reading- or mobility focused tracks to make better use of the
participants' and specialists' time.
