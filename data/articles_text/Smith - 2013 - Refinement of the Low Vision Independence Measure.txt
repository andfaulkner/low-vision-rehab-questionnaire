Refinement of the Low Vision Independence
Measure: A Qualitative Study
Theresa Marie Smith, PhD, OTR/L, CLVT
Department of Occupational Therapy and Occupational Science, Towson University,
Towson, Maryland, USA

ABSTRACT. Purpose: The purpose of this qualitative study was to improve clinical
utility of the Low Vision Independence Measure (LVIM) and strengthen its content validity. Methods: A two-part qualitative emergent design was used. Low vision therapists’
opinions were collected first with a questionnaire and then with a focus group. Results:
Findings indicated that clinical utility of the LVIM could be improved by adding comment sections and moving some of its items or subsections. The content validity of the
LVIM could be strengthened through the additions of new items to some subsections,
new subsections, and item lists, as well as rewording of some items. Therapists acknowledged current LVIM strengths of client-centeredness, ability to identify problem areas
upon which to formulate a plan of care, versatility for use in different treatment settings, efficient administration, and cultural sensitivity. Conclusions: Further research is
needed on the refined LVIM to establish its psychometric properties.

Studies have shown that rehabilitation services improve functional outcomes

low vision rehabilitation constitutes best practice is not established, Medicare reimburses for therapy services for clients with low vision provided by occupational
therapists, physical therapists, and speech therapists (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2002). There are a number of reliable and valid assessments available
to measure low vision rehabilitation but the majority of these were developed by
researchers for research purposes. Assessments are needed that measure the outcomes of low vision rehabilitation provided; one of these is the Smith’s Low Vision
Independence Measure (LVIM).
Although test–retest reliability has been established for the LVIM (Smith, 2009),
clinical utility and content validity have not. The purpose of this qualitative study
was to improve clinical utility of the LVIM and strengthen its content validity from
expert opinion of experienced low vision therapists. At this end, two research questions were used: (a) what insight can experienced low vision therapists provide to
improve clinical utility of the LVIM? And (b) what modifications of LVIM items
do the same experienced low vision therapists suggest be made to strengthen the
content validity?
LITERATURE REVIEW
Clinical Utility
Clinical utility refers to an assessment’s usefulness in clinical practice (Smart, 2006).
According to Polgar, Reg, and Barlow (as cited in Smart, 2006), there are 6 key components of clinical utility: (a) ease of use, (b) time, (c) training and qualifications, (d)
format, (e) interpretation, and (f) meaning and relevance of information obtained.
Ease of use considers the availability and price of an assessment, the completeness
and clarity of instructions for use, specification or provision of materials and methods, and clarity of environmental requirements (Smart, 2006). Time refers to the
amount of time required for administration and the effect of timing on reliability
and validity. Training and qualifications include the requirements of professional
knowledge, ability, or training to administer; as well as, the availability of training
for administration. The format of an assessment is the ease of use for the client and
clinician. Interpretation considers ease in scoring and understanding the results of
an assessment as well as any training required for interpretation. Finally, the meaning and relevance of information obtained from an assessment refers to the applicability of the results in a clinic setting and any factors that could compromise the
use of the information.
Rudman and Hannah (1998) added that in order for an assessment to have
clinical utility it must be acceptable to clients. If it is not, clients’ willingness to
participate in the evaluation may be adversely affected. Factors that may influence
whether an assessment is acceptable by clients include if its purpose is understood,
age, language requirements, and cultural applicability (Rudman & Hannah, 1998,
p. 271).

Visual Function Assessments
Visual function assessments (VFAs) are used with clients with low vision to evaluate their functional ability to perform ADLs and IADLs (Binns et al., 2012). They
are designed to collect data through self-report, observation, or a combination of
the two. Assessments that use a self-report rating scale are beneficial to the clinician because they provide a more comprehensive assessment of which factor is
really affecting the client’s performance. However, self-report ratings are subjective because of the client’s bias and the possibility of over- or underrating himself
or herself. The limitation of client bias can alter the true results of the outcomes of
visual rehabilitation. The objective data derived from observation can be valuable
because these are obtained from an expert clinician who can adequately evaluate
the quality and independence of the client’s performance of a task. Just as self reports contain client bias, observations contain clinician bias as each clinician may
have his or her own perception of how to rate a specific item. In addition, these
assessments require more personnel time to administer.
Several VFAs were designed for use with a specific visual diagnosis thereby limiting their application with the general population. For example, the Manchester
Low Vision Questionnaire (MLVQ; Harper, Doorduyn, Reeves, & Slater, 1999)
was developed to evaluate clients with AMD. Other VFAs, such as The Visual Function Index (VF-14; Steinberg et al., 1994), Activities of Daily Vision Scale (ADVS;
Mangione et al., 1992), and Visual Disability Assessment (VDA; Pesudovs &
Coster, 1998) were designed specifically for patients with cataracts. Lastly, the Functional Independence Measure for Blind Adults (FIMBA) focuses on rehabilitation
outcome measures for blind clients (Long, Crews, & Mancil, 2000).
Technology is expanding at a rapid rate and more health practitioners are using
technology in the clinic. One example of an assessment using new technology is
the Activity Inventory (AI) that is a computer-based adaptive VFA (Massof et al.,
2007). Administration of the AI requires a computer and Internet service neither
of which may be available in all clinics and it may not be useful to therapists who
lack computer skills. In addition, due to its extensive list of 459 tasks and 50 goals,
the AI is time intensive to administer.

Visual Functional Assessments and Occupational Therapy
VFAs developed by occupational therapists include: the Self-Report Assessment of
Functional Visual Performance (SRAFVP; Gilbert & Baker, 2011), the Melbourne
low-vision ADL index (MLVAI; Haymes, Johnston, & Heyes, 2001), DahlinIvanoff, Sonn, and Svensson’s (2001) low vision assessment, and the LVIM (Smith,
2009). Both the SRAFVP (Gilbert & Baker, 2011) and the MLVAI (Haymes et al.,
2001) have observable tasks. The SRAFVP is two-part with the first part being
a self-report questionnaire and the second an optional observational assessment.
When the second part is used, 7 tasks can be observed to determine the client’s functional abilities. To date, no outcome studies are available on the SRAFVP. Haymes
et al. (2001) developed the MLVAI, a standardized assessment with 27 test items;
9 are self-report items including bathing, eating, food preparation, shopping, cleaning, and medication management. Sixteen of the test items are observable tasks

The LVIM (Smith, 2009) was designed to be administered to the general low
vision population. It has been refined with input from experienced low vision practitioners and clients with low vision (Smith, Glasson, Tioseco, & Welborn, 2009). It
uses the constructs and terminology outlined in the Occupational Therapy Practice
Framework (OTPF; American Occupational Therapy Association [AOTA], 2008)
and focuses on performance of ADLS and IADLS commonly affected by low vision. It provides therapists with a framework and common terminology to assess the
client’s occupational performance and measures the effectiveness of the treatment
commonly provided in low vision rehabilitation.
METHODS
Design and Rationale
The current research study used a qualitative design consisting of two parts: a questionnaire and a focus group, which both included open-ended questions that gave
participants the opportunity to elaborate on their responses and provided the researchers with rich description. Questionnaires have been used to elicit expert
opinions needed in the process of establishing content validity of an instrument
(Kimberlin & Winterstein, 2008). They are generally efficient and convenient for
participants to complete since they can finish them in their own environment. In this
study, participants provided their initial opinions of the LVIM with the questionnaire and their responses aided in the development of the focus group questions,
which allowed for an emergent design (Creswell, 2007).
Focus groups utilize informal discussion among individuals regarding a topic
of concentration in order to understand different perspectives on the topic (Stancanelli, 2010; Wilkinson, 2004). They allow participants to interact in a natural social environment, which elicits responses that may not have been captured in an
individual interview (Gaiser, 2008; Kroll, Barbour, & Harris, 2007). This is further facilitated because participants are able to bounce ideas off of each other,
which can lead to deep discussions about topics and opinions expressed that may
not have arisen in a typical one-to-one interview. Virtual focus groups were used
in the current study to facilitate a deep discussion on the LVIM among experienced low vision therapists from a wide geographic area. Further, focus groups have
been shown valuable in refining an instrument to improve content validity (DahlinIvanoff, 2002; Long et al., 2000).

Setting
The first stage of data collection occurred with participants in outpatient or home
settings (2 participants worked in the same outpatient setting) answering a questionnaire. The second stage took place in the virtual context of an online focus
group. During this stage, data were collected through Internet connections by researchers in a classroom located in their affiliated university and the participants
contributed from their individual computers in their respective locations.
Instruments
The LVIM was the instrument under review. It measures a client’s level of difficulty
in performing ADLS and IADLs (Smith, 2009). It is a self-report questionnaire
consisting of 55 items, divided into 8 subsections of “Self-care,” “Food Preparation,”
“Home Management,” “Shopping,” “Communication,” “Financial Management,”
“Leisure,” and “Mobility.” The level of difficulty for completion of each item is rated
using a four-point Likert scale where 1 = unable, 2 = very difficult, 3 = moderately
difficult, and 4 = not difficult. If an activity is not performed, the client can choose
n/a = not applicable. The assessment takes approximately 15 min to administer and
has good test–retest reliability of 0.822–0.967 across the 8 subsections (Smith et al.,
2009).
The LVIM Content Validity Questionnaire, developed by the researchers, was
used to explore the participants’ expert opinion of the LVIM and to develop the
focus group questions. The questionnaire included open-ended questions about recommended additions or modifications to the LVIM, clarity of the LVIM directions,
its format and organization, cultural relevancy, as well as participants’ demographic
information. Questions on participants’ demographic information were placed at
the end of the questionnaire to avoid participants questioning the relevance of the
demographic questions to the study (Forsyth & Kviz, 2006).
Focus groups were held online using WebEx technology. Using an emergent design, the focus group questions were generated from the results of the LVIM Content Validity Questionnaire and were designed to further explore and reaffirm the
participants’ opinions elicited with the questionnaire.
Trustworthiness
Trustworthiness of this study occurred through credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability (Gay, Mills, & Airasian, 2012). Credibility was established by using a two-part study. Participants’ opinions were first elicited with
an open-ended questionnaire and then reaffirmed during a focus group. Also,
study findings were replete with participants’ direct quotes. Transferability was
increased by describing participants’ characteristics. Dependability was obtained
by using field notes from several researchers and the faculty advisor (author),

Data Collection Procedures
Twelve potential participants were contacted to explain the study and determine
their interest in being in the study. After 10 of them agreed to participate and sent
a letter of support to the author, recruitment of participants was concluded. Next,
participants signed and returned informed consent letters approved through the author’s affiliated Institutional Review Board. Once all informed consent forms were
returned, participants were sent a copy of the LVIM as well as the LVIM Content
Validity Questionnaire. Participants completed the content questionnaire and returned it to the author. Over the following month, each participant administered
the LVIM to 6 clients in order to judge its utility and to develop his or her opinion
on how to further refine the tool.
The second part of the study utilized online focus groups to collect data. The
researchers conducted two back-to-back focus groups that lasted over 75 min
in length using WebEx technology. This technology allowed participants and researchers to interact in real time and record audio and video of the sessions. Although an equal number of participants were planned for each WebEx session, interface difficulties prevented 1 participant from entering the first focus group. As a
result, 4 participants interacted in the first focus group and 6 in the second.
Data Analysis
Data analysis began with participants’ responses to the LVIM Content Validity
Questionnaires. Initial coding took place independently, with student researchers
reviewing questionnaire responses to determine categories or topic areas (Creswell,
2013). When all questionnaires were coded, the student researchers met with the
faculty advisor to organize categories based on the replies to each question on the
LVIM Content Validity Questionnaire. These categories were collapsed into 3 categories with related subcategories and were used to formulate the focus group questions and probes.
Directly after each focus group was conducted, the audio and video recordings were obtained. Several of the student researchers transcribed the focus group
recordings verbatim and other student researchers reviewed the transcriptions for
accuracy. Three student researchers independently read and coded the first focus
group transcript, while 2 others read and coded the second. After the initial coding
was completed, the researchers met with the faculty advisor to discuss coding discrepancies and to develop a code book (Saldaña, 2009). Similar codes were grouped
together to form focus group themes and subthemes (Creswell, 2013). Thematic development provided an understanding of participants’ opinions on how to refine the
LVIM.

RESULTS
The 10 participants in the study included 7 females (70%) and 3 males (30%). Education level of participants included bachelor and master degrees and 3 different types of professional certifications in low vision rehabilitation including: Certified Low Vision Therapist (CLVT), Certified Orientation and Mobility Specialist
(COMS), and Specialty Certificate in Low Vision (SCLV) with some participants
having dual professional certificates (see Table 1). All but one of the participants
was a certified occupational therapist. Participants practiced in 8 different states
including: Maryland, Louisiana, Illinois, Virginia, Maine, Texas, Pennsylvania, and
New Jersey. The range of total years of practice for participants was 23 with a mean
of 12.95 years and the range of years of practice in low vision was 20 with a mean
of 6.45.
Data analysis of the LVIM Content Validity Questionnaire yielded three topic
areas to explore in the focus groups. These included: (a) rewording of items and
changes to the sequence of subcategories to increase clinical utility, (b) items to add
or omit from the LVIM to improve content validity, and (c) existing client-centered
aspects of the LVIM.
Analysis of the focus group transcripts yielded 18 codes that were combined and
collapsed to form 3 themes and related subthemes. Theme 1: Recommended refinements to the LVIM to improve clinical utility consisted of the 2 subthemes formatting
and rating clarification. The subtheme of formatting was developed to address participant recommendations related to the inclusion of areas where comments could
be documented, the organization of some of the items, and the provision of lists of
items to replace some single items.
In reference to organization of items, Participant 9 explained that sometimes
the item order impeded the clients’ ability to understand the context of the item.
For example, because the item “cutting food” came before “identifying foods when
eating,” she had to explain this item referred to cutting food on their plate to eat,
not for preparing food in the kitchen.
Development of the subtheme of rating clarification was garnered from two participants’ comments. Participant 9 suggested that administration of the LVIM utilize rating cards similar to the cards used in the COPM (Canadian Occupational
Performance Measure) because her clients kept forgetting what their choices were.

Other suggestions by participants included adding new subsections of “Medication Management,” “Safety/Mobility,” and “Reading.” Participant 5, remarked,
“Right now we use FIM [Functional Independence Measure] scores, and I follow
ADLS, IADLS, near reading, intermediate, and distance viewing. So for me personally I would like a subsection [for reading].”
The second subtheme, rewording of items, emerged through suggestions from
participants to help clarify items such as “choosing clothing,” “telling apart toiletries,” “volunteering in the community,” “making appointments,” “cutting food,”
and “reading medication directions.” The majority of participants agreed with
the recommendations to change “choosing clothing” to “identifying clothing” and
“telling apart toiletries” to “identifying toiletries.” Furthermore, the majority of
participants agreed on changing the item, “volunteering in community” to “community involvement.” Participant 2 rationalized the suggested change:
Being involved in the community is a lot more broad. . . . I think the impression
a few of my people got when I asked if they were volunteering in the community
was almost, they pulled back from it. . . I don’t want to say it made them feel
guilty, but almost something along that line.
Some participants felt the item “making appointments” was not difficult but
recording or documenting appointments was, and that this item should be reworded
accordingly. In addition, one participant suggested item, “cutting food” was confusing to clients and required further explanation to clarify that this item referred to
“. . . cutting food on their plate.” Lastly, some participants felt that the wording of
item “reading medication directions” was too restrictive. Instead of the word “reading,” Participant 7 suggested that “managing” was a more accurate way of capturing
all the tasks involved in taking medications.

DISCUSSION
Results of this study yielded modifications to the LVIM to improve its clinical utility and strengthen its content validity. To improve clinical utility, sections could be
added where therapists could document comments and the sequence of items and
subsections could be better organized to provide a more logical progression during administration of the assessment. LVIM content validity could be strengthened
by adding new items and/or subsections, item lists, or by rewording items. Positive
attributes of the LVIM make it useful in the provision of low vision rehabilitation
by experienced low vision therapists and acceptable by the clients. No cultural bias
was detected and for assessments items, which may be affected by age, the client
can choose the items are not applicable.
In contrast to other studies, participants in this study did not recommend any
of the 55 items of the LVIM be discarded indicating that items on the LVIM represented typical activities addressed in low vision rehabilitation. In a study by Becker,
Lambert, Schulz, Wright, and Burnet (1985) on the refinement of an instrument to
measure the activity level of the blind, participants recommended 23 of the instrument’s 99 items be culled. In their development of an ADL assessment, DahlinIvanoff et al.’s (2001) assessment initially consisted of 65 items. This number was reduced to 53 from discussions in 2 focus groups with elderly participants with AMD.
Then, 3 occupational therapists in their study identified 24 of the remaining items
as not useful in the clinic setting and they were removed.
Combining and rewording of items suggested in this study have been used in the
refinement of other low vision assessments. Head, Babcock, Goodrich, and Boyless
(2000) dropped some of their initial items but also reduced the number of items
through combining and rewording of others. The number of items in the revised
LVIM did not decrease by the number of items combined (5) because 4 items were
added.

Implications for Practice and Future Research
The LVIM has been shown to have good clinical utility and can be used in evaluating a client with low vision and in determining rehabilitation outcomes. It is easy to
use, quick to administer, and no additional training is needed to administer it as the
directions are self-explanatory. The information solicited with the LVIM is meaningful and relevant to the rehabilitation process for clients with low vision. Lastly, it
has been reformatted to reflect the recommendations of practicing low vision therapists (see Appendix). The content validity of the LVIM has been strengthened by
including and confirming that its items are valid in the rehabilitation of clients with
low vision.
It is critical at this stage of development and refinement of the LVIM that additional research be done to document its reliability and validity. A study is underway to establish the measurement validity of the LVIM through a Rasch analysis.
A Rasch analysis of the LVIM will lead to a more efficient VFA and one which will
reduce client burden by assessing the clients on only the most relevant assessment
items (Classen & Velozo, 2014). Once the LVIM’s psychometric properties are fully
established, it will be a valuable low vision assessment that contributes to low vision
therapists’ client-centered practice.
CONCLUSION
In this qualitative study, 10 experienced low vision therapists provided insight on
suggested improvements to the LVIM. Refinements made to the LVIM have improved its clinical utility and content validity. It is a client-centered tool that aids
in formulating a treatment plan, is easy to administer, and can be used in different contexts. In addition, it measures outcomes of the treatment provided. Further
research is needed on the refined LVIM to establish its psychometric properties.
