SIGNIFICANCE: This research is significant because, although vision-related quality of life (VRQoL) is improved
after vision rehabilitation (VR), patients with certain characteristics respond less positively on VRQoL measures,
and this should inform future care.
PURPOSE: The purposes of this study were to evaluate how two VRQoL questionnaires compare in measuring
change in patient-reported outcomes after VR and to determine if patient characteristics or occupational therapy
(OT) predict higher scores after rehabilitation.
METHODS: In a prospective clinical cohort study, 109 patients with low vision completed the Impact of Vision Impairment (IVI) and the National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire (NEI VFQ-25) before and after VR.
Comprehensive VR included consultation with an ophthalmologist and OT if required. The relationships of six baseline characteristics (age, sex, visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, field loss, diagnosis) and OT were assessed with
VRQoL scores using multivariable logistic regression.
RESULTS: The mean (SD) age was 68.5 (19.2) years, and 61 (56%) were female. After rehabilitation, increases in
scores were observed in all IVI subscales (reading [P < .001], mobility [P = .002], well-being [P = .0003]) and all
NEI VFQ-25 subscales (functional [P = .01], socioemotional [P = .003]). Those who were referred to OT but did not
attend and those who had hemianopia/field loss were less likely to have higher VRQoL in IVI mobility and well being. Those attending OT for more than 3 hours were less likely to have better scores in emotional NEI VFQ.
Men were less likely to have increased scores in functional and emotional NEI VFQ, whereas those with diagnoses
of nonmacular diseases had higher odds of having increased scores on the emotional NEI VFQ (all, P < .05).
CONCLUSION: Both the IVI and the NEI VFQ-25 detected change in patients' VRQoL after rehabilitation. Most of
the patient characteristics we considered predicted a lower likelihood of increased scores in VRQoL.

Vision loss is a global health problem affecting 36 million who
are blind and 217 million who are visually impaired.1 Vision rehabilitation is an option for those whose vision cannot be improved
with treatment, surgery, or refractive correction, also referred to
as low vision. Close to 4 million Americans have low vision or blindness.2 Vision rehabilitation aims to optimize patients' functional
ability and quality of life with assistive devices, training, adaptive
strategies, and psychosocial supports. Interventions vary depending on degree and pattern of vision loss.3

As part of usual care, the Vision Rehabilitation Centre at
Massachusetts Eye and Ear collects many patient variables: demographics, visual function, diagnosis, field loss testing using a macular perimeter, and occupation therapy progress notes. Based on
our clinical experience, we hypothesize that older patients with
worse fields, worse visual function, and less occupational therapy
would demonstrate less improvement in vision-related quality of
life compared with their counterparts. Understanding which
patient characteristics are associated with improved patient reported outcomes can inform effective models of care.

METHODS
Recruitment
This prospective clinical cohort study was conducted at the Vision Rehabilitation Clinic of Massachusetts Eye and Ear Infirmary
from December 2012 to December 2014. Patients attending the
clinic for the first time were invited to participate. Participants were
older than 18 years, English speaking, and without severe hearing
or cognitive impairment as assessed by a clinician. Written consent
was obtained from all participants. The research study was approved by the institutional review board at the Massachusetts Eye
and Ear Infirmary and complied fully with the tenets of Declaration
of Helsinki.

Comprehensive Vision Rehabilitation
The Vision Rehabilitation Clinic at the Massachusetts Eye and
Ear Infirmary provides a multidisciplinary model of comprehensive
vision rehabilitation that addresses five areas of patient function:
reading, activities of daily living, safety, continuing to participate
in activities despite vision loss, and psychosocial well-being
(American Academy of Ophthalmology, 2013).3 All subjects had
an assessment by an ophthalmologist (MLJ) who is a specialist in
vision rehabilitation and were referred to occupational therapy if
goals identified on the initial consultation could be addressed with
training and/or training with devices. Occupational therapy included up to 11 hours of therapy at home or in the clinic and focused on addressing the patients' individual goals with appropriate
training and devices, such as training patients to navigate pages of
text with a magnifying device, using alternate strategies such as
operating an audio book player, or using a computer with accessibility options. Occupational therapists also provided referral to
other multidisciplinary services such as orientation and mobility
training to use a long white cane. As appropriate, patients were
offered resources and referral to support groups, counselors,
and social work services.

Baseline Characteristics
Six baseline sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were
extracted from patients' medical records: (1) age, (2) sex, (3) visual
acuity, (4) contrast sensitivity, (5) visual field loss, and (6) diagnosis
of eye condition. An additional variable, whether the subject completed occupational therapy, was also included. Best-corrected visual
acuity in the better eye was measured using the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study chart at 1 m or a projected chart (in logMAR).
Binocular contrast sensitivity was measured using the Pelli-Robson
chart at 1 m. Extent of central field loss was measured monocularly
using the OPTOS scanning laser ophthalmoscope macular perimeter
OPKO/OTI (OPKO Instrumentation, Miami, FL). The extent of the
scotoma was estimated using monocular perimetry tests and by determining the scotoma that would overlap, viewing binocularly.9
Peripheral field loss was obtained from patient notes.

Administration of Questionnaires
All patients were mailed the Impact of Vision Impairment questionnaire and National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire 2 weeks before their baseline appointment at the clinic and
asked to complete the questionnaires themselves or with the assistance of others (family, friends, or clinic staff ). Post-rehabilitation
questionnaires were sent to subjects 1 month after discharge or
within 3 months after last occupational therapy appointment for
those who did not return for further occupational therapy treatment. Subjects were followed up by telephone if questionnaires
were not returned, and questionnaires were administered by telephone if subjects wished it so. Administration of questionnaires
was performed by the clinic research coordinator.

National Eye Institute Visual
Functioning Questionnaire
The National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire is a
vision-related quality-of-life questionnaire that has been previously
validated in patients with eye diseases (including age-related macular degeneration and low vision).7,8,10 The original questionnaire
scoring has 12 vision-specific subscales.7–9 The driving subscale
was not used in the current study, and three additional questions
about near vision (A3, A4, and A5) were included in the hope that
additional questions will help measure improvement in patients
with central vision loss after rehabilitation.

Impact of Vision Impairment Questionnaire
The Impact of Vision Impairment is a vision-related quality of-life questionnaire that has been previously validated in a
low-vision population.11 The questionnaire has a total scale12,13 and
three subscales: reading and accessing information (reading), mobility
and independence (mobility), and emotional well-being (well-being).

Psychometric Assessment of the Outcome Measures
Rasch analysis was used to assess the psychometric properties
of the Impact of Vision Impairment and the National Eye Institute
Visual Functioning Questionnaire using the Andrich rating scale
model14 with Winsteps software (version 3.81; Chicago, IL).15
Rasch analysis is a form of item response theory and enables ordinal raw questionnaire scores to be transformed to estimates of interval measures (expressed in log of the odds units, or logits),
thus enabling subsequent parametric analyses to be confidently
performed.16 In the case of the Impact of Vision Impairment and
the National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire, a high

Rasch analysis also provides significant insight into the psychometric properties of the scale, including (a) appropriate
use of response categories, (b) measurement precision, (c) how
well items “fit” the underlying trait, (d) unidimensionality, (e)
targeting of item difficulty to patients' ability, and (f) differential
item functioning.17
To generate valid pre-rehabilitation and post-rehabilitation person measures, the data were anchored to item measures and structure calibrations at baseline for the subsequent analyses, which is
standard in pre/post–Rasch analyses.18,19 This approach reduces
distortion of the estimated person measures by anchoring the Impact of Vision Impairment questionnaire and National Eye Institute
Visual Functioning Questionnaire to baseline, thus ascertaining
that the same instrument with identical measurement properties
is used to repeatedly test participants over time. Baseline and
follow-up data were also stacked to establish that differences between the scores for all outcome measures at baseline and follow up were valid indicators of changes over time.20
Overall, the composite Impact of Vision Impairment scale
displayed suboptimal fit to Rasch model parameters. Although
scale precision and targeting were excellent, there was evidence
of multidimensionality, which could be resolved only by splitting
the Impact of Vision Impairment into three separate scales: reading
(items 1, 3, 5 to 9, 14, and 15), mobility (items 2, 4, 10 to 13, and
16 to 20), and well-being (items 21 to 28). These three scales—
which were proposed in the original validation studies of the Impact of Vision Impairment21,22—were unidimensional, displayed
excellent precision and targeting, were free from differential item
functioning, and were used for our subsequent analysis.
Previous Rasch analysis of the National Eye Institute Visual
Functioning Questionnaire has demonstrated that the composite
scale and 6 of the 12 proposed subscales are dysfunctional, and
a two-factor structure—visual functioning and socioemotional—
has been proposed.7,8 Guided both by this prior analysis and by
the standardized residual loadings in principal components analysis from our own data, we used a similar two-factor structure. The
National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire has seven
different rating scales for patients to record their responses. Our
analysis found that rating scale 6 (items 17 to 19, all of the time
to none of the time) and rating scale 7 (items 20 to 25, definitely
true to definitely false) were disordered. To resolve disordered
thresholds, categories 4 and 5 were collapsed for rating scale 6,
and category 3 (“not sure”) was coded as missing data in rating
scale 7, based on empirical evidence from a similar validation
study of the National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire using Rasch analysis.7,8 Rasch analysis of the National Eye
Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire also revealed that the
scale was multidimensional, with a high eigenvalue (3.1) and
seven misfitting items. The visual functioning scale (items 2, 4, 5
to 14, 19, and A3 to A5) displayed good precision and targeting;
however, multidimensionality was evident, with a high eigenvalue
(2.7) and two misfitting items. There was also evidence of differential item functioning for sex for item A5 (“how much difficulty do
you have doing things like shaving, styling your hair, or putting
on makeup”). This item was more difficult for men than for
women, despite the two groups having a similar underlying level
of visual functioning. Therefore, we iteratively deleted items A3 to
A5 to resolve multidimensionality and differential item functioning, and we deleted item 4 because of misfit. The remaining

12 items displayed excellent fit to Rasch model parameters. The
socioemotional scale (items 3, 17, 18, and 20 to 25, which relate
to the original dependency, mental health, and role limitations
subscales) displayed excellent psychometric properties and did
not require any amendments to its structure. Thus, the two subscales of visual functioning and socioemotional were used for
subsequent analysis.

Statistical Analyses
All statistical analyses were performed using Stata 14.2 for
Windows (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX). Patients' baseline
characteristics were reported as mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables and as frequency count (percent) for categorical
variables. Independent-sample t test and χ2 test for continuous
and categorical variables, respectively, were used to compare baseline characteristics between those who enrolled in the research
study and those who declined and between patients who completed both questionnaires and those who did not complete the
post-rehabilitation questionnaires. Paired t tests were then performed to determine if change in questionnaire scores was significantly different from pre-rehabilitation to post-rehabilitation, with
Bonferroni adjustments used for multiple comparisons. A multivariable binary logistic regression model was then used to determine
the impact of participants' basic demographic and clinical characteristics, together with occupational therapy attendance and duration, on post-rehabilitation vision-related quality-of-life scores in
the subscales of the Impact of Vision Impairment and National
Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire. A two-sided P value
of less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS
Participants
A total of 189 patients agreed to participate in this study and
completed pre-rehabilitation questionnaires. Patients (n = 219)
who did not agree to participate in this study during the same period did not differ in age, sex, visual acuity, or contrast sensitivity
(P > .05, data not shown). Only 109 (58%) of the 189 patients
completed both pre-questionnaires and post-questionnaires, and
the overall mean age (standard deviation) of these participants
was 68.5 (19.2) years, with 61 (56%) being female. The reasons
for failure to obtain post-rehabilitation questionnaires included
(1) subjects not mailing questionnaires after reminders, (2) subjects lost to contact, (3) refusal, and (4) subjects reporting poor
health that made it too difficult to complete questionnaires. When
baseline characteristics of those who completed both questionnaires and those who did not complete post-rehabilitation questionnaires were compared, the groups did not differ significantly
in sex (P = .42, χ2 test), age (P = .06), or visual acuity (P = .08).
However, the group who did not complete post-rehabilitation questionnaires had significantly (P = .02) better average contrast sensitivity (mean [standard deviation], 1.30 [0.46]) than did the group
who completed both pre-questionnaires and post-questionnaires
(mean [standard deviation], 1.14 [0.43]). Table 1 shows the characteristics of patients who completed both pre-rehabilitation questionnaires and post-rehabilitation questionnaires and participated
in the study. Patients who were not referred to occupational therapy
included those who in the clinician's opinion did not require further
training after the initial consultation.

Change in Vision-related Quality-of-life Scores
After Rehabilitation
There was a significant increase in vision-related quality-of-life
scores on all three Impact of Vision Impairment subscales after rehabilitation: reading (mean change, 0.59 logits; P < .001, paired t test),
mobility (mean change, 0.38 logits; P = .002), and well-being (mean
change, 0.68 logits; P = .0003; Table 2). Both National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire visual functioning and
socioemotional subscale scores also increased significantly after
rehabilitation (P = .01 and P = .003, respectively). The effect
sizes were moderate for Impact of Vision Impairment reading (Cohen
d = 0.50) and small for Impact of Vision Impairment well-being
(0.37), Impact of Vision Impairment mobility (0.31), and National
Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire visual functioning
(0.22) and socioemotional subscales (0.34).23

Scores from Patient Characteristics and
Occupational Therapy

DISCUSSION
Millions of people in the United States and worldwide have irreversible vision loss, and vision rehabilitation remains the only treatment option for these people. Unlike in most ophthalmology
studies, measurement of visual function is not an outcome that reflects the contributions of vision rehabilitation. As a result, patient reported outcomes in the form of vision-related quality-of-life
questionnaires have become the most commonly used outcome
measure of rehabilitation.5 In our study, we measured perceived
efficacy of our usual care vision rehabilitation at Massachusetts
Eye and Ear Infirmary using two commonly used patient reported outcomes: Impact of Vision Impairment and National
Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire. We found that indeed
our usual care vision rehabilitation led to increased vision-related
www.optvissci.com

quality-of-life scores in our patient cohort. Because individual patients
respond to vision rehabilitation differently, we examined the data
to elucidate whether patient characteristics and participation in
occupational therapy predicted a positive change in scores on
vision-related quality of life. Surprisingly, sex, field loss, and occupational therapy attendance/duration predicted a less likelihood of
higher scores, and only diagnosis other than macular disease was
predictive of increases in vision-related quality-of-life scores.

Change in Vision-related Quality-of-life Scores
After Rehabilitation
Our main result from the study was that perceived vision-related
quality of life increased in all three Impact of Vision Impairment
subscales and in the two National Eye Institute Visual Functioning
Questionnaire subscales after comprehensive vision rehabilitation.
The reading subscale of the Impact of Vision Impairment had the
largest effect size (Cohen d = 0.50), and the National Eye Institute
Visual Functioning Questionnaire socioemotional subscale had a
smaller effect size (0.29) than did the similar Impact of Vision Impairment well-being subscale (0.37). Our effect sizes are similar to
or a little larger than Impact of Vision Impairment effect sizes
reported in published studies, for example, Lamoureux et al.11
(mobility, 0.17; reading, 0.20; and well-being, 0.30) and

For the National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire,

Although our effect sizes (between 0.2 and 0.5) are on par with
most other low-vision rehabilitation outcome studies,26 several
studies have reported larger effect sizes after vision rehabilitation.27,28 The LOVITT 1 trial by Stelmack et al.27 had effect sizes
between 1.14 and 2.51 using the Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual
Functioning Questionnaire patient-reported outcomes. The study
offered intensive multidisciplinary rehabilitation and provided low vision devices at no cost to a sample of largely white, male veterans.
Because it is unlikely to encounter such a homogeneous sample and
uniform treatment paradigm in typical clinical practice, it is reasonable to assume that effect sizes for general vision rehabilitation are
much smaller, and in fact, this has been the case in most studies.
The effect sizes in the study by Goldstein et al.28 range between
0.38 and 0.82 using Activity Inventory patient-reported outcomes. Our effect size range of 0.25 to 0.50 has a good concordance with that of Goldstein et al.28 as well as similar
follow-up rates, which may contribute to selection bias. It is
possible that their slightly larger effect sizes were a result of
the instrument used (Activity Inventory) or study design, or a
combination of both. Discrepancies in effect sizes, of course,
can also be indicative of noise in questionnaires: high effect
sizes may mean that the questionnaire is exaggerating the effect after treatment, whereas low effect sizes may mean that
the questionnaire is understating the real treatment effect.
Such discrepancies make extrapolating increased scores in
vision-related quality of life to patient's actual improvement
difficult. Ideally, both the Impact of Vision Impairment and
the National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire
need to be psychometrically evaluated for reproducibility and
reliability in our clinic population. However, such analysis was
beyond the scope of the study.

Predicting Change in Vision-related Quality-of-life
Scores from Patient Characteristics and
Occupational Therapy
Results from our multivariable logistic regression show that patient characteristics such as being male, visual field loss, and treatment options such as referral to occupational therapy and length of
occupational therapy predict less increase in vision-related quality of-life scores, whereas having a diagnosis other than macular disease
predicts an increase in vision-related quality-of-life scores. Vision rehabilitation studies indicate that age rather than sex is a predictor of
vision-related quality of life.27,28 Most associations depend on sample
size and distribution, and it is possible that sex was not a predictor in
our study because of small sample size and the uneven distribution of
our age group (our participants' median age was 75 years). Because
sex is not an established predictor in vision rehabilitation, perhaps
men's lower likelihood of increased scores on vision-related quality
of life reflects the idiosyncrasy of our study population. Field loss, especially hemianopia and peripheral field loss, predicted a lower likelihood of positive change in scores on vision-related quality of life. Field
loss is known to negatively affect vision-related quality of life in glaucoma and hemianopia patients.29,30 In our study, higher scores on
www.optvissci.com

the Impact of Vision Impairment well-being subscale but not on the
mobility subscale were reduced by visual field loss. However, this result should be interpreted with caution because there were only eight
participants in the hemianopia and peripheral field loss group. Because the Impact of Vision Impairment was designed for patients with
poor central vision, it is possible that it may not be sensitive enough to
detect mobility-related vision-related quality-of-life change in patients
with bad peripheral vision but good central vision.31 Our findings
about occupational therapy completion and duration are unique because, to our knowledge, no one has looked at occupational therapy
as a predictor of vision-related quality of life. It certainly seems intuitive that those who are referred to occupational therapy but do not attend do worse on the Impact of Vision Impairment mobility subscale,
as our occupational therapists do training about mobility issues such
as fall prevention strategies. It is also reasonable that those who attend
more than 3 hours of occupational therapy do not do well on the
socioemotional National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire subscale. Longer occupational therapy hours often indicate patients with greater need for rehabilitation or more rehabilitation
goals. It is worth noting that the “not referred” occupational therapy
group, which was taken as a reference group in the analyses, had
only nine participants in it, and thus, the results should be
interpreted with caution. The small sample size in the group likely
led to large confidence intervals for the occupational therapy completion variable in Table 3. In our study, diagnoses other than macular disease predicted greater scores on the National Eye Institute
Visual Functioning Questionnaire socioemotional subscale. Because
it is likely that nonmacular diseases often encompass diseases of peripheral vision, it is counterintuitive that those with visual field loss
have decreased scores on Impact of Vision Impairment well-being,
an opposite result with the National Eye Institute Visual Functioning
Questionnaire. This discrepancy is an example of a discordance between the Impact of Vision Impairment questionnaire and the National Eye Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire.

Limitations
There are several limitations to our study results. First, our results may not be generalizable to other populations or other models
of vision rehabilitation. There was variability in how soon patients
completed questionnaires after rehabilitation, which may have
confounded results; however, such inconsistency is inevitable in
usual care, and no significant relationship between follow-up time
and effect size was reported in a systematic review of outcomes of
vision rehabilitation.6 Second, those with better vision (i.e., better
contrast sensitivity) were more likely to not respond to postrehabilitation questionnaires. This may have created a sample bias
in our study population. Those who consented for the study and
those who did not consent, however, did not differ significantly,
thus showing that our overall sample was representative of all patients attending the clinic. Third, an adaptive response questionnaire, one that estimates a patient's goals and his/her difficulty
based on their relevance and importance to the patient, was not
used in this study. The use of one such questionnaire, Activity Inventory, has been advocated in vision rehabilitation research.25,27
These questionnaires are generally lengthy to administer and do
not include a well-being subscale, which we find important as an
outcome of vision rehabilitation; however, comparing the performance of a quality-of-life questionnaire and an Activity Inventory–
type questionnaire may be of interest in future studies. Lastly, as
mentioned previously, the questionnaires needed to be analyzed
more thoroughly because discrepancies in effect sizes, as well as
