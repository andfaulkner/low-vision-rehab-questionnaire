Abstract
Purpose: The aim of the study was to develop evidence-based recommendations for the diagnosis, functional rehabilitation,
and follow-up of people with low vision between 7 and 18 years of age and > 18 years of age. Methods: A development
group was formed with the participation of patients. A systematic search was performed, and the quality of the studies and
the body of evidence were evaluated. Recommendations were made using the Grade Evidence to Decision Framework, and
then validated by national experts. Results: In the diagnostic section, the quality of evidence regarding visual acuity and
visual field was low; in these tests, a weak recommendation was made favorable to the use of the Early Treatment Diabetic
Retinopathy Study and Microperimetry primer, respectively. Regarding contrast sensitivity, the quality of evidence was very
low, and a weak recommendation was given favorable to the use of the Pelli-Robson test. Regarding treatment, the quality
of evidence on electronic optical aids was moderate, and a weak recommendation was given favorable to their use. Regarding manual and electronic magnifiers, telescopes, and tablets, the quality of evidence was very low and a weak recommendation favorable to their use was given. In the rehabilitation section, the quality of evidence regarding this aspect for people
> 16 years of age was moderate, and there is a strong recommendation favorable to multidisciplinary and multicomponent
rehabilitation. On the other hand, the quality of evidence regarding rehabilitation for people between 7 and 16 years of age
was low, and there is a strong recommendation favorable to its use. The quality of evidence for occupational therapy interventions was low and a weak recommendation was given favorable of its use. Regarding psychology, the quality of evidence on interventions like this was low and a strong recommendation was given favorable to their use. Finally, the quality of
evidence for the use of follow-up scales was high and a strong recommendation was given favorable to the use of the National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire-25. Conclusions: The recommendations issued facilitate establishing general practice guidelines for the comprehensive management of low vision to reduce unwanted variability regarding diagnosis, functional rehabilitation, and follow-up.
Keywords: Low vision. Clinical practice guideline. Rehabilitation. Assistive technologies. Vision test. Quality of life.

Resumen
Objetivo: Desarrollar recomendaciones basadas en la evidencia para el diagnóstico, la rehabilitación funcional y el seguimiento de personas con baja visión entre 7 y 18 años y mayores de 18 años. Método: Se conformó el grupo desarrollador
con la participación de pacientes. Se hizo una búsqueda sistemática yse evaluaron la calidad de los estudios y el cuerpo
de la evidencia. Las recomendaciones se hicieron utilizando Grade Evidence to Decision Framework, las cuales fueron validadas con expertos nacionales. Resultados: En el apartado de diagnóstico, la calidad de la evidencia respecto a agudeza
visual y campo visual fue baja; en estas pruebas se generó recomendación débil a favor del uso de la cartilla Early Treatment
Diabetic Retinopathy Study y de microperimetría, respectivamente. En cuanto a sensibilidad al contraste, la calidad de la
evidencia fue muy baja y se da recomendación débil a favor del uso de la prueba de Pelli-Robson. En cuanto al tratamiento, la calidad de la evidencia respecto a ayudas ópticas electrónicas fue moderada y se da recomendación débil a favor
para su uso. En cuanto a las lupas manuales, electrónicas, telescopios y tabletas, la calidad de la evidencia fue muy baja y
se da recomendación débil a favor para su uso. En el apartado de rehabilitación, la calidad de la evidencia para personas
mayores de 16 años fue moderada y se da recomendación fuerte a favor de la rehabilitación multidisciplinaria y multicomponente, mientras que la calidad de la evidencia respecto a rehabilitación para personas entre 7 y 16 años fue baja y se
da recomendación fuerte a favor de su uso. La calidad de la evidencia para intervenciones de terapia ocupacional fue baja
y se da recomendación débil a favor de su uso. Con relación a psicología, la calidad de la evidencia para las intervenciones
de este tipo fue baja y se da recomendación fuerte a favor de su uso. Finalmente, la calidad de la evidencia para el uso de
escalas de seguimiento fue alta y se da recomendación fuerte a favor del uso del National Eye Institute Visual Function
Questionnaire-25. Conclusiones: Las recomendaciones emitidas permiten dar un lineamiento general para el manejo integral
de la baja visión, reduciendo la variabilidad indeseada en el diagnóstico, la rehabilitación y el seguimiento.
Palabras clave: Baja visión. Guía de práctica clínica. Rehabilitación. Ayudas ópticas. Pruebas de visión. Calidad de vida
relacionada con la salud.

Introduction
Low vision is part of the functional spectrum of people with visual disability including moderate and severe
visual deficiencies according to the best corrected visual acuity (VA) and visual field. It was reported that
back in 2020 nearly 295 million people developed this
condition, which amounts to 3.74% of the entire world
population, with 37.4 cases reported for every 1000
people1. Within this vision loss category, 260 million
people had a moderate visual disability (VA between
20/60 and 20/200) and 34.8 million had severe visual
disability (VA between 20/200 and 20/400)2. That same
year, in the Central American region, where Colombia
is located, 34 million people were reported to have vision loss of whom 1.3 million were blind3. By 2050,
taking into account population aging, the growth, and
progressive urbanization of countries, among other determinant health factors, it has been anticipated that, at
least, 474 million people will be living with this type of
visual disability3. The main causes reported in 2021
were undercorrected refractive errors (86.1 million cases) and unoperated cataracts (78.8 million cases)4.
Back in Colombia, preliminary reports of the 2018 population census report a national prevalence of this disability
around 7.1% (3,065,361 Colombians have some disability
of which 1,784,372 correspond to severe or total disability)
190

and difficulties like not being able to see around them, near
or far amount to 18.7%. Based on these numbers, visual
disability could rank second in terms of frequency within
the population with disabilities in the country5.
Low vision can impact quality of life negatively and
equitable access to education and employment, among
others. This has considerable financial implications for
people, families, and communities1. Visual disability
usually compromises independent mobility and walking,
decreases mental welfare, increases the probability of
falls and car crashes, increases the need for social care
and, ultimately, and leads to higher mortality rates6.
In Colombia, there are not practical guidelines for the
management of visual disability with recommendations
regarding the diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation, and
follow-up of people with low vision. Therefore, a team
was set up to develop one. This article describes evidence-based recommendations for the diagnosis of
visual deficiencies in the low vision range, treatment
with optical aids, functional vision rehabilitation, and
follow-up of people with low vision between 7 and
18 years and in patients > 18 years, which provides
support for the health-care personnel, people with low
vision, their families, and the Colombian health-care
system for the comprehensive management of people
with this condition to improve the health-care process,
its functionality, and participation.

Methods

Population

These clinical practice guidelines were designed
based on the Colombian Methodological Guidelines by
the Ministry of Health7.
A group was set up to develop the guidelines with an
optometrist leader with a master’s degree in public
health and in policies and practices regarding development. Furthermore, a physiatrist methodological leader
and a clinical epidemiologist experienced in evidence-based clinical practice guidelines. The remaining members of the clinical group were an optometrist
with a master’ degree in public health, an optometrist
specialized in low vision, a health-care administrator
with a master’s degree in epidemiology, and a physician specialized in neuropsychological rehabilitation.
The methodological group including a physician with
a PhD in health-care policies and a physiatrist with a
master’s degree in clinical epidemiology, both expert in
systematic reviews, meta-analyses, evidence-based
clinical practical guidelines drafting, and in the Grading
of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and
Evaluations (GRADE methodology).
The group that developed the guidelines had permanent support from a specialist in archives and information sciences trained at the Iberoamerican Cochrane
Center. External clinical experts from different colleges,
hospitals, and organizations across the country were
summoned. A group of 22 people with low vision and
support from their relatives was built. Each of the participants signed a conflicts of interest statement form
and they were approached according to the methodological manual.
This project had institutional backing from Research
and Innovation Management from the Universidad Santo Tomás de Bucaramanga in full compliance with the
1993 Resolution 008430 enacted by the Colombian
Ministry of Health on health-care research standards.

The guidelines develop recommendations for the
comprehensive care of patients between the ages of 7
and 18 years, and > 18 years with permanent moderate
and severe visual impairment of the functional spectrum of visual disability who have visual function alterations that complicate or prevent performing activities
of daily living, occupation, and participation.
These guidelines do not include patients with the
following characteristics:
− < 7 years of age
− With mild visual impairment since both their visual
condition and procedures regarding management are
different to those performed in the context of low
vision
− Surgical treatments are not included.

Objective of the guidelines
The aim of the study was to develop evidence-based
recommendations systematically for the diagnosis, functional rehabilitation, and follow-up of people with low vision between 7 and 18 years of age and > 18 years,
which can provide support for health-care personnel and
patients for the comprehensive management of people
with this condition to improve their participation and functionality, and optimize the health-care process within the
Colombian Health System.

Drafting the questions while defining and
rating the outcomes
According to the scope, purposes, and objectives of
the guidelines, questions were asked with the Population, Exposure or Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes,
and Time (PECOT) strategy. Afterward, the group developing the guidelines and the patients rated the outcomes
independently following the GRADE system8. Each outcome was graded from 1 to 9. Medians between 7 and
9, 4 and 6, and 1 and 3 were categorized as critical,
non-critical yet significant, and unimportant, respectively8. Other clinical practice guidelines on low vision or
visual disability were looked for and assessed. Furthermore, additional information was considered using the
following criteria: availability of the full text in English or
Spanish, target population of patients > 7 years with low
vision and whose overall score with the Appraisal of
Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II)
tool was > 40%9. The inclusion and exclusion criteria
were used to select the clinical practice guidelines. They
were assessed using the AGREE II too by two independent investigators9. A total of three clinical practice
guidelines were included that met the selection criteria.

Systematic search for evidence
The systematic search for evidence included the
following databases: Cochrane Library, Embase,
PubMed, Bireme, and Epistemonikos. The members of
the development group conducted manual and free
searches through Current Controlled Trials, Trip Database, and Google Scholar from the beginning until

November and December 2019. Furthermore, they
were updated between June and July 2020. MeSH
terms were defined and selected, systematic reviews
and meta-analyses were looked for and, additionally,
clinical trials and observational studies.

Study selection and evidence description
The group that developed the guidelines was categorized into three groups including, at least, one or two
thematic experts and a methodological one. Eligibility criteria were established for the articles regarding the methodological design, population, intervention, and minimum
quality characteristics. Studies were excluded because
they did not answer the PECOT question or because
there was better or more recent evidence. The study selection by title and abstract regarding the treatment questions and scales was conducted by the three different
epidemiologists who selected the studies that should be
fully read. The thematic experts conducted the matrix of
the characteristics of the studies with the variables number
of study, title, author, journal, year, population, intervention
or exposure, comparator, and outcomes. Afterward, all selected studies were described.

Quality of evidence
The quality of evidence of controlled clinical trials was
supervised based on criteria defined by Cochrane: inadequate generation of the sequence, lack of allocation
concealment and random allocation to therapy, masking, and loss to follow-up. Regarding systematic reviews
and meta-analyses, the A Measurement Tool to Assess
Systematic Reviews tool was used10. Regarding primary
diagnostic studies, the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies tool11 was used. Regarding primary
observational studies, the Strengthening the Reporting
of Observational studies in Epidemiology methodology
was used12. Assessment of the quality of the body of
evidence was conducted based on the concepts the
GRADE methodology. The assessment of the quality of
evidence is associated with confidence that the true
effect is close to the estimated effect. Therefore, it was
defined in four levels: very low, low, moderate, and high.
The score was obtained after grading each outcome.
The systematic reviews of clinical trials begin scoring
with high quality (level 1) while reviews of observational
studies begin with low quality (level 4). The aspects that
can lower quality are bias risk, result inconsistency and
in accuracy, indirect evidence, and publication bias. One
point (−1) or two points (−2) are subtracted based on
192

severity and damage for each aspect. Although observational studies can lower quality due to the aspects
mentioned, they can also increase quality when certain
favorable methodological aspects are included. The
three factors that can increase quality are the presence
of a large effect size (relative risk, > 2.0 or < 0.5), the
evidence of a dosage-response relation gradient, and
the lack of residual bias or confounding factors13-18.
Most quality of evidence for these guidelines was low
or very low quality of evidence.

From evidence to recommendation
Following the GRADE system, recommendations were
drafted based on these judgments: problem is a priority,
desirable effects, unwanted effects, certainty of evidence,
values, balance between sought and unsought effects,
required resources, certainty of evidence of resources
required, cost-effectiveness, equity, acceptability, and feasibility. With these criteria, a table was built including the
summary of judgments. Furthermore, the direction and
strength of the recommendation were decided19.
The strength of recommendations was discussed with
the entire group designing the guidelines, and was ranked
as “strong for” or “strong against,” “weak for,” and “weak
against.” Two socializations were made with experts: one
at the beginning of the process to present the methodology
and the questions of the guidelines, and the other at the
end to present recommendations. The experts’ observations were analyzed and the suggestions discussed within
the group. Eventually, the pertinent were accepted.

The health-care process of people with low vision requires a comprehensive approach including different interventions and professionals are included who, all together,
contribute to lessen the experience of the disability of
people with low vision. The recommendations issued
guide the health-care process of the health professionals
regarding diagnosis, management, therapy, functional rehabilitation, and clinical follow-up of people with low vision
> 7 years. Furthermore, they guide the patients and their
caregivers regarding health-care aspects the recommendations, their quality of evidence, strength, and degree of
recommendation as shown in table 1.

Visual acuity
Assessment of VA for far sight is advised with the
Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS
chart) compared to the assessment with the Snellen
chart to detect alterations or deficiencies in VA for far
sight in people > 7 years with low vision. (The quality
of evidence regarding VA was low. Weak recommendation is given favorable to the use of the ETDRS
chart).
The systematic review conducted by Chou et al.22
back in 2009 identified six studies23-27, but none of them
directly compared the diagnostic accuracy of VA assessment to the ETDRS chart and Snellen regarding
sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratio or predictive values. In the systematic review of the medical literature
available on VA screening-of moderate quality – «all»
methods are advised22. In the five articles of agreement/reproducibility, the ETDRS test performed better
thanks to having less variability compared to the Snellen chart, although the use of the ETDRS test23-27 involves longer application times. Despite of this and
considering the lower variability, greater agreement,
and reproducibility of the ETDRS compared to the
Snellen test, and the design characteristics and its logarithmic progression, it is advised to add this test to
determine and quantify the capacity of VA discrimination more accurately, and therefore be able to identify
whether the person is in the low vision range or not.
Probably when balancing sought and unsought effects,
the implementation of the ETDRS test is promoted because the only unwanted effect reported had to do with
the additional time associated with its use.

Implementation considerations
The implementation of the ETDRS charts and the decision to update clinical care involves costs in materials
and personnel training27. The ETDRS chart costs between USD 40 and USD 45. The backlit ones costs
between USD 2500 and USD 400028. Similarly, the
health-care professional training process should be reinforce for the proper use and interpretation of ETDRS.

Contrast sensitivity
Assessment of contrast sensitivity is advised with the
Pelli-Robson test to detect contrast sensitivity alterations or deficiencies in people > 7 years with visual
impairment as an additional way to determine VA. (The
quality of evidence regarding contrast sensitivity was
very low. Recommendation favorable to the use of the
Pelli Robson test is weak.) Mars test is advised for the
lack of the Pelli-Robson test.
Five articles on contrast sensitivity assessment with
the Pelli-Robson test were identified, the Mars test or the
Functional Acuity Contrast Test in people with eye conditions that can cause visual deficiencies in moderate-to-severe categories29-33. No systematic reviews or
controlled clinical trials were found. None of the studies
made direct comparisons of diagnostic precision or the
discrimination capacity of the contrast sensitivity tests
among them. Both the Pelli-Robson and Mars tests had
similar reproducibility and excellent agreement. However,
the former had an area under the Receiver Operating
Characteristic curve of 0.89, which is indicative of an
excellent discrimination capability32. The effects of implementing the use of the Pelli-Robson test basically translate into an improved visual and ocular healthcare since
it allows us to determine and quantify visual quality with
greater precision in people with visual deficiencies in the
low vision range, which is essential to perform activities
of daily living. Its clinical significant was revealed in studies that confirm that many ophthalmological conditions
and disorders such as glaucoma34, macular degeneration35, diabetic retinopathy36, and cataracts37, among others, show measurable contrast sensitivity losses even in
cases where VA can be in the normal range38.

Implementation considerations
Implementation of the Pelli-Robson test and the decision to update clinical care involve cost in equipment and
personnel training. The cost of acquiring the Pelli-Robson
and the Mars tests go from USD 1000 to USD 1500, and
from USD 200 to USD 300, respectively.

Visual field
Visual field assessment is advised through microperimetry compared to digital campimetry examination to
detect visual field alterations in people > 7 years with
low vision. If this test is not available, the use of digital
campimetry is advised. (The quality of evidence regarding visual field was low. Recommendation favorable to the use of microperimetry was weak).
Seven diagnostic precision studies were selected39-45, six of which are favorable to microperimetry
and one does not identify any differences between
microperimetry and digital campimetry. The reproducibility reported is better for microperimetry compared to
digital campimetry39,41,45. It was found that the area
under the curve for microperimetry was greater compared to digital campimetry (0.0190 and 0.735, respectively)39,42. The MP-3 microperimeter has a better
structure-function relationship and correlates better
with optical coherence tomography40. The use of microperimetry allows us to determine the patient’s visual
field alterations and improve the prognosis of the visual
condition since the earlier visual field deficiencies are
found, the better they can be treated and rehabilitated.
Regarding the presence of ocular diseases, several
studies have confirmed their utility to assess visual
function in patients with eccentric or unstable fixation44
and analyze different retinla diseases such as age-related macular degeneration and glaucoma46.

Implementation considerations
Although microperimetry is used in different settings
not only for diagnosis, but also for vision rehabilitation,
the process of training human resources, as well as its
use and interpretation should be reinforced since it is
a test that is just beginning to be used in Latin America
and Colombia.

Treatment recommendations
Electronic optical aids
Use of electronic optical aids is advised for people
> 16 years with low vision like closed-circuit TV mounted
on stands, portable electronic devices with mouse and
mouse-free hand-held electronic devices (like the portable electronic magnifier) to improve reading speed, time,
and acuity, and quality of life. (The quality of evidence
regarding electronic optical aids is moderate. Recommendation given favorable to its use was weak).

There is a wide variety of priority back-up products
or low vision aids considered like any other device that
allows somebody with low vision to improve his visual
performance. The evidence found supported mainly by
the meta-analyses conducted by Virgili et al.47 shows
that reading speed can improve with closed-circuit TV
mounted on a stand compared to hand-held or
stand-mounted magnifiers (evidence of low quality). On
the other hand, reading time is longer with electronic
compared to ordinary optical aids devices, and they are
also easier to use (moderate evidence). In addition,
reading time is longer with electronic devices with a
camera mounted on a mouse compared to traditional
optical aids (moderate evidence). Finally, only one
study reported showed that reading time is longer at
1 month when the patients were prescribed closed-circuit TV at the beginning and not only optical aids. Regarding good clinical practice, it is advised that if
electronic aids are unavailable, optical aids such as
microscopes, magnifying glasses (any available), and
transparent filters should be used to improve reading
speed, time, and acuity48-50.

Implementation considerations
Electronic aids are not readily available in the market
in Colombia. Most of them need to be imported and are
not covered by the health-care system51. Price goes
from USD 190 for electronic magnifiers, USD 280 for
closed-circuit TVs, and USD 2796 for head-mounted
devices. Studies published in other countries show that
they are cost-efective51. Patients would accept interventions with electronic optical aids if they could assess
their effectiveness, and accept them based on size,
portability, and accessibility.

Manual or electronic magnifiers, telescopes,
and tablets

Use of manual or electronic magnifiers, telescopes,
and tablets is advised in children and teenagers with
low vision between the ages of 7 and 16 years to improve reading speed, VA, reading comprehension,
learning, perform activities of daily living, participation,
and quality of life. (The quality of evidence regarding
hand-held or electronic magnifiers, telescopes, and
tablets was very low. Recommendation given favorable
to its use was weak). The optical aids commonly used
in people between 7 and 16 years include hand-held
magnifiers with a stand (with and without lighting), magnifying reading glasses with high dioptric power of up

optical magnifiers, stand magnifiers without lighting and
electronic devices for near vision, telescopes for far
vision, and tablets with their corresponding software
and applications should be used, as well as non-optical
aids like filters, reading stands, large print, and lamps
to improve reading and comprehension, learning,
independence in activities of daily living, and social
participation54-57.

Implementation considerations
The Colombian Health-care System should be reinforced since back-up devices are not included in the
Health-care Benefit Plan. In addition, there are not
enough human resources available trained in the process of prescription and training in this category of aids,
and the ones that do exist are in large cities58. Barriers
are recognized regarding cost and availability of aids
in the country, as well as fear of stereotypes associated
with disability that can cause these people to oppose
the use of optical aids.

Rehabilitation recommendations
Rehabilitation in people > 16 years
Multidisciplinary and multicomponent rehabilitation is
recommended in people with low vision to improve their
health-and-vision-related quality of life, basic and instrumental activities of daily living, and their reading
ability. (The quality of evidence regarding rehabilitation
of people > 16 years was moderate. Recommendation
given favorable to multidisciplinary and multicomponent
rehabilitation was strong).
Multicomponent rehabilitation consists of vision rehabilitation programs including education and information
exchange on low vision, problem-solving skill training,
relaxation techniques, and teaching/training on the use
of optical and non-optical low vision devices59.
These rehabilitation services include a wide variety of
196

services, additional elements beyond the mere prescription, and training in the use of optical devices such as
training in orientation and mobility, occupational therapy, the acquisition of skills for independent living, expert
advice and prescription, group activities, training regarding aid devices, and educational and employment advice to facilitate continuous participation in various
activities and promote well-being60. This multicomponent model is provided through integrated or multidisciplinary services61-63. We should mention that there is
high variability in the type of interventions, comparators,
and outcomes. The recommendations issued are based
on four systematic reviews63-66 and four individual
studies67-70.

Implementation considerations
The dynamics under which rehabilitation services are
rendered in Colombia should be reinforced since the
fragmentation of the Social Security System does not
allow comprehensiveness, continuity, or opportunity of
processes71. Similarly, there are not enough healthcare institutions equipped to implement these recommendations associated with multidisciplinary and
multicomponent vision rehabilitation processes, and
the ones that do exist are located in large cities only.
In addition, there are not enough skilled human resources for this process58.

Rehabilitation of people between 7 and
16 years
Rehabilitation is advised in people between 7 and
16 years with low vision. These interventions should
include physical training and group programs and
training in the use of optical devices to improve performance in activities of daily living, participation, and
quality of life. (The quality of evidence regarding rehabilitation for people between 7 and 16 years was
low. Strong recommendation is given favorable to
its use).
Vision rehabilitation services adjust to a wide variety
of different models; some approach only the person’s
functional needs with an emphasis on provision of optical and non-optical aids while others take a more
holistic approach. The recommendation is based on the
systematic review conducted by Elsman et al.55 in
which the studies included show that providing physical
training or sports camps is probably effective to improve physical performance. Training in path-following
tasks was effective to improve fine motor skills,

Psychology

Inclusion of occupational therapy interventions in
vision rehabilitation programs targeting people with low
vision is recommended to improve reading speed, performance of basic and instrumental everyday life activities, and social participation. (The quality of
evidence regarding occupational therapy interventions

Based on the individual needs and characteristics of
the person with low vision, occupational therapy interventions or sessions in adults should be initially provided individually with short but more frequent sessions
including adaptation to low vision devices72. In certain

occupational therapy interventions can be performed in

Psychological group or individual interventions are
advised in people > 16 years with low vision based on
problem-solving self-management, educational, and
intervention programs, peer-support-based therapies,
or a combination of several strategies to improve vision-related quality of life, self-effectiveness and
self-esteem, reduce depressive symptoms, and improve independence in everyday life activities64,76.

well-designed trials are needed paying special attention to interventions adapted to the needs of elderly
patients with low vision.

Implementation considerations

Conclusions

Comprehensive care programs should be created to
better coordinate psychological interventions with the
interdisciplinary team. Similarly, an essential factor regarding implementation is the patients or their caregivers’ resistance to accept intervention in psychology.

These clinical practice guidelines have produced recommendations based on the best evidence available
regarding the diagnosis, rehabilitation therapy, vision
rehabilitation, and clinical follow-up of people > 7 years
with low vision. The GRADE approach allowed us to
develop a complete, explicit, and transparent methodology to make several specific and useful recommendations on how to approach people with low vision in
different health-care stages.
The drafting of the guidelines allows us to make an
impact by reducing unwanted variability in the diagnosis, rehabilitation, and follow-up of people with low vision. The guidelines provide general outlines for the
comprehensive management of this condition and use
of therapies and clinical approaches to optimal quality
standards, which is expected to translate into a less
potentially negative impact of low vision as a type of
visual disability in the patients’ activities of daily living.
The recommendations issued are essential to cope
with the current challenges of mankind since with an
aging world population, visual disability has become a
public health problem regarding measures to prevent
and detect, in a timely manner, visual deficiencies capable of causing permanent low vision, access to vision
rehabilitation services and programs, supply and demand of aid devices, and better knowledge of the functional diversity and quality of life of people with low
vision. Therefore, a key aspect to promote the rights of
people with visual disability is to improve their functional abilities by increasing access to vision rehabilitation
services and creating more inclusive environments by
reinforcing inclusive policies and laws. Therefore, like
Lancet Global Health Commission on Global Eye
Health puts it, we need to make sure that vision rehabilitation should be considered a comprehensive component of eye care services in our quest for universal
health-care coverage1. Still, people who seek vision
rehabilitation face a variety of challenges including,
among others, scarce services, physical barriers, inadequate skills and knowledge by the health-care team,
and lack of knowledge by other sectors of society.
These barriers contribute to the overall estimate that
only 15% of people could probably benefit from these
services if they had access to them2.

Follow-up recommendations
Follow-up scales
Use of the NEI-VFQ (National Eye Institute Visual
Function Questionnaire) is advised for the early assessment and follow-up of vision-related quality of life
in patients > 18 years with low vision. (The quality of
evidence regarding the use of follow-up scales was
high. Recommendation given favorable to the use of
NEI-VFQ was strong).
The studies that evaluated the psychometric properties of NEI-VFQ through the classic theory of the test
reported adequate psychometric properties and good
internal consistency. The test-retest stability was adequate in all validations while the validity of the construct
and the content was appropriate in several studies77-86.
There are two different versions: the original one including 51 items and the short one including 25. NEIVFQ-25 is the scale with the largest number of
validations reported and is considered the standard of
reference87. Its validity and reliability remain regarding
the original version88. This scale measures the influence of visual disability and visual symptoms in the
domains of physical and mental health of the population in addition to other domains oriented to tasks associated with daily visual performance89.
Psychometric properties show that it is a valid and
reliable scale, constant in time, and sensitive to change.
Using the scale translated, adapted, and validated to
the Colombian context is advised. The driving subscale
answers should be interpreted with caution since several patients with low vision do not drive according to
the legislation, which can change from one country to
the next.

Implementation considerations
The scale should be known and used by health-care
professionals, especially those involved with visual
care. Furthermore, they should train in scoring and result interpretation. It can take additional time in the
office to be used.
