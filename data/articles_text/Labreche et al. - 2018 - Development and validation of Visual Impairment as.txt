Development and validation of Visual
Impairment as a Risk for Falls Questionnaire
TAMMY LABRECHE, KRITHIKA NANDAKUMAR, MOHAMMED ALTHOMALI, SUSAN J. LEAT
Waterloo Optometry and Vision Science, University of Waterloo
Address correspondence to: Tammy Labreche. Tel: 1 (519) 888-4567 ext. 37742; Fax: 1 (519) 746-2337.
Email: tammy.labreche@uwaterloo.ca

Abstract
Purpose: visual impairment is associated with an increased risk of falls, yet eye care professionals are infrequently members
of falls prevention clinics. The aim of this preliminary study was to validate a newly created Visual Impairment as a Risk for
Falls Questionnaire intended to be used by those professionals not involved in eye care.
Methods: about 53 participants with various visual impairments known to be associated with falls and 33 participants with normal
sight were contacted within 4 months of a full oculo-visual assessment and were asked the questions from the current questionnaire
pertaining to their visual function. A retrospective ﬁle review was undertaken and the sensitivity and speciﬁcity of participants’
responses were calculated compared to the actual vision impairment based on the ﬁndings from their visual assessment.
Results: the question regarding ability to read was included to identify people with central vision loss, a risk factor for
falling. It was found to have sensitivity of 74% and speciﬁcity of 87%. Both sensitivity and speciﬁcity improved when
participants with cognitive impairment were excluded. The question on recognising facial features gave sensitivity of
73% and speciﬁcity of 97% for this subgroup. However, questions related to impairments in stereopsis and peripheral
ﬁelds were not useful.
Conclusion: the study demonstrates that several questions of the new questionnaire are useful; however, further testing
with a larger population is needed to fully validate the questionnaire for use by health care professionals.
Keywords: low vision, falls, fall prevention questionnaire, screening, older people

Background
It has been well established that advancing age is a risk factor for falling. In fact, 28–35% of adults over 65 years,
experience a fall each year [1]. The impact of a fall can be
disastrous, leading to both physical and psychological
impairment and increased mortality [2–5]. With an aging
population, the number of older individuals falling is likely
to increase, necessitating the need to prevent the occurrence
of falls. Age, however, is not the sole risk factor for a fall,
with the underlying cause often being multifactorial in
nature [1, 4, 6–8]. Vision plays an integral role in maintaining posture and balance. This is evidenced in the ‘subway
platform’ phenomenon; wherein an individual’s peripheral
view of a moving object leads to the illusion of self-motion,
or through increasing postural sway by removing vision
simply by closing one’s eyes [9]. A deﬁciency in vision can
lead to an increased risk of a fall [1, 2, 6, 7]. Impairment in
visual acuity, stereopsis, contrast sensitivity or visual ﬁeld
has been consistently reported to increase the likelihood of

falling [10]. Visual acuity refers to an individual’s ability to
distinguish objects at a speciﬁc distance [11]. Contrast sensitivity refers to an individual’s ability to distinguish lower
contrast objects. Stereopsis is the ability to see objects in
depth and visual ﬁeld is a measure of the extent of peripheral vision. An unsuitable spectacle prescription has also
been identiﬁed as a risk factor for falling [3, 10]. In order to
address the multifactorial nature of a fall, multidisciplinary
fall prevention clinics have been created. Eye care professionals, however, are often not consulted in Canada and
possibly in other countries, and are seldom invited as participating members of the multidisciplinary clinic team.
The American Geriatrics Society/British Geriatrics Society
(AGS/BGS) Clinical Practice Guideline for the prevention of
falls in older persons dictates that ‘Patients should be asked to
identify and describe any vision-related problems and concerns. If they report problems or concerns, their vision should
be formally assessed and any remediable visual abnormalities
should be treated, particularly cataracts [12].’ While several
questionnaires detailing visual function currently exist, they are

function measure [13, 15]. The Visual Impairment as a Risk

tool to assist in the recognition of a visual risk for a fall. It is

falling, to assist with triage and appropriate referral for eye
care. The purpose of this preliminary study was to develop/
compile and validate the questionnaire, ensuring that the questions chosen accurately identiﬁed a visual impairment.

Method
This cross-sectional study received clearance through a
University of Waterloo Research Ethics Committee and
adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.
The questionnaire was developed from existing questionnaires with the addition of one newly created question.
Three initial background questions (A–C, see Figure 1)
were selected to determine the date of the last eye exam,
and spectacle use. Questions A and B were adapted from
the Saskatoon Health Region Fall—Risk Screening: MultiFactorial Questionnaire [13], and the Desmond Fall Risk
Questionnaire [14], respectively, and C was newly created.
Questions 2–7 were chosen and revised in order to attempt
to accurately identify impaired visual function measures that
are deemed risk factors for falling. Questions 2–6 were
modiﬁed from similar questions found in the National Eye
Institute Visual Functioning Questionnaire-25 (VFQ-25)
[14]. The third question was adapted from both the
Saskatoon Questionnaire and the VFQ-25 [13, 14].
Question one was an altered version of that found in the
Desmond Fall Risk Questionnaire [15]. The last question
was derived from the Independent Mobility Questionnaire
[16]. The questions were prefaced by the prompt ‘with your
glasses on’ to serve as a reminder that the response should
be based on their habitual vision rather than the need for

glasses. It does not preclude those who do not wear glasses
from responding. Aside from the preliminary questions A–
C, all questions required a yes/no response. A copy of the
questionnaire is shown in Figure 1.
Participants with and without a visual impairment were
recruited consecutively from the Waterloo Optometry and
Vision Science (WOVS) Primary Care (general care), Ocular
Health (ocular disease management) and Low Vision (vision
rehabilitation) Clinics by a research assistant. To ensure that
it was possible to validate questions identiﬁed those with
particular visual function impairments, the sample was deliberately enriched with those with visual impairment by
recruiting from the Ocular Health and Low Vision Clinic.
The study participants were adults over the age of 65 years
and who were able to speak English. Both female and male
participants were invited. The participants had had a full eye
exam at WOVS within the previous four months.
Individuals who agreed to participate were provided information about the study and if consent obtained, they were
interviewed by phone and asked to answer the questions of
the VIRFQ and the TICS-M (Telephone Interview
Cognitive Status-Modiﬁed) [17]. The telephone interviews
were conducted between May and August 2013 and 2014. A
retrospective chart review was then conducted. The individual’s age, last complete eye examination, type of spectacle
use (e.g. single vision, bifocals, progressive lenses), best corrected distance and near visual acuities, contrast sensitivity, stereoacuity, and visual ﬁeld assessments (central and/or
peripheral) were retrieved. Participants who had some form
of visual impairment were grouped into three impairment
categories: Group 1. Visual acuity (VA) < 0.1 logMAR
(<20/25) in the better eye, OR contrast sensitivity (CS) <
1.4, OR central ﬁeld defect, Group 2. Asymmetric acuities
>0.1 logMAR difference between the eyes or an ocular deviation and Group 3. Peripheral ﬁeld defect. Participants were
considered to have a central defect if they reported metamorphopsia, blur or scotomas on Amsler testing and were
considered to have a peripheral ﬁeld impairment if documented as ‘constriction’ on confrontation visual ﬁelds, or
having a defect of any type (constriction/step/hemianopia/
quadrantanopia) identiﬁed via perimetry.

Preliminary questions
A. When was your last eye exam?
B. Do you wear bifocals, trifocals or progressive bifocals (no-line bifocals)?
C. Do you walk while wearing them?
For the following questions, please answer for when you are wearing your regular glasses.
1. With your glasses on, is your vision notably better in one eye compared to the other?
2. With your regular reading glasses or bifocals on, do you have difficulty reading the newspaper?
3. Because of your eyesight, do you have difficulty noticing objects off to the side?
4. Because of your eyesight, do you have difficulty going down steps or stairs or have trouble
seeing a curb?
5. With your glasses on, are you able to distinguish a person’s facial features from across a room?
6. With your glasses on, do you have difficulty seeing the television?
7. With your glasses on, do you trip over objects because you do not see them?

Analysis: The sensitivities and speciﬁcities were calculated treating the above three impairment categories as the
gold standard diagnosis and the VIRFQ questions (1–7) as
the test predictors. See Table 1 for grouping of questions
relevant for each category. For question 5, an impairment
was indicated if a ‘no’ response was given because of the
direction of the question, instead of the ‘yes’ response to all
the other questions. The 2 × 2 tables were constructed for
each impairment category versus questions selected as
detectors of the impairment, and the sensitivity and speciﬁcity (with 95% conﬁdence intervals) of each question were
calculated. Questions 2 and 4–6 were analysed for Group 1
participants, Question 1 was analysed for Group 2 and
Questions 3, 4 and 7 were evaluated for Group 3. It is possible that an individual with asymmetric acuities would also
fall into the group with reduced acuity or peripheral vision
loss. They were therefore analysed in both groups.
Participants were further subdivided to identify responses
of participants with a TICS-M score >21 and the same analysis undertaken for each group separately to determine if
the questionnaire was more valid for those without a cognitive impairment. Finally, all the questions were evaluated as
an aggregate (a response to any one question being a positive response) for detecting any visual impairment as
deﬁned above (including Groups 1, 2 and 3).

Results
Ninety-three individuals participated in the study. One participant was excluded because s/he did not wish to answer
the TICS-M. Two were excluded as they did not complete
the questions of the VIRFQ and one declined to answer
any questions after agreeing to participate. The ﬁnal sample
included 89 participants, aged 65–96. Age and TICS-M
scores are described for visually impaired and not visually
impaired individuals according to each impairment group in
Table 2. About 65 (73%) of participants wore some form
of multifocal spectacle prescription with 60 (67%) stating
that they wore the glasses while walking.
Question number 2 yielded the most promising sensitivity
(74%) and speciﬁcity (86%) values for Group 1 impairments.

These values increased when considering only those with a
TICS-M > 21–87% and 92%, respectively. Question 5 also
gave good values with a sensitivity of 73% and speciﬁcity of
97% for detecting Group 1 impairment. The remaining
questions had a high speciﬁcity (90–98%) but low sensitivity.
Question 1 for Group 2 against question 1, gave moderate
sensitivity and speciﬁcity (68/58%) which decreased when
considering those only with TICS-M > 21. Questions 3, 4
and 7 for Group 3 gave high speciﬁcity (81–86%) but lower
sensitivity. This did not change signiﬁcantly for those with
TICS-M > 21.
Questions 1–7 in aggregate (a positive response from
any question) for detecting any visual impairment, gave
high sensitivity (88%) but the speciﬁcity was low (48%) and
did not change signiﬁcantly when including only those with
TICs-M > 21.

Discussion
The VIRFQ was developed to assist health professionals
not involved in eye care to identify individuals who may be
at risk of falling due to suboptimal vision or visual appliances. The initial preliminary (A) question was included as
the best intervention is prevention. The patient should be
referred for an eye examination if one was not conducted
within the past year. The prevalence of ocular disease
increases with age but many ocular diseases do not present
with symptoms at the onset, necessitating regular routine
eye examinations [4]. In the older age group, yearly routine
eye examinations are effective at detecting new eye diseases
and prescription changes [18]. Prompt extraction of ﬁrst
eye cataract has been demonstrated to decrease the falls
risk [19]. Inappropriate spectacle prescription has also been
linked with an increased falls risk [10]. Older individuals
should have the most up to date spectacle prescription, taking care that the prescription is not changed greatly to allow
for adaptation [3]. For active individuals, there should be
consideration of separate distance glasses for walking [20]
and other similar activities as the bifocal add portion blurs
the area where a person steps and also alters depth perception [21]. Attention should be given to lens tint to reduce

There are several studies that demonstrate that a
decrease in visual acuity is a falls risk [4]. The Beaver Dam
Study found that those over the age of 60 with a habitual
acuity of 20/25 or less were 2.6 times more likely to have
multiple falls [22] while the Blue Mountain Study stated
that an acuity of less than 20/30 was associated with 1.9×
greater risk of multiple falls [23]. Currently there is no
established cut-off for acuity loss associated with an
increased risk of falling [4]. Contrast sensitivity is also
believed to be a signiﬁcant falls risk as studies show that it
is a good predictor of postural stability and mobility [24].
Individuals with a contrast sensitivity of less than 1.4 are
considered to have an early impairment affecting the ability
to read, and as such, this was chosen as the limit for CS
loss in this study [25]. As with acuity, there is no established
degree of contrast sensitivity loss associated with increased
prevalence of falls [4] and it is difﬁcult to isolate reduced
contrast sensitivity from visual acuity loss when there is a
central defect. Therefore, impairments in acuity, contrast
sensitivity and central defects were grouped together. The
results indicate that question 2 effectively identiﬁes individuals with these impairments as well as possibly question 5
for those with TICs-M > 21; however, a larger sample size
would provide stronger evidence.
When one considers vision and falling, it would seem
obvious that a loss within the inferior visual ﬁeld would
lead to an increase in falls due to the individual’s ability to
detect trip hazards, and this holds true [4]. However, those
who have head injuries as a result of other forms of peripheral ﬁeld loss [26] or central ﬁeld impairment [27] are also
more likely to fall. Unfortunately, in our study sample, there
was an insufﬁcient number of individuals with measured
peripheral visual ﬁeld loss (n = 10) to accurately determine
whether the questions chosen were valid. Further research
with speciﬁc types of peripheral ﬁeld defects is required.
The ﬁnal visual measure often identiﬁed as being a risk
factor for falls is impairment in stereopsis. Lord and Dayhew
concluded that stereopsis was the greatest visual risk factor
for multiple falls [10]. In that study, those with asymmetric
acuities, deﬁned as at least one line of difference between
eyes, had a similar risk for falls as those with poor visual acuity in the better eye [10]. Felson et al. showed a higher risk for
those who had a similar inter-ocular difference [28]. Question
1 was evaluated for this group, however, the speciﬁcity and
sensitivity using this criterion was not promising.
Interestingly, the two questions (numbers 2 and 5) which
were found to have the best sensitivity and speciﬁcity were
those adapted from the VFQ-25 [13] and used in the 2006
PALs survey of Canadians who identify as having a seeing
disability [29].

A few limitations to this study were identiﬁed. A greater
sample size for those with visual ﬁeld impairment is recommended. Data utilised in the study was routinely collected
clinical data. Thus visual ﬁelds and stereopsis were not
measured on many patients. Additionally identiﬁcation of
visual ﬁeld defects relied largely on confrontation testing,
leading to a less accurate classiﬁcation. Also, the assumption
was made that participants were using the most up to date
prescription as recorded in their clinical ﬁle which would
yield optimal acuities, but this may not always be the case.
Finally, The WOVS clinic is in an area of slightly above
average socioeconomic status compared to the province of
Ontario but [30] the clinic population is similar in terms of
patient age and sex distribution to a nationwide survey
of Canadian optometric practices [31].

Conclusion
While this study demonstrated that those with a reduced
visual acuity, contrast sensitivity and central defect would be
accurately identiﬁed by the VIRFQ, further testing with a
greater sample size is required using a research population
with controlled measures of visual function. The preliminary three questions would be included, to identify those
who would beneﬁt from an eye examination and advice
regarding their type of spectacles lenses. A useful, yet succinct visual risk for falls questionnaire would prove helpful
in identifying individuals who have an increased falls risk
due to vision loss.

Key points
• Visual impairment is a risk factor falls.
• Visual impairment should be considered when assessing a
patient who is at risk for falling or has a history of falls.
• The Validation of Visual Impairment as a Risk for Falls
Questionnaire (VIRFQ) is a questionnaire that helps identify those who have an impairment in acuity, contrast sensitivity and central ﬁeld.
• Additional research is required to further develop the
questionnaire to identify those with impairments in peripheral vision and depth perception.
