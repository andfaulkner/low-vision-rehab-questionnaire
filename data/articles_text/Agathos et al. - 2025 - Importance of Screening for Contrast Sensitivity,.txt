Importance of screening for contrast sensitivity, falls, and mobility
limitations in older adults with maculopathy
Catherine P Agathos 1,a, Natela M Shanidze 1, Donald C Fletcher 1
Author information Article notes Copyright and License information
PMCID: PMC12462652 NIHMSID: NIHMS2111896 PMID: 40889625
The publisher's version of this article is available at Am J Ophthalmol

Abstract
Objective:
Assess the potential value of adding questions on falls/balance difficulties due to vision loss and testing
contrast sensitivity in patients with maculopathy (mainly age-related macular degeneration, AMD) to
help guide referral for mobility rehabilitation.

Design:
Cross-sectional study.


[page 2]
Participants:
125 patients over 55 years old with binocular maculopathy and intact peripheral vision presenting for a
low vision rehabilitation consultation.

Methods:
Patients were asked questions regarding difficulties with their balance and mobility, including the 9-item
Glaucoma Activity Limitation questionnaire (GAL-9). For analysis, patients were grouped by visual
impairment severity using better-eye visual acuity (VA) and binocular contrast sensitivity (CS).

Main Outcome Measures:
Visual function, self-reported balance and mobility difficulties, falls history, and rehabilitation referrals
were examined across visual impairment severity groups.

Results:
52% of patients reported balance or gait difficulties and 36% attributed mobility limitations to vision
loss. Increasing vision deficit severity related to greater mobility concerns (>50% of patients in the
severe categories rated difficulty as 3/5 or higher) and higher referral rates. Scores on the GAL-9
increased with greater vision loss and were predicted by contrast sensitivity, female sex, larger relative
scotomata and falls history (R2 = 0.45, p < 0.0001). CS was a better predictor of mobility difficulties
than VA. Based on reported concerns, 31.2% of patients were referred to orientation and mobility
training and/or physical therapy (>50% in the severe groups).

Conclusions:
Mobility limitations and falls affect a substantial proportion of AMD patients yet may go undiscussed in
eye clinics. Incorporating screening questions and explicit discussion of mobility difficulties in clinical
practice in maculopathy can help elucidate patient mobility limitations. Realization of these limitations is
the prerequisite to initiate appropriate referrals to low vision and mobility specialists to improve mobility
and manage falls risk. Clinical practice may also benefit from assessment of contrast sensitivity as this is
a better indicator of difficulties in daily living. Our results, along with prior literature, underscore the
need to recognize the role of central vision loss, and vision loss more broadly, in mobility decline. Thus, a


[page 3]
central vision loss-specific mobility questionnaire may be needed to facilitate patient screening in the
future.
Keywords: central field loss, age-related macular degeneration, screening, falls, mobility, aging, contrast

sensitivity

Introduction
Falls are a major and growing concern as they are the second leading cause of unintentional injury
deaths worldwide1 and the mortality rate of falls in individuals over 65 is increasing2. Falls among older
adults are dangerous and costly: in the US alone, falls-related costs are projected to increase to $100
billion by 20303.
Visual impairment impacts mobility, increases fall risk, and is most prevalent among older adults4–6. Age related visual impairments due to peripheral retinal damage, (e.g., glaucoma), are considered significant
contributors to mobility limitations7,8 given that peripheral vision is important for motion perception9,10,
spatial orientation11, and balance and locomotion12,13. The association between falls and central visual
impairment (e.g., age-related macular degeneration, AMD), however, is less emphasized clinically14,15,
despite a body of work revealing mobility difficulties16–21, increased fall risk22–24 and fall-related
anxiety25–28 in these patients, all of which reduce quality of life, and increase incidence of depression
and mortality29. AMD is typically associated with and assessed in terms of loss of high acuity vision30–33,
as patients usually notice visual impairment when they experience difficulty with high-acuity tasks34. As
a result, these tasks represent primary complaints35 and falls are typically not considered a priority in
AMD care36. Additionally, although central visual field loss is associated with decline in contrast
sensitivity34,37, which is important for successful performance of daily tasks including balance and
mobility19,38–46, standard clinical practice for maculopathy does not include contrast sensitivity
assessment47. Given that AMD is the most common cause of irreversible age-related visual impairment in
industrialized countries48, affecting nearly 200 million individuals worldwide49 and increasing with the
aging population50; failure to systematically consider its effects on balance and mobility can lead to
significant consequences for the autonomy, safety, and longevity of this population.
This study aimed to assess the potential value of a short falls/balance and mobility screening during low
vision consultations for the management of central visual field loss. Additionally, given the lack of a
central field loss-specific equivalent, we used the Glaucoma Activity Limitation questionnaire (GAL-951)
to further probe patients’ mobility difficulties. Finally, in addition to assessing patients’ self-reports of
balance and mobility concerns relative to the severity of their vision loss, we also report resultant patient
referral rates for mobility rehabilitation.

Methods
Participants
This cross-sectional study was reviewed by the Smith-Kettlewell Eye Research Institute’s (SKERI)
Institutional Review Board (IRB) and conforms with the the tenets of the declaration of Helsinki and the
Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) regulations. Questionnaire, visual function,
and demographic data were collected for 125 patients with maculopathy (26 males) who visited DCF’s
clinical practice at the Bicknell Envision Vision Rehabilitation Center in Wichita, KS over a one-year
period. Inclusion criteria were the presence of binocular maculopathy and an intact peripheral visual
field. Participants represent patients who were referred to DCF’s clinic for low vision rehabilitation. Male
and female sex was defined as having one or two X chromosomes respectively; in our sample, all
participants had no more than two sex chromosomes. Prior to the start of their appointment, patients
were asked for their consent to be asked additional (mobility-related) questions and for de-identified
data collected during the appointment to be used for research and shared with the study team. All
patients invited to participate agreed to do so. All patients with glaucoma, retinitis pigmentosa,
neurological vision loss, diabetic retinopathy, or other peripheral vision loss were excluded. Patients
presenting with other concomitant eye pathologies such as retinal vascular occlusions, cataracts, or other
ocular media opacities were also excluded. Out of 125 patients, 9 had hereditary maculopathy
(Stargardt’s disease) and the other 116 presented with age-related macular degeneration. Maculopathy
was confirmed via macular perimetry using the Scanning Laser Ophthalmoscope (MAIA, CenterVue,
Fremont, CA, USA) monocularly in each eye. Intact peripheral visual fields were confirmed using
confrontation visual field testing and medical history.

Visual function testing and visual impairment severity grouping
Visual acuity (VA) was measured monocularly in each eye, with habitual correction, using the ETDRS
chart at 0.5, 1.0, or 2.0 meters (according to individuals’ abilities). Contrast sensitivity (CS) was
measured binocularly, with habitual correction, at 30 cm using the LEA symbols test52, which provides
figures between 25% and 0.6% contrast in six increments with score equivalents of 0.6 to 2.2 logCS. The
LEA test is used in DCF’s clinical practice as it does not require familiarity with the roman alphabet, thus
accommodating a wide range of ages and linguistic backgrounds. The California Central Visual Field Test
(CCVFT)53 was used to estimate dense and relative binocular scotoma sizes. Horizontal and vertical
scotoma diameters were averaged.


[page 5]
We examined questionnaire responses against vision metrics along a continuum and by grouping
individuals according to visual impairment severity. Visual impairment severity as defined by the
International Classification of Diseases (ICD) is based on visual acuity of the better eye and influences
patient care and eligibility for benefits/legal rights. Given contrast sensitivity’s higher relevance to
mobility and daily living tasks19,38–46, we also categorized visual impairment severity based on binocular
CS. For VA-based grouping, we used standard WHO ICD-11 cutoffs54 of mild: VA < 0.54 logMAR (<
20/70), moderate: VA = 0.54 – 1.0 logMAR (20/70 to 20/200), and severe acuity vision loss: VA >1.0
logMAR (>20/200). For contrast sensitivity-based grouping, we classified our participants based on
prior work55; mild: CS ≥ 1.6 logCS (≤2.5% contrast), moderate: 1.30 logCS (5%), and severe contrast
vision loss: CS ≤1.0 logCS (≥10%).

Mobility-related Questions and Referrals
Patients were asked general questions relating to their mobility and balance (Table 2, G1–5), followed by
nine questions pertaining to activity-specific task scenarios (GAL-951), to which participants reported
their degree of difficulties on a 5-point Likert scale (Table 2, Q1–9). We chose the GAL-9 given its focus
on mobility scenarios in daily life for visually impaired individuals (albeit with glaucoma), and its short
length – key consideration for an in-consultation screening tool. While the GAL-9 was developed for
peripheral vision loss, most mobility concerns it assesses are common across low vision. We used this
tool to highlight mobility-related issues often overlooked in individuals with maculopathies in the clinic.
Developing and validating a maculopathy-specific analog to the GAL-9 was beyond the scope of the
current study.

As part of DCF’s standard practice, all patients are referred to occupational therapy (OT). Referrals to
orientation and mobility training (O&M) and/or physical therapy (PT) were made on a case-by-case
basis for patients with significant mobility/balance complaints, and considering their insurance coverage
and other factors influencing their ability to follow-up with the referral (e.g., transportation).

Analysis
This study’s outcomes measures were patients’ responses on the falls and mobility questionnaires (Table
2) and whether they obtained referrals. All outcomes were examined across the entire patient sample
and grouped by visual impairment severity. The total score of responses on the GAL-9 was also examined
in relation to the covariates of age, sex, visual function metrics, and number of falls. The GAL-9 total
score was calculated as the sum of individual question scores (thus with a maximum score of 45). Self reports on falls, balance, and mobility difficulties were examined in terms of frequency. Statistical
relationships were examined using Spearman correlations. Normality was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk
test. We used a generalized linear model of the GAL-9 total score with age, sex, visual acuity in the better
and worse eye, binocular contrast sensitivity, binocular relative and dense scotoma size estimates, and
number of falls (G1, Table 2) as the initial set of predictor variables to identify key factors in mobility
limitations of our participant sample (Full Model in Table 3). The model was optimized using Akaike
information criterion (AIC) estimation and retaining variables that did not present high collinearity
(Preferred Model in Table 3). Alpha level was set at 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using
Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, LLC, Boston, USA).

Results
Patient Characteristics
125 patients participated in this study, including 26 males. Participant ages ranged from 56 to 98 years
(average: 80.6 ± 8.7). There were no sex differences in terms of age (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test,
p=0.14) and number of falls in the past year (Fisher’s exact test, p=0.43). Summary visual function data
are presented in Table 1 along with demographics for the entire sample and according to severity of
visual impairment. In our sample, 30.4% of patients had mild, 47.2% had moderate, and 22.4% had
severe deficit, as defined by visual acuity. Grouping by contrast sensitivity, 31.2% of participants had
mild deficitb, 46.4% had moderate, and 22.4% had severe vision deficit. The proportion of patients
grouped in the same level of visual impairment severity based on VA and CS was 59% in the mild, 60.3%
in the moderate, and 42.9% in the severe category. There were no age differences between categories for
either grouping (one-way ANOVA, p>0.2).

Self-Reports of Falls, Balance, and Mobility Complaints
41.6% of patients reported having at least one fall in the previous twelve months. The maximum
reported number of falls was 10 (one participant), and the average was 1 fall/person within each
severity group (regardless of classification type – VA or CS). Figure 1 A&B shows a detailed breakdown
of the number of falls across the cohort (peach bars) and separated into the three severity groups. While
there was a higher ratio of patients with severe impairment (teal) who had 2–4 falls, several participants
in the mild (violet) and moderate (blue) groups had the highest number of falls in a year.

In our sample, 52% of patients reported having balance or gait problems (“Balance & Gait” in Figure 1,
C&D). This incidence was closely maintained across severity groups when grouped by visual acuity of the
better eye: 50.0% of participants in the mild, 52.5% in the moderate, and 53.6% in the severe
impairment categories complained of balance or gait issues. When grouped by binocular contrast
sensitivity, however, incidence of reported balance problems increased with severity: 38.5% in the mild;
48.3% in the moderate, and 78.6% in the severe participant group.
36% of patients reported mobility difficulties which they attributed to vision deficits (“Mobility Vision” in
Figure 1C&D), compared to 31.3% with non-vision-related mobility difficulties (“Mobility Other” in
Figure 1C&D), with a 19.1% overlap (“Mobility Both” in Figure 1C&D). When grouped by VA, 23.7% in
the mild, 33.9% in the moderate, and 57.1% in the severe groups complained of mobility difficulties due
to their vision deficit. When grouped by CS, 18.0% in the mild, 31.0% in the moderate, and 71.4% in the
severe groups complained of vision deficit-related mobility difficulties.
When grouped by VA, 21.6% in the mild, 30.2% in the moderate, and 48.0% in the severe groups said
they had mobility difficulties that were not related to vision loss. When participants were grouped by CS,
20.5% in the mild, 26.4% in the moderate, and 60.9% in the severe groups reported mobility difficulties
that were not vision related.

Activity limitation questionnaire responses
The average GAL-9 total score for the entire sample was 21.0 ± 7.9. Figure 2 A&B shows mean total
scores on the GAL-9 questionnaire for patients grouped according to visual impairment severity. Mean
total scores were significantly higher in patients with severe as opposed to moderate and mild visual
impairment, whether severity grouping was based on VA or CS (one-way ANOVA with Tukey adjustment
for multiple comparisons for VA-grouping: F(2,97) = 8.05, p < 0.001; mean scores per VA group:
meanmild = 18.0 ± 8.0; meanmoderate = 20.8 ± 7.5; meansevere = 25.4 ± 6.5; for CS-grouping: F(2,97)
= 37.47, p < 0.0001; mean scores per CS group: meanmild = 15.8 ± 5.5; meanmoderate = 20.5 ± 6.9;
meansevere = 29.1 ± 5.7). Additionally, when grouped by CS, the scores of patients with moderate vision
loss were also significantly higher than those of patients with mild vison loss.

Next, we examined the GAL-9 total scores against patients’ visual function metrics and number of falls in
the previous year. Figure 2 also shows the relationship between the total score on the GAL-9 and visual
acuity (Figure 2D&E) and contrast sensitivity (Figure 2C) metrics for all patients. We found significant
relationships for all three metrics with a higher score being associated with worse visual function (better
eye VA: Spearman ρ = 0.39, p < 0.0001, 95% CI: 0.22 — 0.53; worse eye VA: ρ = 0.37, p < 0.0001,

95% CI: 0.21 — 0.52; binocular CS: ρ = −0.62, p < 0.0001, 95 % CI: −0.72 — −0.49).
To determine the best set of predictors of the GAL-9 total response score, we used linear regression
modeling with age, sex, better and worse eye visual acuities, binocular contrast sensitivity, relative and
dense binocular scotoma estimates, and the number of falls in the last 12 months as the initial set of
predictors. We simplified the model using AIC prediction error estimation (R2 = 0.45, p < 0.0001).
While both relative and dense binocular scotoma estimates were identified as significant predictors, they
were highly correlated leading to elevated variance inflation factors (see VIFs in Full Model of Table 3),
only the relative scotoma estimate was included in the simplified model as this was associated with the
best AIC score. The significant predictors in the optimized model were: sex (β = 5.38, p = 0.0001),
contrast sensitivity (β = −7.58, p < 0.0001), relative binocular scotoma size (β = 0.23, p = 0.0123) and
falls (β = 1.08, p = 0.0019). Age was also included in the model but was not in itself significant (β =
−0.09, p = 0.173). Lower contrast sensitivity, female sex, larger relative scotomata, and number of falls
were linked to greater mobility-related activity difficulties (higher GAL-9 total). The model results (full
and AIC preferred) are presented in Table 3.
Figure 3C shows the frequency distribution of patients’ ratings of their difficulties for individual items on
the GAL-9. Activities relating to dark environments (Q1,2), lighting transitions (Q4,5), and uneven
surfaces (Q3) were rated as most difficult. Q3 “walking on uneven ground” and Q6 “seeing objects from
the side” were rated as the most and least difficult, respectively. The degree of difficulty reported
increased with visual impairment severity, with more than 70% of patients in the ‘severe’ group rating
most questions as ‘somewhat’ or more difficult (≥3/5, Figure 3A&B).

Referrals
A total of 31.2% of our cohort obtained referrals to mobility rehabilitation (Figure 3D). When grouped
by acuity, 21.1% of patients in the mild, 28.8% in the moderate, and 50.0% in the severe groups were
given referrals to O&M and/or physical therapy rehabilitation professionals. This breakdown when using
contrast sensitivity for severity grouping was: 15.4% in the mild, 25.9% in the moderate, and 64.3% in
the severe groups. Patients receiving referrals had significantly higher total GAL-9 scores (U=373.5,
p<0.00001; meanreferred= 28.5 ± 6.9, meannot-referred= 17.6 ± 5.6). Prior to the study, four patients in
our sample had been referred to O&M (G5, Table 2; and thus did not obtain new referrals). All patients
received referrals to occupational therapy as part of clinic’s standard practice.

Discussion
Balance, mobility, and falls are clinically important issues in central field loss
The outcomes of this study highlight the importance of low vision care professionals addressing fall risk
and mobility in individuals with central visual field loss (CFL) in the clinic, both to guide referrals and
help patients reflect on the nature of these limitations. Participants’ responses challenge the traditional
view that low vision patients with an intact peripheral field are less likely to have balance and mobility
limitations15,56,57. Over half of our sample reported balance problems, and many expressed mobility
difficulties attributed to vision loss, adding to evidence suggesting that mobility limitation and fall-risk
are major issues in CFL needing consideration in low vision care15,22,23,58.
Screening patients by asking about fall history and balance/mobility complaints is an important first step
in enhancing standard clinical practice, since patients, especially older adults, seldom spontaneously
volunteer information that feeds into the stigma of disability59 (and likely not to their eye-care
provider). A mobility-focused questionnaire, complemented by visual function screening, can also inform
appropriate referrals.
In line with prior work28,23,24,60, 41% of our sample reported at least one fall in the previous year. The
fact that only participants in the mild and moderate groups reported having 5 or more falls may reflect
reduced activity in the severe group16,25,26. This relationship warrants further investigation as fewer falls


[page 18]
may also be masking the serious issues of isolation. Indeed, this confound is why a finer screening tool
that addresses daily living tasks is needed.
Our findings suggest that AMD patients have similar mobility difficulties as those with glaucoma, as
evidenced by the reported concerns on the mobility scenarios of the GAL-9 questionnaire (except,
unsurprisingly, Q6). Total scores were also close to those reported by glaucoma patients in the
literature61. While we do not attempt to validate the GAL-9 in AMD or suggest that it be used for
screening in patients with maculopathies, our findings are in line with studies reporting comparable
mobility issues, and fall risk and incidence in individuals with central and peripheral field loss21,60,62.
The prevalence of falls (Figure 1A&B) and referral rates in the mild group (Figure 3D) suggest that the
bar for balance/mobility rehabilitation needs to be lowered for all CFL patients – particularly since
patients with better VA or monocular field loss are unlikely to be referred for low vision rehabilitation
despite clear benefits63,64.
Given the high prevalence of visual impairment and comorbidities/fall risk factors in older adults6,59,
inquiring about such issues by all clinicians is important for rehabilitation needs and recommended in
the fall-risk literature65. Early identification of balance/mobility difficulties in the vision clinic is
important as i) vision loss often progresses over time, likely compounding age-related mobility decline
and ii) these difficulties tend to be attributed to aging66,67 rather than visual impairment by both the
patient and their provider. Indeed, in our cohort, most patients did not volunteer their concerns prior to
being asked about balance and mobility.
Finally, research on fall risk and balance/motor control68–72 suggests that balance/mobility
deficits18,19,29,73,74, higher incidence of falls22–24,75, and concerns about falling26,28,76 in CFL may also
be due to a combination of visual and oculomotor deficits77–79, visual dependence80, and cognitive
decline74 in this population. Mobility rehabilitation professionals can leverage their expertise to address
these problems in CFL patients following appropriate referrals from eye-care professionals.

Role of visual function in mobility concerns
In agreement with prior work19,40,81, a key finding in our study is that contrast sensitivity is associated
more strongly and is more discriminative of balance and mobility difficulties than visual acuity. These
findings further underscore the clinical value of CS assessment for understanding functional vision in
AMD (and more generally low vision), particularly in the context of fall risk19,40,41. Indeed, a number of
studies highlight the better discriminative ability of CS measures, as opposed to VA31,46,82, and its
relation to performing activities of daily living, including balance/mobility19,38–46. Further, CS is a more
sensitive marker of AMD progression and treatment effects than VA37,83, making contrast sensitivity


[page 19]
testing especially valuable in clinical practice. In the present study, as in studies of peripheral field
loss51,84, we found that the GAL-9 total score was associated with both CS and VA. This commonality
highlights that mobility is of broad concern across low vision.

Study limitations
There are several limitations to our study. First, while we do report on patients’ balance and mobility
concerns, we do not use an AMD-specific, validated questionnaire to do so. Instead, we rely on an
instrument developed for assessment of patients with peripheral field loss, general mobility questions,
and questions relating to falls. We chose this approach for several reasons. First, unlike for peripheral
vision loss51,84,85, there is currently no CFL-specific mobility questionnaire. The more general quality of
life questionnaires validated for AMD, like the Impact of Visual Impairment86 and the Macular Disease
on Quality of Life87 or general low vision questionnaires, such as the NEI-VFQ are longer and have a
limited focus on mobility concerns, making their implementation in the clinic less practical. On the other
hand, general balance and mobility-specific questionnaires like the Activities-specific Balance Confidence
(ABC) scale88 are not focused on vision-specific limitations. Thus, we chose the GAL-9, which was shown
previously to be effective at detecting mobility concerns in glaucoma, with a good discriminative ability
of disease stage89. Our findings suggest that those with AMD have similar mobility concerns to those
with glaucoma61 on the same set of tasks.
A further limitation is the disproportionate number of female to male patients in our sample. While there
were no sex differences in terms of age and number of falls (see Methods), the regression models
revealed that women were more likely to report mobility and balance concerns. Given the older age of
our participants, the larger proportion of women in our sample is likely due to differences in lifespan (as
illustrated by the demographics of our sample’s region90). This trend is consistent with similar studies in
macular degeneration28, as well as what is observed in our clinic, where women outnumber men by a
factor of 5 regardless of pathology.
Finally, it is important to note that all participants surveyed in this study were patients in a low vision
rehabilitation clinic, having been referred there due to functional limitations. It is possible that if
surveyed from general eye clinics, the patient population profile may be different both in terms of
patient awareness of the functional deficits, as well as the deficits themselves. These potential differences
bear investigation in future research.
Finally, recall bias may be a concern when self-reporting falls history, particularly as studies indicate that
older adults tend to under-report their number of falls91–93. This is mostly due to misperceptions of what
constitutes a fall or forgetting falls that were not injurious. This issue with self-reporting falls history is


[page 20]
also why it is encouraged to complement history with questionnaires regarding specific mobility or
stepping situations, questionnaires on concerns about falling and objective balance and mobility
assessments92.

Conclusions and future directions for falls prevention in maculopathy patients
Contrast sensitivity and balance/mobility screening are key to enhancing CFL (and more broadly low
vision) care. While the GAL-9 revealed mobility difficulties of maculopathy patients in our study, our
research suggests the need for a mobility questionnaire validated specifically for CFL. Building on this
study’s and prior literature’s findings that contrast sensitivity is most closely related to mobility
limitations, other ways of mitigating fall risk could be through contrast enhancement. For example,
refraction could be optimized using contrast enhancing filters and augmented reality devices, while
indoor lighting can enhance scene contrast in different environments. Screenings like the one used in
this study can also be refined and complemented with objective balance/mobility assessments like
measuring walking speed59 which is known to be affected by CFL18,94, and is easy to implement in the
clinic. Today, fall-risk identification and mitigation are feasible and promising. The development of
enhanced screening and referral practices is a key next step in the care of individuals with central field
loss.

Highlights.
Balance and mobility difficulties are prevalent in individuals with maculopathies
Screening via self-report in the clinic can reveal balance/mobility difficulties
Inquiring about fall risk-related concerns can lead to appropriate referrals
Contrast sensitivity correlates better than visual acuity with mobility issues
