Abstract
Purpose: The Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire-48 is among the most validated tools to
collect patient-reported outcomes in a low-vision population. We have aimed to conduct a pilot validation of the Italian
version of the Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire-48.
Methods: The Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire-48 was translated using a standardized
procedure and then administered to consecutive low-vision patients attending rehabilitation services in three centers.
Patients were interviewed by a trained psychologist regarding the individual items of the tool.
Results: We included 131 patients with a mean visual acuity of 0.91 logMAR (standard deviation: 0.42 logMAR), mostly
affected by age-related macular degeneration. The Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire-48
showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.98) and good item-test and item-rest correlation (median:
0.73 and 0.71, respectively). Both the overall score and the subscale (reading, visual motor, mobility and visual
information) scores significantly correlated with visual acuity, reading acuity and speed. Reading speed achieved
the best absolute correlation with the Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire-48 scores
(Spearman r: 0.39–0.49).
Conclusion: The Italian version of the Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire-48 is a valid tool
to assess patients attending low-vision services. Revising a few items may further improve the tool.

Introduction
The aim of low vision rehabilitation (LVR) is to improve
the quality of life (QOL) of incurable visually impaired
people. The assessment of the needs and abilities of these
patients, such as the outcomes of LVR, must be patient centered and measurable. Therefore, a tool for good
patient-centered outcome (PCO) measures is necessary in
order to assess and compare the QOL of each patient during LVR.
A direct measure can be obtained during observation of
a patient attempting a specific task, usually scored on the
time required and/or on the number of errors made, but
recently many authors have preferred a patient-reported
outcome (PRO) measure to assess the ability to complete
the same task.1 Some questionnaires have been developed
in past years with this aim, in particular five instruments
studying vision-related functions in low vision patients.2–6,7
In particular, the Veterans Affairs Low-Vision Visual
Functioning Questionnaire-48 (VA LV VFQ48)8 is a
48-item questionnaire developed and validated by
Stelmack to measure the PCO of low-vision patients
undergoing LVR. The 48-item content reflects the activities that are targeted by LVR, which depend heavily on
vision, and are important to most patients.9 This instrument is able to measure LVR results10 and was selected to
be used in the “Low Vision Intervention Trial” (LOVIT
and LOVIT II).11,12
The aim of this study is to develop an Italian version of
the VA LV VFQ48 to be integrated into a web-based register of visually impaired patients undergoing LVR in many
LVR centers in Italy.

Methods
Adult patients (age 18 years or more) were recruited at
three low-vision services located in Padua, Florence, and
Rome. There was no selection based on visual acuity or
disease, since we aimed to recruit a representative sample
of patients in need of vision rehabilitation, including central or peripheral visual loss.
The primary goal was to assess the usability and internal consistency of the translated version of the VA LV
VFQ48, which was administered to all patients by trained
psychologists. Visual acuity (Early Treatment Diabetic
Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) charts), contrast sensitivity
(Pelli–Robson charts) and reading performance data
(MNREAD charts) were available for a subset of patients
when an ophthalmic examination was conducted the same
day as the psychological assessment.
The back-translation procedure was used to translate
and adapt the VA LV VFQ-48 questionnaire to the Italian
context, based on Vijver and Tanzer’s suggestions.13 Once
the back-translation was complete, the VA LV VFW-48 was
administered (the assessment phase was conducted in two
one-to-one interviews which lasted approximately 60 min

European Journal of Ophthalmology 00(0)
each), asking participants to respond and reformulate the
items, indicate unclear words, and identify possible difficulties. The Italian version of the VA LV VFQ-48 (included
in supplementary material) was used with other instruments
to assess depression (Beck Depression Inventory),14 life
satisfaction (Satisfaction with Life Scale),15 hope (The
Hope Scale),16 optimism (Life Orientation Test),17 and
courage (Courage scale).18 These instruments were used to
examine the validity of the Italian version of the VA LV
VFQ-48, which will be the subject of a separate report, and
to provide all participants with a personalized report on the
results achieved.
We used descriptive statistics and histograms to report
on the score and missing data structure of each item in the
questionnaire. The internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Item-test correlation and item-rest correlation were also calculated.
Criterion validity was studied using visual function measures as an external criterion, including visual acuity (logMAR), contrast sensitivity (log10 units), reading acuity
(logMAR), and reading speed (words/minute). For this
purpose, we computed Spearman correlation between the
overall score and each subscale domain score with visual
function measures. In order to check the robustness of our
correlation estimates using the averages of the raw questionnaire scores (1 to 5), we also extracted the person-specific latent trait using an Item Response Theory model for
ordinal responses (Graded Response Model, GRM)19 fitted
by means of the irt grm command in Stata 15.1 (Stata Corp,
College Station, TX), which was used for all our statistical
analyses. The study followed the tenets of the Declaration
of Helsinki and was approved by the Local Institutional
Review Board.

Results
Sample characteristics and questionnaire
performance
For the validation of the Italian version of the VA-VFQ-48,
we interviewed 131 patients who received the questionnaire and a battery of psychological tests and other questionnaires, with the purpose of confirming whether the
translation met an Italian setting. This work was also
based on a series of focus groups and will be the subject
of a separate report. The mean age was 69 years (standard
deviation (SD): 14 years) and 75 out of 131 patients
(54%) were female. Age-Related Macular Degeneration
(AMD) was the most common cause of visual impairment (58 subjects), followed by myopic macular degeneration (n = 27), diabetic retinopathy (n = 21), glaucoma
(n = 14), and a miscellanea of acquired and inherited diseases (n = 21). In order to confirm the validity of the
translated VFQ, using visual function and reading performance as external criteria, we computed Cronbach’s
alpha and found it very high and reliable (alpha: 0.98).

Association with visual and reading function
data
Questionnaire data could be linked with best-corrected
visual acuity (BCVA) data for 106 patients and with reading performance and contrast sensitivity data for 76
patients. There was minimal and non-significant Spearman
correlation between age and overall or subscale scores
(Spearman r between −0.02 and −0.13 in absolute values)
and no association was found with sex in regression
models.
Table 2 presents pairwise correlation (Spearman r)
between the functional variables and the overall scores of
the questionnaire, as well as the subscale scores. All coefficients were in the range of low to moderate correlation
but statistically significant, except for the lowest value
(reading acuity with mobility subscale score). Although
we did not conduct formal comparisons of the associations
between visual function measures and questionnaire
scores, we observed that the highest correlation was
reached for reading speed with the overall score, as well as
with all subscale scores.

Discussion
The VA LV VFQ48 questionnaire is the most extensively
validated tool to assess PROs in patients attending low vision services and has been used in large-scale randomized clinical trials.12
To the best of our knowledge, no other studies have
been published that report on the translation and validating
of the VA LV VFQ 48 in non-English speaking countries.
We have reported on the first translation of the VA LV
VFQ48 questionnaire, which we have adapted to the
Italian low-vision rehabilitation setting. The VA LV
VFQ48 proved to be usable with minimal adaptation, as
shown by the low rate of missing values for most items. As
we expected, we found a moderate correlation with all
visual function measures, which was higher for reading
speed.
Based on this initial assessment, we plan to develop a
refined version in which specific items are rephrased or
adapted to the Italian low-vision setting. Considering the
participants’ observations collected during the interviews,
the high frequency of omitted items is due to the following: (a) the activity is not performed for age reasons: for
example, some participants reported that they did not play
sport because they were no longer young; (b) the activity is
not performed for lack of availability: for example, some
participants reported that they did not have a garden; (c)
the activity is not performed for lack of interest:

for example, some participants reported that they had not
written checks because they did not need to; (e) the activity is generally carried out by other people: for example, a
female spouse or a support figure usually carried out
domestic cleaning; and (f) the activity is not performed
due to impairment or health problems: for example, some
participants reported that they were unable to come down
the stairs because they had other disabilities.
A limitation of this study was that we did not use
Rasch analysis for the statement check and selection and
to assess the psychometric properties of the questionnaire. Since this is a preliminary study, we used simple
methods, such as Cronbach’s alpha and correlations. We
plan to use more sophisticated analytic techniques to
validate a revised version of the Italian VA LV VFQ48
on a larger sample.
Based on the data collected in this study, we believe
that the Italian version of the VA LV VFQ48 can be used
as a structured interview in clinical practice during LVR
in order to understand individual patient’s needs regarding day-to-day activities and their perceived functional
ability. Therefore, we recommend that further studies
investigating the QOL in low-vision patients undergoing LVR in Italy use the VA LV VFQ48 as a measurement tool.
