The relationship between visual function, duration and main
causes of vision loss and falls in older people with low vision
Ecosse Lamoureux & Sandeep Gadgil &
Konrad Pesudovs & Jill Keeffe & Eva Fenwick &
Mohamed Dirani & Satu Salonen & Gwyn Rees

Abstract
Background Falls are an alarming health problem and a
major cause of injury among the elderly. The healthcare
cost associated with falls is considerable. Visual acuity has
been found to be an independent risk factor for falls;
however, the results are not unanimous. Moreover, other
aspects of visual function such as visual field, contrast
sensitivity and depth perception have not been adequately
investigated in relation to falls. The aim of this study,
therefore, was to determine the relationship between visual
function, duration, and main causes of visual impairment,
and falls in individuals with low vision.

the association between vision-related factors and falls in
older people with visual impairment.

Introduction
Falls are an important health problem, as more than a third
of community-living elderly individuals experience at least
one fall annually [1–4]. Falls are the leading cause of
injury-related deaths among elderly individuals [5, 6], and
can result in restriction of mobility and activity, feelings of
helplessness, loss of confidence, depression, and institutionalization [7]. Economically, the health care costs
associated with falls in Australia were estimated at AU
$500 million in 2001, and this figure is expected to almost
triple by 2051 due to the anticipated ageing of the Australian
population [8]. In the US, the cost of fatal and non-fatal falls
is estimated to be even higher, standing at more than USD
$19 billion (AUTHOR please correct this figure!) in 2000
[9]. Effective screening, prevention, and rehabilitation programs are required to prevent falls, as well the identification
of the risk factors for falls. Falls have a multi-factorial
etiology, and a considerable number of risk factors have been
described as either personal or environmental [10]. Several
population-based studies have identified reduced visual
acuity as one of the most common risk factors for falls
[11–13]. Also, individuals with compromised vision, compared to fully sighted populations, are almost twice as likely
to fall, have recurrent falls, and sustain fractures [14].
However, other smaller prospective studies have failed to
show any significant difference in visual acuity between
fallers and non-fallers [15–20]. Other aspects of visual
function are also not consistently related to falls. For
example, a number of studies have demonstrated that contrast sensitivity can increase the risks of falls [15, 18, 20],
while others have failed to replicate such findings [17, 21,
22]. Some studies have reported the role of depth perception
and visual field in falls [23]. These findings indicate an
equivocal role of visual function as a mechanism for falls,
and suggest that further research in this area is needed.
In this study, we investigated evidence of the relationship between vision-related factors and falls in people with
low vision. For example, we probed whether there was a
relationship between severity of visual impairment and a
higher frequency of falls. Such information is fundamental
to prioritize prevention and rehabilitation programs for
people who are at higher risk of falling within a visually impaired population. Consequently, the aim of this study
was to determine the relationship between visual function,
severity, duration and causes of visual impairment, and falls
in community-living elderly adults with low vision.

Materials and methods
All patients from the tertiary referral outpatient eye clinics
at the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital (RVEEH) were

initially approached between October 2005 and March
2006. They needed to be community-living individuals
aged 60 years or above, have low vision (visual acuity>0.3
LogMAR (6/12 Snellen) in the better eye), be mobile and
able to communicate in English or with a translator. All
consenting participants provided information regarding
demographics, general health, current medical history,
present and past ocular history, presence of common risk
factors for falls, use of medications, history of falls,
participation in daily activities [24], level of physical
activity, and education level. Ethics approval for this project
was granted by the RVEEH Human Research Ethics
Committee. This research adhered to the tenets of the
Declaration of Helsinki.
Main outcome: falls
A fall was defined as an event which resulted in a person
coming to rest unintentionally on the ground or other lower
level [16]. All patients were asked to report the number of
falls experienced during the previous month, 6 months and
year. A patient was classified as a “Faller” if he or she
reported a history of one or more falls in any of the above mentioned periods. Otherwise, the patient was classified as
a “Non-Faller”. The specific characteristics of each fall
were also recorded, namely the activity prior, location,
presence and type of any obstacle involved, injuries, and
prior warning signs [25].
Visual acuity
Visual acuity (VA) was tested individually and binocularly
with a 4-m LogMAR chart. Patients with LogMAR>1.0
(6/60 Snellen) were allocated the following LogMAR
equivalents: counting fingers (LogMAR=1.7); hand movements (LogMAR=2.0) and perception of light (LogMAR=
2.3) [26].
Contrast sensitivity
The participants’ contrast sensitivity was tested individually
and binocularly using a 1-m Pelli–Robson contrast sensitivity chart [27].
Depth perception
Binocular depth perception was assessed using the Stereo
Fly Test. Polarised vectograph plates are viewed through
polarised viewers, allowing patients with stereopsis to view a
3-D image. If a loss in gross stereopsis is present, the patient
will not be able to describe the figure of the housefly as solid
or 3-D, it will appear flat. Patients were asked to use reading
glasses if used habitually, and the polarised viewers were

worn over these. Patients were judged as having gross
stereopsis or having a loss in gross stereopsis.
Visual field
Visual field loss was determined using the Humphrey
Visual Field Analyser. The test used was the central 24-2
threshold test, using the Swedish interactive threshold
algorithm (SITA) standard strategy on the Humphrey Visual
Field Analyser (HFA) (Humphrey Zeiss Visual Field
Analyser Model 750). As the name suggests, it can locate
losses in the central 24 degrees of the visual field. Visual
field loss was measured based on the number of abnormal
stimulus locations, defined as those with statistical probability levels of p<0.05. A pattern deviation plot was used to
determine the number of affected points [28].
Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL)
The Lawton’s Instrumental Activities of Daily Living scale
[24] was also administered to ascertain the patients’ level of
functioning and independence. The scale rates the difficulty
of performing eight daily living tasks, which are: ‘using the
telephone’, ‘getting to places beyond walking distance’,
‘grocery shopping’, ‘preparing meals’, ‘doing housework or
handyman work’, ‘doing the laundry’, ‘taking medications’,
and ‘managing money’. The response categories are
‘unable to do’, ‘need some help’, and ‘need no help’, and
were coded as 0, 1 and 2 respectively. A summary score
was generated where a participant needing no help with
the eight items will produce a maximum score of 16.
Conversely, a person unable to do any of the eight items
will have a summary score of 0.
Statistical analysis
Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to characterize the participants’ sociodemographic and clinical
data. The distribution of variables such as visual acuity
(logMAR), contrast sensitivity (db), visual field (points
missed), and the IADL summary score was initially
assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov,
skewness and kurtosis test statistics, and found to be
normally distributed. Statistical analyses using the χ2
statistic for categorical data and Student’s t-test or one way ANOVA for continuous data were performed to
identify factors that differed between fallers and nonfallers. Binary logistic regression analyses for predictors
of falls were performed for variables which were significant
in the univariate analyses. Data were analyzed using the
SPSS statistical software (Version 14.0.1, SPSS Science,
Chicago, IL, USA). A p value<0.05 was used to indicate
statistical significance.

Results
One hundred and twenty-seven patients (53%; 67 males)
with a mean age of 76.3±8.3 years were recruited for the
study (Table 1). Only 39.4% of the participants (n=50)
were born in Australia. Ninety-five participants (74.8%)
were involved in regular physical activity. More than 60%
of the participants (n=75) rated their health as being
‘good’, and the most common medical conditions were
diabetes and arthritis which affected 35% (n=44) and 38%
(n=48) of the participants respectively. The mean IADL
summary score was 11.6±4.3 (range 0–16), which suggests
that overall the participants were generally functional and
independent.
The participants’ clinical characteristics and visual
functioning are shown in Table 2. The most common eye
conditions were glaucoma (33.1%, n=42) and cataract in
either eye (21.3%, n=27). The mean ± SD for best
corrected binocular visual acuity (BVCA) and contrast
sensitivity were 0.72±0.47 LogMAR (6/30) and 1.02±0.47
db respectively. Over forty percent of our participants (n=
53) had a lack of gross stereopsis, and the mean ± SD
points missed on the visual field test was 16.03±12.6.
Overall, 37% of the participants (n=47) had mild, 50% (n=
64) moderate and 13% (n=16) severe visual impairment
(VA (better eye) >0.3–0.5; >0.5–1.0; and >1.0 LogMAR
respectively).
Frequency and characteristics of falls
The frequency of recalled falls was 42.5% with 54 patients
reporting 111 falls in the last 12 months. However, the
participants could only recall the details of 71 of these falls,
suggesting a recall bias of 36%. Of these, 16 (29.6%)
reported more than one fall. Overall, 37 patients (29.1%)
reported having fallen at least once in the previous
6 months, while 18 (14.2%) reported having fallen at least
once in the last month.
Almost 65% of the falls (n=46) happened indoors (e.g.,
the bedroom, bathroom). The majority of falls (60%)
occurred at the patients’ home and surrounding environments (garden or backyard areas). Negotiating obstacles
(e.g., curbs and steps) and slippery surfaces contributed to
more 50% of all falls. ‘Walking’ was the most common
activity in which the participants were involved at the time
of the fall (62%). Only 18.3% of the falls were preceded by
any warning sign such as dizziness, palpitations, or
weakness. More than one third of the falls resulted in
bruises and grazes, 15% in cuts and almost 10% in
fractures. Approximately 40% of the patients were unable
to get up by themselves after the fall, and 34.4% sought
medical attention following a fall.

Comparison between fallers and non-fallers
In the univariate analyses, fallers compared to non-fallers
were significantly less likely to be involved in vigorous
physical activity (χ2; p<0.05) (Table 1). Overall, they also
experienced significantly greater difficulty performing activities of daily living (ANOVA; p<0.05). A significantly
greater proportion of fallers reported a history of stroke and
making use of an assistive device while walking (χ2; p<
0.05). There were no differences between fallers and nonfallers for eye conditions and the duration of vision impairment (χ2 and t-test; p>0.05, Table 2). Critically, our analyses
found no statistically significant differences for visual acuity,
depth perception, contrast sensitivity and visual field
between fallers and non fallers (ANOVA; p>0.05; Table 2).
Multivariate analysis
Although visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, depth perception, and visual field were not found to be significantly

associated with falls, each was included separately in a
regression model together with age, gender, and other
variables significantly related with falls. Non-participation
in physical activity was found to be independently significantly associated with recalled falls in three of the four
models (Table 3).
Overall, a strong relationship was observed between
falling, visual impairment and non-involvement in any form
of physical activity.

low vision attending a public tertiary eye care hospital fell
in the past 12 months. The frequency of falls reported here
is similar to or even higher (28–35%) than that in normally
sighted community-dwelling elderly individuals [1, 2, 4,
16, 19], suggesting that visually impaired people are at a
substantial risk of falls. Furthermore, almost 30% of our

participants had had more than one fall in the last
12 months. This finding is lower than that reported in a
recent prospective study of normally sighted community dwelling elderly people in which 50% of single fallers
reported multiple falls [15]. The difference is likely to be
due to reliance on memory recall within retrospective
studies, resulting in an underestimate of falls [29].
Only non-participation in physical activity was found to
be independently and significantly linked with falls. Our
finding is not unusual, as several studies using other
population samples have also shown in activity to be a
consistent risk factor for falls and moving from being
sedentary to moderately active can reduce the risks of falls
and hip fractures by 20–40% [16, 30, 31]. Physical activity
reflects aspects of mobility, dynamic stability and musculoskeletal system function. Previous investigations that have
shown measures of muscle weakness, such as the inability to
get up from a chair without using the hands, to predict falls
[17, 19]. Given that these factors are readily modifiable with
appropriate intervention strategies, further work is required
to examine these elements and their associations with falls
within the visually impaired population.
The four aspects of visual function were not found to be
associated with falls. Several population-based studies have
found visual acuity (<6/12) to be significantly associated
with falls [13, 21, 32], including injurious falls [32, 33].
Whilst visual impairment (absence or presence) may be an
independent predictor of falls in a heterogeneous population, our results indicate that the degree or level of visual
acuity has limited association with falls in this specific
population. It has been argued that visual acuity plays
a more significant role as a falls risk factor when the
participants are dependent [33]. It should be noted that our
sample were mobile and independent community-dwelling
individuals, and therefore the role of visual acuity as an
independent predictor of falls may be less important.
Furthermore, over 90% of our participants had mild or
moderate vision loss, and it is possible that a floor effect in
the range of visual impairment may help to explain our lack
of association between level of visual acuity and falls.
Future studies, using a larger sample size and an even
representation of visual impairment, are needed to confirm
these hypotheses and to determine more precisely the level
of visual acuity at which the risk of falls increases.
That visual field loss was not found to be associated with
falls is perhaps also not surprising, as there is a lack of
consensus on this issue. Several studies have shown an
increased risk of falls in patients with compromised visual
field [13, 34, 35], while others have shown no association
[22, 36]. Similarly, some studies have found contrast
sensitivity to predict falls [37], while others have found
no significant association [21, 22]. Equally, the association
of depth perception with falls has been ambiguous, as some

studies have demonstrated a link [15], while others have
found none [22, 35]. Our finding that there is no association
between falls and eye conditions is also not unprecedented,
as there has been conflicting evidence on the role of eye
diseases as potential risk factors for falls [11, 12, 32, 38].
In conclusion, this study found that more than two out of
five individuals with low vision fall annually, suggesting
that a substantial proportion of this specific population is
vulnerable to falling and its debilitating impact. Four
aspects of visual function were not found to be associated
with falling, suggesting that once the visual system
deteriorates, other factors such as participation in physical
activity may be more significant. Such information is
valuable in the planning of prevention and rehabilitation
programs for people who are at higher risk of falling within
a visually-impaired population, and could be the focus of
larger studies in the future. Due to the cross-sectional nature
of this study and the relatively small sample size, further
investigation is required to determine whether a fear of
falling or a previous fall limits participation in physical
activity and, additionally, that the non-involvement in
physical activity increases the risk of falling. Future
prospective research involving a larger sample from diverse
settings and including formal assessment of functional gait,
mobility, balance, and muscular strength is also required to
identify the independent predictors of falls in individuals
with low vision.
