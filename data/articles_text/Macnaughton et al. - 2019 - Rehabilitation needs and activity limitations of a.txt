Abstract
Purpose: To evaluate outcome measures of the Participation and Activity Inventory (PAI) in a sample of adults with acquired visual impairment entering vision
rehabilitation. Both Priority Scores, indicating level of rehabilitative need, and
Person Measures, indicating goal difficulty, were considered.
Methods: Participants were newly registered adults with visual impairment
within Leicestershire, United Kingdom. The importance and difficulty of 48 goals
of the PAI were assessed, as were demographic factors, clinical visual function
(visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, reading function) and psychosocial function
(adjustment to visual loss, depression, anxiety and fear of falling). Priority scores
were calculated as the product of importance and difficulty of each goal. All
questionnaires were Rasch analysed, and person and item measures of perceived
difficulty with goals were derived.
Results: Sixty people (mean age  S.D. = 75.8  13.8 years) took part. PAI
goals with greatest rehabilitative need were reading (6.82  2.91), mobility outdoors (6.55  3.92), mobility indoors within an unfamiliar environment
(5.52  3.93) and writing (5.27  3.02). Greater rehabilitative need was associated with younger age (b = 0.46, p < 0.001), and with higher depressive symptomatology (b = 0.35, p < 0.01; model R2 34%). Goals with greatest difficulty
were mending clothing ( 1.95  0.35 logits) and hobbies and crafts
( 1.32  0.23 logits). Greater difficulty was associated with higher depressive
symptomatology (b = 0.39, p < 0.001), lower visual acuity (b = 0.42, p < 0.001)
and lower adjustment of visual loss (b = 0.31, p < 0.01; model R2 53%).
Conclusions: Key rehabilitation needs for adults at entry to services require both
optical and non-optical interventions. As rehabilitative need was not associated
with the level of visual impairment, eyecare professionals should not wait until
the end of medical treatment before referral for support. Similarly, rehabilitative
need was associated with younger age, indicating the importance to refer younger
people with sight loss at an early stage. The use of structured assessment, such as
the PAI, ensures goals that have an impact upon quality of life are specifically
identified. Depression screening on entry to rehabilitation is relevant as it predicts
both perceived difficulty and rehabilitative need.

Introduction
The practical implications that acquired vision loss have
upon the performance of daily activities will vary between

individuals. Furthermore, the disabling effect resulting
from the inability to read, or the loss of independent
mobility following the withdrawal of a driving licence, may
lead to additional emotional consequences. Therefore,

being able to assess the impact of vision loss and understand the key factors that may influence rehabilitation
needs for each individual are fundamental at entry to vision
rehabilitation services.1–4
It is well understood that the number of individuals with
visual impartment will rise as a consequence of demographic ageing,5,6 increasing the pressure upon service providers to deliver timely and effective rehabilitation services.
The Care Act 2014 requires that all UK local authorities
(LAs) support the emotional well-being and personal independence of people in need of assistance and support, in
order to reduce the long-term burden on health and social
care.7 Historically, the delivery of rehabilitation services to
adults with sight loss in the UK has been described as fragmented,8 which subsequently led to a call for a multidisciplinary approach to service delivery.9–11 The response to
change has been slow, and although there has been a shift
towards multidisciplinary service provision, there is no
standard model of delivery.12 A national survey of LAs and
voluntary (not-for-profit) organisations providing vision
rehabilitation services, commissioned by the Thomas Pocklington Trust, reviewed 87 providers across England (57%
response rate).13 Over a 2-year period between 2012 and
2014, the survey found that 61% of services were delivered
by the LAs and a further 28% from the voluntary sector.
Although the importance of rehabilitation interventions
has been highlighted, there is little evidence on the priority
needs of people with a visual impairment entering rehabilitation services in England that could assist in the planning
of an effective, patient-centred approach to service delivery.
Empowering patients to be actively involved in the management of their own health and care is a move towards a
person-centred National Health Service (NHS).14 In the
context of visual impairment services, rehabilitation aims
to minimise the disabling effects of sight loss and improve
quality of life. Arguably, these are aspects that only people
with a visual impairment themselves can assess. In recent
years the development and use of patient-reported outcome
measures (PROMs) has expanded to include the assessment
of vision-related activity limitations.15 Despite this, PROMs
are not routinely used by service providers in the UK
despite evidence to suggest that more personalised commissioning of support could potentially enhance clinical care
and reduce costs.16
In comparison, multidisciplinary service providers in the
Netherlands are using PROMs routinely as a means of
identifying personalised rehabilitation needs at service
intake. The Participation and Activity Inventory (PAI),17
formerly known as the Dutch ICF Activity Inventory,18,19 is
a validated instrument used to investigate the priority rehabilitation needs of adults with a visual impairment in a systematic way. The PAI builds upon the hierarchal goals and
task structure of the Activity Inventory (AI)20 and
114

combines it within the nine Activity and Participation
domains of the World Health Organisation’s (WHO) International Classification of Functioning, Disability and
Health (ICF),21 together with an additional domain, of
‘Emotional Health.’ By using a systematic approach, the
PAI has been shown to more comprehensively identify the
needs of people with visual impairment by preventing personal goals from being overlooked.22 This study compared
two methods of assessment of priority needs, semi-structured and structured, and found that only 22% of the rehabilitation needs identified by the PAI were present in the
‘usual’ semi-structured intake records. Consequently, the
PAI is now used by many Multidisciplinary Rehabilitation
Centres across the Netherlands to set individual rehabilitation needs and to evaluate rehabilitation outcomes.
The PAI was developed and validated using Classical Test
Theory.23 To determine self-reported priority rehabilitation
needs, Likert scales are used to create ordinal priority scores
for individual goals of activities of daily living and social participation as a product of importance and difficulty of goals.
The original conceptual framework of the PAI is based upon
the assumption that goals with both high importance and
high difficulty together indicate a higher rehabilitative
need.20 However, the use of Likert scale categories assumes
that all goals are of equal difficulty and contribute the same
amount to the measurement of the underlying construct.24,25
The PAI has subsequently been further validated using Rasch
analysis.26,27 The Rasch model converts raw ordinal scores
into an interval scale and creates person measure and item
measure estimates for the underlying construct.28 A person
measure expresses the individual’s perceived difficulty across
all of the goals of the PAI, whereas an item measure is a
measure of the overall difficulty of each item or goal.
The purpose of this study was to evaluate the use of the
PAI in a sample of adults with an acquired visual impairment entering vision rehabilitation in England: to compare
both the Priority Scores (PAI-PS) calculated from the raw
ordinal score data indicating participants’ priority rehabilitation needs and the Rasch Person Measures (PAI-PM)
measuring participants’ difficulty level with goals or activities of daily living and social participation. This enables
comment on both the priority rehabilitation needs, and
those activities found to be the most difficult facing people
with a visual impairment in order to guide service commissioning decisions.

Near visual function was assessed with MNREAD Acuity

speed of the largest 10 print sizes on the MNREAD chart

adults with normal vision (200 wpm).44
A number of PROMs were administered to assess participants’ psychosocial function. Adjustment to vision loss
was assessed with the Acceptance and Self-Worth Adjustment Scale (AS-WAS)45 which evaluates aspects of adjustment to visual impairment associated with self-esteem,
locus of control, self-efficacy and acceptance. Nineteen
items are assessed on a 4-category Likert scale. Anxiety and
depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Hospital
Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).46 The instrument
presents two 7-item alternating subscales of anxiety and
depression (HADS-A and HADS-D) assessed on a 4-category Likert scale. Ordinal scores across each subscale range
from 0 to 21 with categorical grouping by score
(0–7 = normal; 8–10 = borderline abnormal; and 11–
21 = abnormal).47 Fear of falling was assessed with the
Short Falls Efficacy Scale-International (Short FES-I).48
The instrument presents seven items assessed on a 4-category Likert scale.
The outcome measure for the study was the PAI. Participants were asked to rate the importance of the PAI’s 48
goals on a 4-category Likert scale from 0 to 3 (not important or not applicable, slightly, moderately, or very important).23 If a goal was considered important (score ≥ 1) then
participants were asked to consider the difficulty of that
goal on a 5-category Likert scale from 0 to 4 (not, slightly,
moderately, very difficult or impossible) with any aids used
where relevant (such as a low vision device for reading),
but without assistance from anyone else.
Analysis
Participation and Activity Inventory priority scores were
calculated as the product of the importance and difficulty
for each goal (minimum 0, maximum 12). Priority scores
116

for both individual participants (PAI-PS) and individual
goals were computed.
Rasch analysis was undertaken using a single Andrich
rating scale model49 using Winsteps software version 4.0.1
(www.winsteps.com) to create person and item measures
from the difficulty scores of PAI goals, and also for the
responses to the other Likert-scale instruments (AS-WAS,
HADS, Short FES-I). Person and item measures are
described in terms of logits (or log odds units), which represent the likelihood of a person having the ability to
achieve an item (or an item being achievable for a person).
The average logit value for both persons and items was set
to 0 for each set of analyses on each instrument. For each
instrument, a higher person measure indicates that the
individual perceives that they have greater difficulty with
the items. A higher item measure indicates that an individual perceives the item as easier to achieve.
Where categories were underutilised or disordered, they
were repaired by collapsing adjacent response categories
until rescaling produced utilisation of all categories in an
ordered structure.50 Infit and Outfit mean-square (MNSQ)
values lower than 2.0 were considered acceptable,51 given
that the purpose of this study was to create person and item
measures reflecting difficulty levels across a wide range of
rehabilitation needs and levels of difficulty,52 and only
items with MNSQ > 2.0 have the potential to damage the
integrity of the scale. MNSQ values >2 were removed iteratively, with the most misfitting removed first and the analysis repeated until all item fits fell below 2.0.
Principal components analysis (PCA) assesses the extent
to which an instrument measures a single latent trait or
ability, or its unidimensionality. Reasonable overall unidimensionality is indicated where at least 60% of the variance
is explained by the primary measure.53 Also, if the unexplained variance within the first contrast found within the
residuals is less than two items (an eigenvalue of <2.0) or is
significantly lower than the raw variance explained by the
items in the principal Rasch measure, then unidimensionality is also indicated.
A person separation value of >2.0 and reliability of >0.8
was considered to indicate an ability to reliably discriminate between participants, and an item separation of >3.0
and reliability of >0.9 to indicate that items are reliably
ordered in terms of difficulty28,50 Ideal targeting was considered to exist when the mean of person measures is
within 1.0 logits of the mean of item measures.25

of any significant relationships between each predictor variable and the two outcome measures of the PAI (PAI-PS,
PAI-PM). Mann–Whitney U tests were used in the analysis
of dichotomous predictor variables and Kruskal–Wallis
tests in the analysis of categorical data. Two-tailed Spearman’s rho bivariate analysis was used in comparison with

Results
Sixty participants (22 male, 38 female) took part in the
study, with a mean age of 75.8 years (range 30–97;
Table 1). Ninety-seven per cent were White British, 80%
were retired, 53% lived with someone, and 47% lived alone.
Thirty-two per cent had a previous history of anxiety or
depression, and there was a mean of 2.6 comorbidities
(range 0–8). The primary self-reported cause of vision loss
was macular degeneration (50%) followed by glaucoma
(12%), diabetic retinopathy (5%), and hereditary retinal
disorder (5%).
The Reading Accessibility Index (ACC) was established
for 55 participants; two participants were unable to read
aloud following brain injury associated with their visual
impairment, and a further three were found to have corrupt audio files. Contrast sensitivity measure was established for 59 participants. It was not possible to record one
participant; due to ultra-low visual acuity, the participant
was unable view the chart.

Comparison of priority scores and person measures
The Rasch person and item measures focus purely on the
level of difficulty expressed by people who found each goal
of at least some importance, and does not take the relevance of each goal into account as the priority scores do. A
comparison of priority scores and person measures is
shown in Figure 2. In general, those with higher priority
scores (greater rehabilitative need) have higher person measures (expressing greater difficulty; r = 0.80, p < 0.001).
There were two participants that contradicted this general
relationship, where perceived goal difficulty is high but
expressed rehabilitative need is low. Both participants died
within 1 year of data collection, suggesting that poor health
could have overridden the importance of visual rehabilitative interventions for these individuals. Whilst the relationship between priority scores and item difficulty is not
perfect (r < 1), indicating that the inclusion of importance
in priority scores has some influence beyond simple difficulty, no significant relationship was found between the

The demographic of the sample was considered representative of those living within the counties of Leicestershire
and Rutland. Across the region of Leicester (city), Leicestershire and Rutland, approximately 550 new registrations
occur per year. This study’s sample of 97% White British is
representative of Leicestershire County54 where the majority of our participants resided. The range of conditions
causing primary loss of vision within the sample was also
considered representative of adults registered in the UK
with sight loss, comparing well with most current published
statistics of visual impairment in the UK.6
Priority scores for PAI goals (Table 3) found key areas
for rehabilitation intervention are reading, mobility, and
writing, suggesting that early assessment for both the provision of low vision devices and training in orientation and
mobility techniques are indicated at the point of referral for
rehabilitation interventions following acquired vision loss.

That reading, writing and mobility are key rehabilitation
needs is supported by studies with older participants in the
UK and Netherlands.18,23,55,56 However, where Dutch rehabilitation services are delivered on these three specific
goals,57 writing skills as a specific rehabilitation need has
perhaps been less well recognised as a priority need in the
UK, with no specific reference to interventions concentrating on writing as an individual goal mentioned in recent
reviews of UK service provision.3,13,58,59 The goal of writing
is frequently coupled together with reading in the literature
however while handwriting is a near vision task, the ability
of a person with visual impairment to use a pen includes
the need for additional strategies including the use of alternative devices or technology to accomplish this task successfully. Therefore, separate attention to this goal is
implied.

that only a small minority of people actually receive a comprehensive programme of orientation and mobility

Regression analysis of the study sample demonstrated
that the most significant predictor in the need for rehabilitation interventions was age, with younger age reflecting
greater need. In this sample, goals relating to employment
or education scored low in priority, reflecting the fact that
most of this study’s population were retired. In comparison, a study using the PAI to determine priority rehabilitation needs in a younger age group (age 18–25; n = 392)
demonstrated priority rehabilitation needs in areas relating
to independent domestic life, education, employment and
independent travel, supporting previous evidence that these
are major themes in the transition to adult life.17 Furthermore, younger people have been shown to demonstrate a
higher number of needs, including more intensive rehabilitation programmes relating to study, and employment, that
are not as relevant to older people. One limitation of the
present study therefore, is that it can only be considered as
generalisable to an older population.
Following regression analysis, the number of participant
co-morbidities was not found to be a significant factor
influencing rehabilitation need; in addition, there was also
no significant relationship found in our sample between
participant age and number of co-morbidities (p = 0.17).
Therefore, poor overall health did not appear to contribute
to the decline in rehabilitation need with age in this sample.
The inevitable lifestyle changes and a gradual reduction in
122

daily activity patterns with older people60,61 may better
explain lower rehabilitation needs with advancing age.
In the Netherlands, the two goals of the PAI that address
the emotional health domain were also identified as high
priority needs. However, these goals (001, 002) were not
included in the ‘Top 15’ priority goals in the present sample
(Table 3), despite one-third of the UK sample self-reporting previous history of either anxiety or depression. While
there is a growing body of evidence to support a high
prevalence of depression in individuals with a visual
impairment,62,63 there appears little evidence on how
patients report symptoms of depression or anxiety to
healthcare professionals. In a London-based study, older
people were found to regard depression as a ‘sign of weakness’; cultural variations were also thought to influence the
help-seeking behaviour of elders from minority ethnic
groups.64 While such evidence suggests an overall underreporting of emotional health, there is no current evidence to
offer a reason as to why there was a difference in attitude
towards reporting depression and anxiety between the British and the Dutch samples. Our UK sample may not be as
comfortable with reporting depressive symptoms with eyecare professionals or may not recognise that they are suffering from depressive symptoms compared to the Dutch
sample. That a higher level of depressive symptoms is a key
factor in regression models for both greater rehabilitations
needs and higher levels of difficulty with PAI goals, suggests
that addressing depression early in rehabilitation assessment may reduce both the need and level of input of service
delivery for that individual. This supports well-established
evidence that depression is a significant factor associated
with self-reported activity limitations.65–69 And although
levels of anxiety were not found to be a significant factor in
our regression models, we conclude that this may be a
reflection on the poor psychometric performance of the
HADS-A subscale. The HADS-A was initially chosen as it is
one of the most widely used screening instruments in psychosocial research and practice. However, the HADS was
developed as a screening measure for psychological distress
with the aim of identifying clinically significant severe
depression or anxiety. The poor targeting value (1.43) indicates that this group had lower levels of anxiety than the
test was designed for. Therefore, the HADS may be less useful for the identification of patients with moderate to mild
levels of distress that existed within our study population.
Further investigation with a more robust measure is indicated to determine whether anxiety is a significant contributory factor.
The need for rehabilitation was not found to be associated with the level of vision loss, suggesting that those with
even mild or early vision loss may benefit from referral for
support and intervention. This supports conclusions from
a previous qualitative study investigating the time delay in

referring people with a visual impairment for UK visual
rehabilitation services. The study found that ophthalmologists did not consider referring their patients for social care
needs until all possible clinical interventions had been
exhausted.70 The same study also reported that 43% of the
participants (n = 20) interviewed, stated that they would
have liked to have been offered support sooner, suggesting
that individuals felt they would benefit from earlier interventions. One of the limitations of the present study’s sample was that 59 of the 60 participants were newly registered
as either sight impaired (SI) or severely sight impaired
(SSI). A wider study of rehabilitation needs to include those
individuals living with early or lower levels of sight loss
may give a more accurate representation of rehabilitation
needs across all groups of individuals living with sight loss.
When specifically considering the difficulty of PAI goals
at entry into rehabilitation through the use of person measures, the goals of highest difficulty included mending
clothing, undertaking hobbies, driving and DIY (Table 5).
While these goals were considered most difficult, they were
not relevant to all individuals within the study sample. For
example, the goal of mending clothes was relevant to only
23% (n = 14) of participants whereas the goal of reading
was relevant to 100% (n = 60) of the sample. Goals with
low relevance may reflect either that they are not common
activities, or, as they are the most difficult to perform, that
they have already been relinquished. However, they may be
of key importance for those to whom they are relevant. The
goal of driving also showed low relevance (5%, n = 3), with
most subjects scoring the goal as ‘not applicable.’ The low
relevance figure is likely to reflect that in the UK, vision
that is impaired to the point of visual impairment registration is incompatible with the visual requirements for driving, as per the UK Driving Eyesight Rules (https://
www.gov.uk/driving-eyesight-rules). However, the subsequent loss of independence from losing a driving licence
has been reported as being overwhelming, with implications on the individual’s quality of life and emotional
health.71 Loss of other high-valued activities such as sewing,
hobbies or DIY (do-it-yourself home improvements), may
also cause initial frustration or distress and be associated
with difficulty adjusting to vision loss, and subthreshold or
major depression.65,69,72
Therefore, establishing the impact vision loss has had
upon an individual’s high-valued activities at an early stage
in vision loss, with the purpose of managing expectations
or assisting with compensatory strategies, may assist in the
adjustment process. Previous studies have associated lower
levels of depression with better levels of adjustment to
vision loss.2,4,66,73–75 Thus the benefit of using a structured
approach not only determines priority rehabilitation needs
but may be used to specifically identify goals with high
levels of difficulty. Although the assumption from the

Rehabilitation needs and activity limitations

conceptual framework of the original AI, that higher need
for rehabilitation is a product of higher importance and
levels of higher difficulty, it is possible that even when a
goal is important, an individual may not wish to seek rehabilitation for that goal. Therefore, it is still important that
individuals are still asked whether or not they wish to proceed with interventions. However, addressing such activities may have a significant impact upon quality of life, but
would otherwise be missed in a case history or semi-structured approach.
Finally, goals relating to activities of reading, writing or
mobility were both high rehabilitation needs and perceived
as most difficult to achieve. Comparing the regression
models of each outcome measure suggests that it is not possible to predict rehabilitation needs as well as goal difficulties (34% vs 52% variance explained by predictor variables,
respectively). Understandably, what is ‘important’ to an
individual is very individual, whereas what is difficult may
be realistically predicted by visual function.
The associations outlined above are only true for one
time point. For example, as the data is cross-sectional, it is
not possible to conclude whether the depressive symptoms
are the result of highly valued goal being very difficult or
impossible to achieve, or whether greater difficulty is
expressed as a result of the individual suffering from
depressive symptoms. Similarly, it is not possible to conclude whether higher levels of depressive symptoms are the
result of increased rehabilitation needs or that depressive
symptoms result in an increased need for rehabilitation. To
be able to determine whether the relationship between variables are either a cause or a consequence of the outcome
measures of the PAI, analysis would require repeating at
different time points.
Conclusions
Low vision rehabilitation services should offer early interventions on reading, mobility and writing to all newly registered adult service users. Providing such services may
involve more than one service provider. As the need for
rehabilitation is not associated with the level of vision loss,
prompt referral for support is indicated as those with early
or mild vision loss may already have rehabilitation needs.
Eyecare professionals should not wait until the end of medical treatment programs before considering a referral for
support and intervention. Higher rehabilitation need is also
associated with younger age, indicating it is particularly
important to refer younger people with sight loss for rehabilitation at an early stage.
Service users with higher levels of depression or depressive symptoms have greater rehabilitative needs and perceive greater difficulty with visual tasks, therefore an
evaluation of their level of depressive symptoms at an early

Rehabilitation needs identified in areas more straightforward or cost effective to provide may currently overshadow

with hobbies or crafts. Addressing these goals may have
important impact upon quality of life and levels of depressive symptoms and the use of a structured assessment such
