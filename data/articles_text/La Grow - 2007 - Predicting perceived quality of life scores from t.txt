ORIGINAL ARTICLE

Predicting Perceived Quality of Life Scores
From the National Eye Institute 25-Item Visual
Function Questionnaire
STEVEN J. LA GROW, EdD
School of Health Sciences, Massey University, Palmerston North, New Zealand

ABSTRACT
Purpose. This study set out to determine the degree to which the composite score of the National Eye Institute 25-item
Visual Function Questionnaire (VFQ-25) predicted perceived quality of life (PQoL) as measured by a single-item global
measure of quality of life (QoL) and therefore, the extent to which the two were measuring the same construct.
Methods. Hierarchical multiple regression was used to determine the ability of VFQ-25 to predict PQoL, while controlling
for other variables significantly correlated with PQoL. The latter were entered as independent variables in the first step
of this analysis and VFQ-25 in the second. This model determines the degree to which VFQ-25 predicted PQoL when all
other variables were controlled for.
Results. Of the other variables assessed, only general health and group affiliation were found to be significantly correlated with
PQoL. These variables, entered in step 1 of the analysis, explained 24.3% of the variance (adjusted r2 ⫽ 0.243) in PQoL. When
VFQ-25 was entered in step 2, the model then explained 29.3% (adjusted r2 ⫽ 29.3) of the variance or an additional 5% when
the effects of the other two variables were controlled for (r2 change ⫽ 0.050, p ⫽ 0.001). In other words, VFQ-25 only predicted
5% of the variance found in PQoL. Therefore, 95% of the variance was not explained by this measure.
Conclusions. It is clear from the results of this study that VFQ and PQoL were not measuring the same construct. It is also
apparent that the single-item global measure of QoL did indeed ask participants to rate their PQoL. Thus, we may
conclude that the measure of vision-related QoL used in this study did not measure PQoL. Further research is required
to determine the extent to which this finding may apply to other measures of vision-related QoL.
(Optom Vis Sci 2007;84:785–788)
Key Words: vision-related quality of life, multi-item measure of quality of life, single-item global measure of quality of
life, perceived quality of life, quality of life, visual impairment, NEI-VFQ-25

T

he use of quality of life (QoL) as an outcome measure has
become increasingly common in vision-related research.1
Although a number of general and health-related QoL measures are in use, there has been a sharp rise in the number and use
of condition-specific, vision-related QoL measures appearing in
the literature in the last several years. For example, a recent systematic review identified 31 vision-related QoL measures of which 24
were first published in the last 10 years.2 Despite the fact that QoL
is fast becoming the outcome measure of choice in this area,3 and
most other areas of health and disability research,4 there is little
agreement on what constitutes QoL.3,4 However, there is considerable agreement that it is a multidimensional construct, which
may include functional ability, degree and quality of social and
community interaction, psychological well-being, somatic sensa-

tion (pain) and satisfaction with life.3,4 General QoL measures
tend to include items from all these domains, whereas condition specific QoL instruments usually do not.2 This practice is reflected
in the existing vision-related QoL measures, where 6 of the 31
measures identified included 4 of the 5 domains listed above, 5
included 3, 13 included 2, and 7 only 1. As a result of the diversity
in the number and range of domains measured, it would be difficult to say that all are measuring the same construct under the
heading QoL. Furthermore, none appeared to have included satisfaction with life as a dimension to be measured.2 As such, it may
also be difficult to say that any of the vision-related QoL measures
currently available are directly measuring the construct which most
individuals are likely to perceive as constituting QoL when asked to
rate their own4 (i.e., how satisfied am I with my life at this time?).

Yet, the majority of vision-related QoL measures were developed
from the premise that improved perception of QoL by clients or
patients may be the most meaningful outcome for low vision or
vision rehabilitation services.1,3 However, rather than measuring
perception of QoL directly, most tend to measure those things that
are expected to impact QoL,5 like function (i.e., 28 of the 31
include a functional domain and 6 measure function only). Although these instruments may provide valuable information on the
impact of visual impairment on myriad factors affecting one’s daily
life, they probably are not measuring the impact of visual impairment and/or vision-related services on perceived QoL (PQoL).1
The purpose of this paper is to determine the degree to which
scores from a vision-related QoL measure are related to scores from
a single-item global measure of PQoL to test this premise. Global
measures of QoL examine the whole of life and appear to measure
a different construct than that measured by condition- or disease specific QoL measures.4,6 This may be especially true with a single item global measure of QoL (i.e., in the past 6 months, I would say
my QoL has been: Excellent, very good, good, fair, poor) where the
participants are asked to determine the meaning of the construct of
QoL for themselves as they respond to the item. Single-item global
measures of general health,7 oral health,8 depression,9 physical
activity,10 stress11 and QoL12–14 have been shown to be reliable,
responsive, and highly correlated with multiple-item measures of
the same construct.

METHODS
The present study uses multiple regression to analyze pretest
data from both groups of an experimental/control group study
designed to investigate the effectiveness of comprehensive low vision services with 186 older visually impaired people. The selection
process, procedures and results of that study have been reported.15
The research followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and
informed consent was obtained from all participants. Ethical approval was granted by the Wellington Ethics Committee for that
study and follow-up analyses. Three primary dependent measures
were used, including a measure of instrumental activities of daily
living, the National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire-25
(NEI-VFQ-25)16 and a single-item global measure of QoL. The
latter two are used as the independent and dependent variables
respectively for the analysis presented here.
The NEI-VFQ-25 consists of 25 questions representing 11
vision-related constructs plus an additional single-item rating
of general health.16 It is available in the public domain, is
normed, has demonstrated reliability and validity17,18 and was
specifically designed to assess health-related QoL of persons
with visual impairment.5 The NEI-VFQ-25 is represented by a
single composite score obtained by averaging scores from all 11
vision-related subscales. Calculation of the composite score specifically excludes the scores obtained from the single-item rating of general health.16 This composite score (VFQ-25) is used
to represent vision-related QoL in this study. A single-item
global measure of QoL consisting of the statement, “In the past
6 months, I would say my overall QoL has been excellent, very
good, good, fair, or poor” was also used. The participants indicated the response that fit best. This measure is used to represent PQoL in this study. Responses from both measures were

transformed so that positive responses had higher values. The
response categories were weighted as 1 ⫽ 0, 2 ⫽ 25, 3 ⫽ 50,
4 ⫽ 75, 5 ⫽ 100.
Hierarchical multiple regression was used to determine the
ability of the composite score of a vision-related QoL measure
(VFQ-25) to predict PQoL while controlling for any other
variables significantly correlated with PQoL. The relationship
between the single-item rating of general health and PQoL was
also assessed, as general health is excluded from the composite
score of the NEI-VFQ-25 and is known to be correlated with
PQoL.4 The relationships between age, gender, visual acuity,
and group affiliation (control/experimental) and PQoL were
assessed as well. Group affiliation was included in this analysis
as it had already been seen to result in significantly different
PQoL scores in this sample at pretest.15 Any variable found to
be significantly correlated with PQoL was entered as an independent variable in the first step of this analysis (model 1).19
The composite score for the NEI-VFQ-25 was entered as an
independent variable in the second step (model 2). The model
as a whole (model 2) was analyzed to determine the degree to
which VFQ-25 predicted PQoL when all other variables significantly related to PQoL were controlled.
Data were analyzed using a total of 184 of the original 186 cases,
as two had missing data for at least one of the two primary variables
of interest. The 184 cases represented 56 males and 128 females;
their mean age was 80.5 and ranged from 65 to 96. Ninety-six
percent identified (n ⫽ 177) themselves as New Zealanders of
European origin. Sixty-eight percent (n ⫽ 125) said they had
health conditions other than visual impairment, which affected
their daily life. Nearly half (n ⫽ 90) reported living alone, while
43% (n ⫽ 80) said they lived with a spouse or other family member
and 8% (n ⫽ 14) said they lived with others in a hostel, nursing
home, or residential care facility. The majority of participants (n ⫽
107) had a distance visual acuity in the better eye while wearing
correction of between 20/100 and 20/200, whereas 26% (n ⫽ 47)
had an acuity rating worse than 20/200, and the remaining 16%
(n ⫽ 30) had an acuity rating between 20/40 and 20/80.

RESULTS
Mean PQoL was 52.99 (SD ⫽ 27.12) for the entire sample.
Mean VFQ-25 was 51.60 (SD ⫽ 13.84), and the mean score for
general health was 52.45 (SD ⫽ 25.62). As can be seen in Table 1,
both VFQ-25 and the score for general health were significantly
and positively correlated with PQoL (p ⬍ 0.0005), with the
strength of the relationship being moderate for general health (r ⫽
0.442) and weak for VFQ-25 (r ⫽ 0.276). Group affiliation was
the only other variable of interest, which was found to be significantly correlated with PQoL (r ⫽ 0.253, p ⫽ 0.001). The strength
of the relationship was weak. The correlations between age (r ⫽
⫺0.110, p ⫽ 0.137), gender (r ⫽ ⫺0.031, p ⫽ 0.672), visual
acuity (r ⫽ ⫺0.007, p ⫽ 0.928), and PQoL did not reach significance. Therefore, general health and group affiliation were the
only independent variables entered into step 1 of the analysis. The
assumptions underlying multiple regression were checked and
none were found to be violated.

DISCUSSION
Of all the variables assessed (i.e., age, gender, visual acuity,
group affiliation, general health, VFQ-25), only general health,
group affiliation, and VFQ-25 were found to be significantly correlated with PQoL. When general health and group affiliation were
entered into step 1 (model 1) of the equation for hierarchal regression, it was found that, together, they explained 24.3% of variance
in PQoL. When these two variables were controlled for in model 2,
it was found that VFQ-25 significantly contributed to the prediction of PQoL. However, that additional contribution was just 5%.
Therefore, it is clear from this analysis that the composite score
of the NEI-VFQ-25 and the single-item measure used to assess
PQoL were not measuring the same construct. It is also clear from
examining the question asked that the single-item global measure
of QoL was asking the participants to rate their PQoL. However,
whether this is an adequate measure of quality life rests on its face
validity. In other words, the reader must decide if asking the participants to “rate their QoL in the last 6 months as excellent, very

good, good, fair or poor” should result in a response that would
adequately represent their perception of QoL in terms of life satisfaction. And if so, if a response to this question would represent a
multifaceted perception of one’s QoL, likely to include various
domains of life important to those responding.
Life satisfaction has been defined as a global assessment of a
person’s QoL according to one’s own criteria.20 Judgments of life
satisfaction are based on a comparison with a standard which individuals set for themselves; it is not externally imposed.21 Clearly,
this is different from health-related QoL, of which vision-related
QoL measures are a subset. In health-related QoL, a range of
domains are assessed, which are judged to affect QoL, and scores
are compiled over those domains to obtain an overall measure of
QoL.21 As such, we would not expect one measure to perfectly
predict the other as they are not identical measures of the same
construct. However, a reasonable amount of prediction from one
measure to the other (e.g., 30% to 40% or more) would be expected if they were indeed assessing the same general construct
(i.e., QoL). The finding that VFQ only predicted 5% of the variance in PQoL in this study is far below that expectation.
This is somewhat surprising as the VFQ-25 has received reasonably high ratings for content validity, has a relatively positive rating
for construct validity, and was rated as one of the best vision related QoL measures, in terms of its psychometric properties, for
use with visually impaired persons in general.2 The VFQ-25 clearly
addresses most of the domains thought to be related to QoL
including functional ability, degree and quality of social and
community interaction, psychological well-being, and somatic
sensation (pain).3,4 Of the 11 subscales included in the composite score, five relate to visual function and/or functional
limitations imposed by visual impairment, one addresses ocular

pain, and one addresses driving limitations. The remaining four
are more global covering social functioning, mental health, role
difficulties, and dependency.16 Yet, all are worded in such a way
that they are restricted to limitations imposed by vision and therefore do not address life as a whole (i.e., limitations imposed by
other conditions or economic and environmental circumstances)
and none directly address the individual’s satisfaction with life or
overall perception of QoL. The one question relating to overall
health, which was not restricted to visual impairment (i.e., general
health), was found to make the greatest contribution in predicting
PQoL of the three variables assessed. However, this question is
deliberately excluded from the composite score. With that measure
included, the instrument would explain a much greater proportion
of variance in PQoL than it does presently. Yet, it still would not
exceed 30%.
Therefore, it appears that the NEI-VFQ-25, especially as represented by its composite score, is more a measure of visual disability
rather than QoL, as it has been previously characterized.22 Although
measures of visual disability can provide valuable information about
functional limitations caused by vision loss, they are not expected to
measure the impact of vision loss on QoL.1 Thus, if one of the purposes of using QoL as an outcome measure is to identify an improvement in PQoL by clients or patients as a result of receiving low vision
or vision-related services, as it has been stated,1, 3 then it appears that
this measure of vision-related QoL will not meet that goal. A measure
that directly assesses PQoL should be used. Such a measure may of
course be used in addition to any other measure of outcome employed,
as PQoL, especially as assessed by a single-item global measure of QoL,
only provides one view of QoL.13 Although it may in some way include all factors affecting QoL,4 it will not provide information concerning those factors.13 Further research is required to determine the
extent to which this finding may apply to other measures of vision related QoL.
