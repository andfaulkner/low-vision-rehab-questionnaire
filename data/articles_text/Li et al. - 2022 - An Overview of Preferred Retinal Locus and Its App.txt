ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

Central vision loss (CVL) caused by macular damage generally disables common daily tasks, which cannot
be reversed by present treatments. Fortunately, it has been found that biofeedback training by inducing
or reinforcing preferred retinal locus (PRL) as an eccentric fixation reference contributes to the improvement of visual performance in patients with CVL. However, the clinical application is still under controversy due to poor knowledge of its fundamental and inconsistent practical standards. This article aims
to summarize the possible rationale for the development, location, re-location and evaluating indicators
of PRL, and the general apparatus, protocol, and outcome of biofeedback PRL training.

PREFERRED RETINAL LOCUS (PRL)
The Development of Multiple PRL
Normal human adults habitually use a highly efficient, fovealcentered visual routine. When the macular area becomes dysfunctional, people with central vision loss (CVL) often adopt a
region outside the central macular area as the surrogate fovea
for visual tasks, termed the preferred retinal locus.1,2 CVL has
been reported to impair visual functions, such as visual acuity,
contrast sensitivity, contrast discrimination, stereoscopic depth
perception, color vision, light sensitivity and fixation stability.3
In cases of central retinal damage, how can we perform visual
tasks? White and Bedell70 provided evidence that saccades
placed the fixational object close to PRL instead of fovea, and
this kind of shift was based on the oculomotor re-reference.
Cohen and Legargasson5 proved that multiple (more than one)
areas of the perifoveal retina might become the new oculomotor reference after the anatomic destruction of the central
foveal retina. In other words, several PRL developed that differed depending on the fixated target. Furthermore, Lei and
Schuchard’s68 research indicated that in the visual system, the
high-illuminance PRL (PRLhi) and low-illuminance PRL
(PRLlo) developed simultaneously when visual function was
adapting to maculopathy, with the use of each depending on
the brightness of objects used in visual tasks, clearly in consistent with the existence of multiple PRL. However, a handful of
researchers suggested that multiple PRLs were likely an expression for an unstable transitional stage of disease, especially for
Stargardt disease. They considered that several PRLs seemed to
be an unfavorable strategy to solve one task.7,26,13
If asingle PRL is more beneficial for a definite training task,
why are multiple PRLs observed for along period of time?

The Functionalization of PRL Based on Visual Cortical
Reorganization
How do single PRL or multiple PRLs develop in patients with
central scotoma? The underlying mechanism is ongoing investigation. We acknowledge that vision loss due to retinal and
central neuronal damage has previously been considered irreversible. However, considerable potential for vision restoration
and recovery even in adulthood has been observed in recent
studies. Sabel etal.73 proposed the “residual vision activation
theory” on how visual functions could be reactivated and
restored. They suggested that cerebral visual injury is usually
partial and that there are some residual fields, including partial
damage areas at the visual field border, “islands” of surviving
tissue inside the blind field, extrastriate pathways unaffected by
the damage, and downstream, higher-level neuronal networks.
These residual structures could be reactivated by engaging
them in repetitive stimulation by different means: (1) visual
experience, (2) visual training, or (3) noninvasive electrical
brain current stimulation. Baker etal.14 reported the large scale reorganization of visual processing in two individuals
with aloss of foveal input from macular degeneration, and
their subsequent research replicated this result in three new
individuals, while in two other individuals with extensive retinal lesions but some foveal sparing, they found no evidence for
reorganization. Therefore, they concluded that the large-scale
reorganization of visual processing occurred only in the complete absence of functional foveal vision.15 Consistently, Dilks
etal.16 reported six individuals with complete bilateral central
vision loss showed visual cortical reorganization, whereas two
with bilateral central vision loss but with foveal sparing did not;
subsequently, in their further research, they also confirmed
that the complete loss of foveal function was anecessary prerequisite for the large-scale reorganization of visual processing
in individuals.17
Additionally, Schumacher etal.1811 used fMRI to measure
brain activity in the calcarine sulcus while visually stimulating
peripheral retinal regions in patients with macular disease.
Their results showed that visual stimulation (a fixation cross)
of PRL in the retina increased brain activity in the cortex,
normally representing central vision relative to the visual stimulation of the perifovea and relative to stimulation in the

evidence clearly indicates that cortical plasticity in the visual
system remains in patients suffered CVL in life and that the
functionalization of PRL is based on visual cortical reorganization which lies on complete foveal visual input loss.
The Location of PRL
As is well known, the location of one PRL can be described
either in terms of its position in the retina or in relation to its
visual field relative to the scotoma, for example, the “superior
visual field” or, in retinal coordinates, the “inferior retina”. In
addition, PRL is usually situated in a relatively healthy area of
the retina close to the edge of the dysfunctional macular
region.1,19 Denniss etal.20 demonstrated that the location of
PRL was typically only foveal to, but not within, aregion of
relatively normal sensitivity for the individual located approximately 15 degrees horizontally and vertically. Verdina etal.26
investigated the morphofunctional features of PRL and the
transition zone (TZ) in a series of patients with recessive
Stargardt disease, and they observed that the eccentric PRL
often distances slightly from the superior edge or border of
the atrophic area, where the TZ is defined. Recently, Erbezci
and Ozturk21 included 72 patients (144 eyes) with macular
degeneration and found the most frequent location of PRL
appeared in the nasal retina (29.2%) and PRL was located in
the left visual field of 34.0% of the patients. In Fletcher’s initial
evaluation for 99 subjects with macular scotoma, they investigated the relative location of scotoma to fovea or PRL in retina:
22% only to the right, 15% only to the left, 26% both to the right
and left, 19% above or below, and 17% no dense scotomas.3
Furthermore, Michael and Gary22 drew conclusions on data
from several research papers featuring the characteristics of
PRL to macular scotomas in retina for alarger low-vision
population (1,500 eyes): 37% use aPRL placed above their
scotoma, 33% to the right, 19% to the left, and 6% below the
scotoma. Less than 2% used a central position (fixating within
their scotoma), and 3% had amixed strategy of multiple PRL.
In summary, PRL occurs in three different locations: first,
within the scotoma with incomplete damage to the fovea; second, on the edge of the lesion; third, far away from lesion,
especially for Stargardt disease. Yet, the specific location relative to scotoma is generally undefined, that is, superior (from
45°-135°), inferior (225°-315°), nasal (315°-45°), and temporal
(135°-225°) scotoma are all possible.26
Superficially, the distribution of PRL seems somewhat confusing and arbitrary; for example, what is the determinator of
PRL location? How can one confirm the position of PRL?
There appears to be no simple rule by which patients “select”
a particular PRL. The underlying mechanism of the selection of
the PRL location is not fully understood. Cheung and Legge23
proposed three selection categories: function-driven selection,

performance-driven selection, and retinotopy-driven selection.
The function-driven selection suggests that PRL location may
be determined by the nature of the visual task. It has been
reported that PRL located in the superior retina (inferior visual
field) is better for reading than the inferior retina (superior
visual field), and PRL location to the left of scotoma will disturb
patients to read the subsequent text.24,18,63 Consistent with the
function-driven selection theory, patterns of PRL locations in
some diseases have been reported. Walsh and Liu35 observed
that in normal subjects with simulated scotoma, there was a
theoretical advantage in using a vertically shifted PRL for reading, as this reduced the effect of the scotoma when making eye
movements along a line of text. Likewise, Greenstein etal.19
demonstrated that patients with Stargardt disease are more
likely to develop a PRL in the superior retina because a PRL
in the superior retina might be more advantageous for performing tasks in the inferior visual field (i.e., writing).
However, if a horizontally shifted PRL was used in patients
with AMD, there was an advantage to place PRL in the right
visual field rather than the left, as this would interfere less with
the perceptual span for reading than would a PRL to the left of
the fixation. Performance-driven selection suggests that PRL
will either be located at the undamaged retinal location with
the highest visual acuity or, on the basis of visual attention, in
regions with high attentional performance due to the enhancement of visual performance in those regions.42 Barraza-Bernal
etal.28 investigated the relationship between the placement of
sustained attention and the location of a developed PRL using
simulations of central scotoma in 13 normally sighted subjects.
Seventy percent of the subjects had a PRL location in the
hemifield where high attentional performance was achieved.
They suggested that attentional capabilities could be used as a
predictor for the development of the PRL and were of significance for low-vision rehabilitation and for the development of
new PRL training procedures. Finally, the retinotopy-driven
selection suggests that PRL selection might be a consequence of
retinotopic reorganizations.23 In conclusion, there may not
only be one governing mechanism involved in the process of
PRL development, which explains various PRLs during prolonged fixation adaptation.60 In addition, to some extent, the
location in which PRL develops may not always be the most
efficient one, because various daily tasks require distinguishing
visual performances and different PRL locations. For instance,
facial recognition or watching television is usually the primary
task for many low-vision patients in daily life instead of reading
or writing; thus, some PRL may not be optimal for one specific
task.6,44,30
The Relocation of PRL
Likewise, Barraza-Bernal etal.31 agree on the hypothesis above
that patients do not always choose the most efficient PRL
position, and consequently, visual performance is not always
fully exploited; therefore, they further induce the relocation of
PRL by applying systematic stimulus. With repeated usage, a
new PRL will be established from a special oculomotor and
visual performance at the retinal location in the normal
periphery will produce anew PRL beyond the natural PRL.
Recently, the training of PRL has been highlighted in low

vision rehabilitation, and researchers have demonstrated that
PRL relocation clearly improves the functional vision and
quality of life of patients with CVL [6, 45, 62, 65]. Deruaz etal.32
designed some procedures to train a new examiner’s selected
PRL and to develop a new oculomotor behavior in patients
with central field loss and eventually confirmed the feasibility
of PRL relocation through visual training. Moreover, in
Hassan’s study, the location of the retinal area used for fixation can be changed after eccentric viewing training.33
Conversely, Guez etal.34 reported that a repeatable retinal
area for fixation developed within 6 months in all patients,
half of them using their final PRL location at the first assessment, which developed within 1 month of scotoma development. For this reason, they believed that there is a slim chance
that PRL locations would change after the initial development. While multiple factors are certainly behind PRL development, in some cases, the natural PRL developed
spontaneously maintains for along period of time without
other training schemes. Additionally, other researchers have
reported that many factors contribute to the PRL development, say, the duration of scotoma formation, the location of
scotomas, pursuit ability, attentional capabilities, and saccadic
ability.7,27,42,26,72 All of these factors might be significant for
the design of training strategies to relocate PRL for visually
impaired patients. Thus, we must take more perspectives into
account in the training scheme to achieve optimal PRL
training.
Chung etal.1 have concluded two challenges in adapting to
a central scotoma and in developing a PRL. The first is scotoma
awareness. Contrary to most of the central scotoma illustrations, most CVL patients do not see a gray or black patch
blocking their direct vision. The other is PRL selection. When
all retinal locations around the simulated scotoma are functionally equivalent, the opportunistic use of retinal locations
that were near or in the direct paths of the search target would
be the most efficient way to perform the task. This view is also
supported by Walsh35 and Treleaven.42

Fixation Location and Stability
Evaluation criterion of fixation location: the fixation is qualified as a“predominantly central fixation” if >50% of fixation
points are within the central circle; eyes with >25% but <50%
are labeled as “poor central fixation”; eyes with <25% fixation
points in the central circle are classified as “predominantly
eccentric fixation”.36
For the first time, the classification of fixation stability
has been suggested by Fujii and colleagues.37 Fixation stability can be characterized both by the percentage of fixation points (PFP) and the bivariate contour ellipse area
(BCEA). PEP is calculated from the scatter of acloud of
fixation points over a retinal image reference map. The PFP
fall inside acircle of 1°and 2°radii is defined, respectively, as
P1 and P2. When P1 is greater than 75%, eyes are classified
as having stable fixation. When P1 is less than 75% and P2
is more than 75%, it is classified as relatively unstable.

When both P1 and P2 are less than 75%, the pattern is
described as unstable fixation.38
Additionally, fixation stability can also be mathematically
measured by BCEA, which calculates the area of an ellipse that
encloses agiven percentage of fixation points according to the
values of the standard deviations of the horizontal and vertical
eye movements during fixation. The lower BCEA values define
better fixation stability. Several advantages remain in this measurement: firstly, more information can be contained than the
initial grading scale reported by Fujii; second, its variability is
independent of the magnitude; finally, its quantitative expression dramatically contributes to the statistical analysis.8,39–42
We can even acquire the x and y coordinates of the centroid of
the ellipse, which represents the average location of PRL, and
the orientation angle (relative to the horizontal meridian) of
the bivariate ellipse, which represents the angular direction of
the fixation ellipse. However, it was impossible to determine
whether the eye visited the same fixation location multiple
times while measuring fixation stability and the computed
bivariate ellipse area may be an overestimation of subjects’
fixation stability if it is defined by the relative time spent at
each point rather than the area covered during a time
interval.33

Pursuit Ability
Pursuit is the tracking of moving objects through space.
The subject is asked to follow amoving cross and to rate on
the basis of whether the moving objects can be maintained
within the discrete-specific PRL. The pursuit movement
score is used to evaluate the pursuit ability, and it is
divided into the following five levels: (1) moving target
speed >5 degrees persecond maintains fast-moving target
within PRL for pursuit; (2) moving target speed <5 degrees
persecond indicates slow-moving target within PRL for
pursuit; (3) movement of eye in same direction as movement of target but cannot maintain target within PRL; (4)
able to appreciate the moving target but cannot move eyes
toward the direction of it indicates no established PRL; and
(5) no appreciation of moving targets.4,43,44

Saccadic Ability
Saccadic is to move rapidly to objects in the visual field away
from PRL. Subjects are asked to look at two targets presented in
the posterior pole 10° apart horizontally and vertically. Then,
individuals rate whether the PRL location is moved from target
to target in awell-defined saccadic motion when the eye is
moving. The saccadic ability score can be used to evaluate the
saccadic ability, and it is divided into the following three levels:
(1) consistently moves PRL to the presented appreciated target
and fixates on targets that are jumping once persecond, normal
eye pattern; (2) inconsistent ability to move PRL to presented
appreciated target and fixate on targets that are jumping once
persecond, may make more than one saccadic eye movement
to targets; and (3) no ability to move PRL to the jumping
target.43,44

Improved acuity is not guaranteed through repeated usage of
PRL. Occasionally, improvements are limited at the retinal
level, so we need to determine whether there is plasticity in
the sensory system. Yashar etal.71demonstrated that cortical

level crowding could be rapidly reduced and the ignorance of
irrelevant flankers could be remarkably improved. Crowding
refers to the “increased difficulty in identifying targets that are
closely adjacent to other targets.45” It is characterized by measuring critical spacing, the minimum distance needed between

An important property of crowding is that both the magnitude

Attentional Capabilities
Attentional performance is unequal for all eccentric positions,
leading to variations in perception. Barraza-Bernal etal.28
investigated the relationship between the place of sustained
attention and the location of adeveloped PRL, and the results
suggested that the training PRL is prone to be located in the
retinal region with high attentional capabilities. Altpeter etal.46
designed the following program to evaluate the measurement for sustained attention: step 1, presenting a fixation cross
in the center of the screen for 1 s; step 2, displaying ared cue
at an eccentricity of 8 degrees for 1 s, in which the target
would be presented afterwards (the locations tested were
placed at 8 degrees of eccentricity along changed meridians
in 45° increments from 0° to 315°, and the red cue was used to
achieve the attentional sustainment); and step 3, a Snellen
Eappeared in the cued location and other meridians, and
subjects were asked to report the orientation of the Snellen E.
Sustained attention can be calculated by the following formula: Rg = per (90)/pre (270); per (90) and per (270) are
defined as the percentage of correct responses at 90° and
270°, respectively. When Rg <0.8, the performance in the
upper location is reduced; when Rg >1.2, the performance in
the lower location is reduced; and when 1.2≥ Rg ≥0.8, the
performance is similar in the upper and lower locations.

BIOFEEDBACK TRAINING FOR LOW-VISION
REHABILITATION USING MICROPERIMETER
Low-vision Rehabilitation for CVL Patients Based on PRL
Training
CVL is accompanied by psychosocial quality of life in difficulty
including reading, driving, watching videos, identifying faces,
and performing countless daily tasks.47–49 The aim of low vision rehabilitation for CVL patients is to use the residual
vision as effectively and efficiently as possible to enable patients
to live independently and to make lives easier, and then
enhance their quality of life.50 However, it is necessary to
individualize the training program for different macular
lesions. For small and sharp scotoma, the natural PRL may be
optimal for patients; thus, there is no need to perform PRL
training. While for large and rough scotoma, fixation may not
be stable enough or PRL may be far away with fovea, in this
case, the appropriate PRL training should be employed to
improve visual function for CVL patients.6 Microperimeter

has become the most popular equipment for macular function
detection.38,51 The microperimetry TRL (trained retinal locus)
mode, based on acoustic or luminous biofeedback training,
enhances fixation stability if PRL is in a suitable location but
is not stable enough or if PRL is not in a suitable location, to
identify and relocate PRL to a locus with higher retinal sensitivity through TRL training sessions.22,50
In addition to microperimetric eccentric viewing training,
some visual search training can also be used in helping PRL
development. David35 used a gaze-contingent display to simulate an isotropic central scotoma in normal subjects while they
were practicing adifficult visual search task, at the end of a
1782-trial training, participants showed fewer fixations, fewer
repeated fixations in the same area of the search stimulus and
aclear tendency of using one area near the border of the
scotoma to identify the search target, but the fixation and
saccade duration have no improvement. Ratra etal.45 explored
the possibility of visual improvement using video game stimulation for visual rehabilitation in Stargardt disease. Recruited
eight patients showed statistical improvement in visual acuity,
contrast sensitivity, fixation stability, stereopsis and pattern
visual evoked potentials after being given 40 hof training with
video game play, but the increase in retinal sensitivity had no
statistical significance.

PRL Training Apparatus and Pattern
The most commonly used apparatus to train PRL are MP-1
and MAIA. In the early 2000s, MP-1 was developed to overcome the SLO limitations. It has ahalogen lamp that generates
an infrared (IR) light conveyed to the retina. The fundus image
is 45° visual field with aresolution of 768 × 576 pixels. An
independent non-mydriatic color fundus camera with an
image resolution of 1,392 × 1,040 pixels is also supplied. The
eye tracker monitors the eye position with afrequency of 25
times persecond (25 Hz). MP-1 automatically calculates fixation stability under the grading of stable, relatively unstable,
and unstable from Fujii. Meanwhile, BCEA can be measured
showing the area (square degrees) and the angle (degrees) of
68.2%, 95.4%, and 99.6% ellipse areas.22,51,53
The optical source of MAIA is an infrared superluminescent
diode at 850 nm. It has a digital camera with aresolution of
1,024 × 1,024 pixels and avisual field of 36°× 36°, and the
optical resolution on the retina is 25 microns that allows
black and white high-quality retinal imaging. The tracking
speed is also 25 Hz.22,38 Compared to MP-1, MAIA provides
an easier interaction between the operator and the device by
automatic eye focusing and very intuitive software. Its 2013
Edition provides apushbutton for presence detection to ensure
the reliability of text and multiple-fixation targets for patients
with light to very severe macular damage. MAIA also has
aquantization of fixation stability by plotting the position of
each fixation on Cartesian axes and calculating the area of an
ellipse that encompasses agiven percentage of fixations (63%
BCEA and 95% BCEA).22,38

they are getting closer to TRL. The tone becomes more continuous as the patient’s gaze is closer to TRL, otherwise it
becomes discontinuous. A black and white checkerboard pattern is superimposed onto the fixation target during luminous
training, and the size is selected according to the visual acuity
of patients. Patients are invited to fixate on the target while the
stimulus is flickering.50,54,55 It has been demonstrated that
acoustic and luminous training are both useful methods to
improve functional parameters in rehabilitated eyes, but luminous training is more likely to induce cortical reorganization
after intensive training compared with acoustic one, because
there may be more information transmitted to the brain to
accelerate the process that TRL transformed to a new PRL and
the visual cortex reorganization.39,54,55

The Training Scheme and Outcome
Although eccentric fixation training (PRL training) for patients
with CVL has been carried out for many years, the biofeedback
fixation training with microperimeter has still been in the early
stage. The appropriate start, training interval, training frequency,
outcome of training and the sustained effect are all divided in
various studies (see table 1). Current studies demonstrated that
independent acoustic biofeedback training commonly performs
a total of 10–12 sessions with training cycle including once
aweek,44,56–58 twice a week,59 three times a week,64 alternative
days,61,62,8 and others.37 While in a very few studies, more sessions were conducted, including a total of 16 sessions by training
once or twice a week,64,65,52 and fewer sessions were implemented in Verdina’s research that patients were trained once aweek
for 8 weeks in total. Combined with audio, luminous biofeedback training has seen more benefits than acoustic methods
alone, with a training scheme similar to mono-training, that is,
perform about 10–12 sessions, once or twice a week.42,54,55
Not only is the training scheme diverged, but the outcome of
training is also varied. Although a majority of patients have
reported a positive outcome from the training and subjective
improvement in doing their near or distance vision-related daily
activities, such as reading, face recognition, walking, identifying
bus numbers, whether the objective measurements will be
improved remains controversial, including the visual acuity, retinal sensitivity, fixation behaviour, reading speed, contrast sensitivity, retinal potential change, and so on. Amyriad of researchers
reported that patients with CVL obtained a statistical improvement
of vision acuity, retinal sensitivity, fixation behaviour, and retinal
potential changes after biofeedback eccentric fixation training (see
in table), while there have still been aminority of studies that
indicated otherwise. Verboschi58 evaluated the efficacy of visual
rehabilitation with MP-1 microperimeter biofeedback in advanced
optic neural dysfunction due to glaucoma, and the results showed
that the mean best corrected visual acuity at the end of the visual
rehabilitation was statistically insignificant, although the mean
retinal sensitivity and reading speed were improved statistically.
Vingolo69 further compared the efficacy of visual rehabilitation by
means of acoustic biofeedback and luminous biofeedback. Thirty

147

patients bilaterally affected by AMD were randomly divided into
two groups and, respectively, received acoustic or luminous training which was simultaneously performed 12 sessions by once
aweek. Both groups showed self-reported better visual performance, while only retinal sensitivity was improved in the acoustic
training group. By contrast, the best corrected visual acuity, reading speed and fixation stability were all improved after luminous
flickering biofeedback training.4 Amore etal.54 designed the same
research, and the results showed that mean reading speed and
fixation stability significantly increased for both training strategies.
However, only patients trained with the flickering pattern biofeedback stimulation enjoyed an improvement in retinal sensitivity.
Therefore, they concluded that both acoustic biofeedback and
luminous biofeedback training improved visual functions and
that more benefits come with luminous biofeedback training.
These various results may be stemmed from the different types
and durations of diseases that patients who are enrolled have
suffered, because these generally lead to different extents of
damage to the function of peripheral retina, let alone scotomas
and PRL locations that may change as disease progresses. Reading,
acomplicated perceptual learning process, is impacted by multiple
factors, say, the basis of visual acuity, the fixation stability, visual span size, crowding and attentional capability, so as the reading
speed. Visual span is viewed as the number of neighboring characters that can be reliably recognized without moving the eyes,
which imposes a sensory limit on reading speed, and the crowding
is a determinant of the scale of visual span.42 Thereby, it is another
contributing factor of reading speed. As we previously mentioned,
attentional capability contributes to PRL development, so extraattentional capacity may be required when using peripheral vision
to read. Alternatively, we need to further consider that visual
performance likely includes multiple cortical levels in both retinotopic and nonretinotopic areas and the connection between various processing stages. Thus, extensive indeterminations are the
basis for the discrepancy among different biofeedback training
studies.

CONCLUSION
Patients with long-standing scotomas usually develop one or
multiple PRLs for eccentric fixation. Helped by biofeedback
training, individuals are able to obtain a better or perfect
anatomical correspondence of the PRL location, which is probably based on cortical plasticity in the visual system.
Furthermore, the training strategies can be summarized as
independent acoustic biofeedback, independent luminous biofeedback, combined training protocol using various microperimeters and other equipment and other visual search training.
Hitherto, functional outcome improvements in training are
still controversial. In addition, the training efficacy can be
evaluated by the following indicators: subjective visual quality,
visual acuity, fixation location, fixation stability, retinal sensitivity, contrast sensitivity, eye movement speed, reading capability, retinal potential change, and so on. After training, an
overwhelming majority of patients can accomplish some daily
tasks that are impossible prior to training, which directly and
clearly improve their quality of daily life.

A MEDLINE/PUBMED search was conducted for the terms,
such as “preferred retinal locus”, “biofeedback training”, “central vision loss”, “eccentric fixation”, “microperimeter”, and
“low-vision rehabilitation” (with no time limitation). First, we
comprehensively read all the abstracts of the articles that were
obtained from the search; then, a total of 73 papers that were
relevant to the contents of this review were selected eventually.
