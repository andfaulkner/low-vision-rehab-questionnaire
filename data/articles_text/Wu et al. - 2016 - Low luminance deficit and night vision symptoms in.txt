ABSTRACT
Background/aims To determine the relationship
between self-reported visual difﬁculties under low
luminance conditions (night vision symptoms) and visual
function measures in intermediate age-related macular
degeneration (AMD).
Methods One hundred participants with bilateral
intermediate AMD were examined in a prospective cross sectional study with visual function measures including
best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), low luminance visual
acuity (LLVA) and microperimetry in both eyes. A 10-item
Night Vision Questionnaire (NVQ-10) was then used to
determine the degree of self-reported night vision
symptoms experienced by each participant. For analyses,
low luminance deﬁcit (LLD) was derived as the difference
between LLVA and BCVA, and microperimetric mean
sensitivity (MS; all points) and central sensitivity (CS;
points within the central 1°) were determined. Rasch
analysis was used to estimate the person measure of
night vision symptoms, and its relationship with visual
function parameters was determined.
Results NVQ-10 person measures were signiﬁcantly
associated with LLD (β coefﬁcient=0.067, 95% CI 0.005
to 0.130, p=0.034), but not BCVA, LLVA,
microperimetric MS or CS ( p≥0.090). Participants with
the highest degree of self-reported night vision
symptoms (fourth quartile of person measure) had
signiﬁcantly worse LLD than those with the least
difﬁculty (ﬁrst quartile of person measure; p=0.019).
Conclusions In individuals with bilateral intermediate
AMD, LLD was associated with self-reported night vision
symptoms, suggesting that this measure may better
capture the visual difﬁculties experienced by these
individuals under low luminance conditions than the
conventional measure of photopic visual acuity.

Vision Questionnaire (NVQ-10). However, the
relationship between the degree of night vision
symptoms and clinical measures of visual function
under low luminance has not yet been examined.
These relationships are important to determine in
order to identify clinical measures that can better
reﬂect such visual difﬁculties, which are not currently captured using the conventional measure of
photopic visual acuity.10
Speciﬁcally, we sought to examine the relationship between night vision symptoms measured by
the NVQ-10 and the visual function measures of
low luminance visual acuity (LLVA) and microperimetry in this study. Microperimetry was examined
since we had previously observed that perimetric
retinal sensitivity was reduced and exhibited a
greater rate of decline in areas that subsequently
developed GA,11 and LLVA was examined since it
exhibits a close relationship with microperimetric
retinal sensitivity.2 In addition, these two measures
are performed under low luminance conditions,
which may better reﬂect the degree of visual difﬁculty under low luminance conditions assessed by
the NVQ-10 than the conventional measure of
photopic visual acuity.

METHODS
This study involved the prospective recruitment of
a consecutive series of participants with bilateral
intermediate AMD, and it was approved by the
Human Ethics Committee of the Royal Victorian
Eye and Ear Hospital (RVEEH) and was conducted
in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.
Written informed consent was obtained from all
participants.

The early stages of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) are associated with a statistically but
not clinically signiﬁcant reduction in visual acuity
measured under optimal illumination and high contrast conditions.1 2 However, previous studies
have reported that individuals with the early stages
of AMD report vision-related difﬁculties in performing daily activities compared with those with
normal retinal health.3–5 These difﬁculties are especially marked for activities performed under low
luminance conditions.5–7
The degree of self-reported visual difﬁculties
under low luminance conditions (referred to as
night vision symptoms) has been reported to be
associated with an increased risk of developing
advanced AMD,8 especially geographic atrophy
(GA).8 9 In these studies, the degree of night vision

Participants were recruited from private ophthalmology clinics and the medical retina clinics at the
RVEEH. The inclusion criteria were being 50 years
of age or older, having a best-corrected visual
acuity (BCVA) of 20/40 (or 0.30 logarithm of the
minimum angle of resolution (logMAR)) or better
and having intermediate AMD (deﬁned as having at
least drusen >125 μm12) in both eyes. The exclusion criteria included the presence of any choroidal
neovascularisation, any atrophic changes including
GA detected on colour fundus photography,
drusen-associated atrophy or nascent GA detected
on spectral-domain optical coherence tomography
(SD-OCT),13 signiﬁcant cataracts, glaucoma,
amblyopia or any corneal pathology that would
compromise vision in either eye. Participants were
also excluded if they had diabetes, had any neurologic or systemic disease affecting vision, were

Examination procedures
Visual acuity measurements and microperimetry examinations
were performed prior to fundus imaging, to prevent bleaching
of the retina from the imaging light sources.

Visual acuity measurements
A standardised refraction protocol was performed monocularly
using an Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study
(ETDRS) refraction chart at a testing distance of 4 m and with a
standard test luminance of 85 cd/m2. BCVA was also measured
at the same distance. A 2.0-log unit neutral density ﬁlter (Kodak
Wratten ﬁlter; Kodak, Rochester, New York, USA) was then
placed in front of the study eye and LLVA was measured without
additional time for adaptation, since a previous study has shown
that no additional period of adaptation was required for eyes
with GA when using this procedure to measure LLVA.14 15 For
all measurements, participants were asked to guess all the letters
in a given line, and visual acuity measurements were recorded in
logMAR, with each letter given a score of 0.02 logMAR.

Microperimetry examination
Microperimetric examinations were performed using the
Macular Integrity Assessment (MAIA, CenterVue, Padova, Italy)
microperimeter as outlined in detail previously.16 Brieﬂy, they
were performed following pupillary dilation using 1 drop of 1%
tropicamide and 1 drop of 2.5% phenylephrine to achieve a
pupil diameter of at least 6 mm. All participants were given
identical verbal instructions regarding how to perform the
microperimetry examination.
The MAIA performs fundus tracking using a line-scanning
laser ophthalmoscope with a super-luminescent diode illumination that has a central wavelength of 850 nm. It uses the entire
fundus as a reference when performing fundus tracking, capturing the fundus images at 25 frames per second. With fundus
tracking, test stimuli can be presented at the same retinal location throughout the test independent of shifts in ﬁxation. A red
circular ﬁxation target of 1° diameter was used in this study, and
Goldman III stimuli were presented for 200 ms against a background of 1.27 cd/m2 using a 4–2 threshold strategy. The
maximum stimulus luminance was 318 cd/m2, creating a
dynamic range of 36 dB of differential contrast. A customised
stimulus grid (CERA AMD 6° grid) consisting of 37 points
located at 0°, 1°, 2.33°, 4° and 6° from ﬁxation was used in this
study, designed by our group speciﬁcally for the assessment of
the macular region in AMD.
In this study, two examinations were performed on the right
eye followed by one examination on the left eye for all participants; approximately 3 min of rest was given between each test
to minimise the effect of fatigue. The ﬁrst examination of the
right eye was discarded from the analyses to avoid the inﬂuence
of an intrasession learning effect, as previously reported.16 Test
reliability for the second examination was assessed by the frequency of false-positive responses, measured by presentations of
suprathreshold stimulus to the physiological blind spot, which
was manually located on the MAIA before the presentation of
the ﬁrst stimuli. Any test with false-positive responses of >25%
was considered unreliable and was repeated. This cut-off was
chosen because the MAIA presents a false-positive stimulus
approximately every 1 min, and there are typically only four to
2

ﬁve false-positive stimuli presented in each test given the short
duration of the examinations.
The average retinal sensitivity of all points within the stimulus
grid in this study was termed mean sensitivity (MS). The
average of the ﬁve stimuli within the central 1° (comprising of
one point at 0° and four points at 1°) was termed central sensitivity (CS), and was analysed in this study since it reﬂects foveal
function, and we have found previously that it corresponds
closely with visual acuity measurements.2

Imaging and fundus grading
Fundus imaging was performed using colour fundus photography (Canon CR6-45NM; Canon, Saitama, Japan), and nearinfrared reﬂectance (NIR), fundus autoﬂuorescence (FAF) and
SD-OCT line and volume scans all performed on a Spectralis
HRA+OCT (Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). A
45° diameter colour fundus photograph and 30°×30° NIR and
FAF images centred on the macular were acquired. For
SD-OCT, a high-resolution horizontal single-line scan was performed with 100 frames averaged, followed by a volume scan of
49 B-scans of the central 20°×20° area, with 25 frames averaged
for each B-scan. An experienced grader performed all the
grading on colour fundus photographs17 to determine the presence of intermediate AMD according to a recent clinical classiﬁcation system.12 The multimodal imaging modalities were then
used to ensure that the inclusion and exclusion criteria were met
(such as the absence of atrophic changes).

Night Vision Questionnaire
The NVQ measures night vision functioning using a 10-item
scale derived from four items of the National Eye Institute
Vision Functioning Questionnaire (NEI-VFQ) 25 items and six
items of a night vision symptom list.8 Response options are
Likert scales with ﬁve options rating difﬁculty (‘None’, ‘A little
difﬁculty’, ‘Moderate difﬁculty’, ‘Extreme difﬁculty’, ‘Stopped
doing this because of your eyesight’; item 1–4) or four options
rating impact (‘Not at all bothered’, ‘A little bothered’,
‘Somewhat bothered’, ‘Very bothered’; item 5–10). Items 1–3
could not be scored if the participant answered ‘Stopped doing
this for other reasons or not interested in doing this’ or ‘Not
currently driving’. Given the ubiquity of lighting at night-time
in the urbanised society where this cohort was examined, the
item content is related to mesopic rather than scotopic visual
function. Thus, we refer to the construct captured by the
NVQ-10 as night vision symptoms, but we assessed its association with measures of mesopic rather than scotopic visual function. A list of all items can be found in online supplementary
table S1.

Statistical analysis
We performed Rasch analysis on the NVQ-10 using Winsteps
software (V.3.68, Chicago, Illinois, USA) to estimate scores that
approximate interval-level measurement ( person measure;
expressed in log of the odds units (logits)) from raw ordinal
data. The NVQ-10 person measures capture night vision symptoms, with a higher person measures score indicating more
severe night vision symptoms.
In this study, the eye with better BCVA was speciﬁed as the
study eye for all visual function measures to be analysed. When
BCVA was the same in both eyes, the eye with the better LLVA
was speciﬁed as the study eye; the study eye was randomly allocated using computer-generated numbers if LLVA was also the
same in both eyes. The association between the person measure

RESULTS
A total of 100 participants were included in this study. Their
mean age was 69.8±7.4 years (range 51–81 years), 74 participants (74%) were female and 13 participants (13%) were not
currently drivers (having answered ‘Stopped doing this for other
reasons or not interested in doing this’ or ‘Not currently
driving’ in response to items 1–3). The characteristics of the
visual function measures are outlined in table 1.

Night vision symptoms and visual function measures
The mean Rasch-generated person measure score for self reported night vision symptoms for this cohort was −2.85
±1.86 logits (range −5.78 to 1.50). Using univariate linear
regression analyses, the person measure score was only signiﬁcantly associated with low luminance deﬁcit (LLD; β coefﬁcient=0.067, 95% CI 0.005 to 0.130, p=0.034), but not

as a covariate in each of the univariate linear regression analyses
(data not shown).

DISCUSSION
In this study, LLD but not microperimetric retinal sensitivity
was signiﬁcantly associated with the degree of self-reported
night vision symptoms measured using the NVQ-10 in individuals with bilateral intermediate AMD. Since the degree of
these symptoms have been previously reported to confer an
increased risk of progression to advanced AMD,8 9 LLD may
also potentially confer such prognostic information and can be
used as a clinical visual function parameter to reﬂect the degree
of such symptoms. Therefore, the ﬁndings of this study suggest
that LLD may be a useful visual function parameter that can be
easily implemented in clinical settings and may better capture
the degree of night vision symptoms experienced by individuals
with intermediate AMD than the conventional measure of
BCVA.
To date, this is the ﬁrst study to examine the relationship
between the degree of self-reported night vision symptoms measured using the NVQ-10 and clinical measures of visual function
under low luminance conditions. The only other questionnaire
designed speciﬁcally to assess self-reported low luminance visual
difﬁculties is the Low Luminance Questionnaire (LLQ), containing 32 items which assesses problems with activities under low
luminance conditions.6 Using the LLQ, persons with AMD have
been found to report a signiﬁcant impairment independent of
their degree of visual impairment.18 The self-reported low luminance difﬁculty as measured by the LLQ has also been found to
correlate with the rod-mediated, but not cone-mediated, components of dark adaptation measured at a peripheral retinal location.6 Since the two studies are not directly comparable because
of the measures used and disease severity of the participants
included, it remains to be examined whether rod-mediated measures better correlate with self-reported night vision symptoms
as measured by the NVQ-10 in eyes with intermediate AMD
without substantial loss of BCVA. However, it is more likely that
a battery of visual function measures is required to capture the
extent of self-reported night vision symptoms, since different
activities under low luminance conditions are mediated by
different components of vision; for example, ‘reading in dim
light’ (item 6 on the NVQ-10) is likely to require foveal cone mediated function, as compared with a more general ‘vision at
night’ (item 5 on the NVQ-10) which may involve rod-mediated
function predominantly. Given the ubiquity of lighting at nighttime in the urbanised society where this cohort was examined,
vision at night is unlikely to be only rod mediated.
In this study, we found that LLD, but not BCVA or LLVA, was
associated with the degree of night vision symptoms by the
NVQ-10. This is likely due to individuals reporting the degree
of visual difﬁculty under low luminance condition relative to
those under optimal lighting conditions as their intraindividual
reference, in a similar way that LLD reﬂects visual function
under low luminance conditions relative to those under optimal
lighting conditions. We also found in this study that only LLD,
but not microperimetric retinal sensitivity, was found to be associated with the degree of night vision symptoms by the
NVQ-10, even though we have previously found a signiﬁcant
relationship between central retinal sensitivity and LLVA.2
Although both measures are performed under a similar luminance level, they capture distinctly different aspects of visual

function. Microperimetry measures luminance increment thresholds (and thus varying levels of contrast against the background)
and involves only the visual process of detection, while the
measure of LLVA involves the visual processes of detection, resolution and identiﬁcation at a single level of contrast.19 Thus,
although microperimetric retinal sensitivity may detect a greater
degree of foveal functional deﬁcit than the visual acuity measures,2 the ﬁndings of this study suggest that visual acuity measures under low luminance conditions may better reﬂect visual
difﬁculties experienced by individuals. These ﬁndings are especially important when considering relevant clinical end points—
namely, ‘a characteristic or variable that reﬂects how a patient
feels, functions or survives’, as deﬁned by the Biomarkers
Deﬁnitions Working Group20—for interventional studies targeting the early stages of AMD.10 21
There are several limitations in this study that are important
to note, including the small sample size and the lack of a
control group, with the latter meaning that the degree of
self-reported night vision difﬁculties experienced by healthy
individuals and individuals with intermediate AMD could not
be directly compared. However, given that the primary aim of
this study was to examine the associations between such night
vision difﬁculties and visual function measures in individuals
with intermediate AMD, the lack of a control group does not
detract from our interpretation of the results. In addition, the
highly standardised measurements of visual function in this
cohort and use of Rasch analysis provides conﬁdence that these
ﬁndings are valid for the sample we examined, and can be extrapolated with caution. While the inclusion criteria of having a
BCVA of 20/40 or better may also have limited the range of participants examined, these participants were still characterised by
having a wide range of different visual functions when measured
using LLVA and microperimetry. In addition, the majority of
individuals with intermediate AMD do not have a BCVA worse
than this cut-off unless they suffer from additional ocular
disease, which makes our sample representative of the majority
of individuals with intermediate AMD. This criterion was also
chosen since it fell within the vision requirements for driving in
Australia, and allowed an examination of participants that
would be otherwise considered to have relatively preserved
vision clinically. Although the degree of night vision symptoms
have been found to confer an increased risk of progression to
advanced AMD,8 9 it also remains to be determined in future
longitudinal studies whether LLD confers prognostic value for
the early stages of AMD, given the association found between
these two parameters found in this study. Despite previous
studies demonstrating that a period of dark adaptation was not
required when measuring LLVA,14 15 there is a possibility that
we might have underestimated LLVA when using this method in
this study. However, this procedure was consistent for all the
measurements performed in this study, and is therefore unlikely
to inﬂuence the results of this study. Future studies would also
beneﬁt from examining near visual acuity (which was not examined in this study), since reading is an aspect of visual difﬁculty
often reported by participants with the early stages of AMD
that can be measured clinically.
In conclusion, this study showed that the degree of self reported visual difﬁculty under low luminance conditions in
individuals with intermediate AMD is signiﬁcantly associated
with LLD, but not microperimetric retinal sensitivity. These
ﬁndings suggest that LLD may be a useful visual function
measure that can be easily implemented in clinical settings to
reﬂect such visual difﬁculty, beyond what is captured by the conventional measure of photopic visual acuity.
