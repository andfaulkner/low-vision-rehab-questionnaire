The Relationship between Visual Disability
and Visual Field in Patients with Glaucoma
Izumi Sumi, MD,1 Shiroaki Shirato,2 Shun Matsumoto,1 Makoto Araie3
Purpose: To evaluate the relationship between visual disability and the remaining visual field in glaucoma
patients.
Design: Prospective noncomparative survey.
Participants: One hundred forty-seven Japanese patients with glaucoma were examined.
Methods: Using a previously developed questionnaire, we assessed the relationship between visual disability indices and both the visual field examined using program 30-2 of the Humphrey Field Analyzer and visual
acuity.
Main Outcome Measures: Pearson’s correlation coefficients between visual disability indices and the 22
visual field and 2 visual acuity measurements were calculated. Multiple regression analysis with stepwise variable
selection identified factors significantly contributing to visual disability.
Results: We identified a significant correlation between visual disability and both the visual field and visual
acuity measurements collected. Of the factors examined, retinal sensitivity in the lower hemifield within 5° of the
fixation and visual acuity in the better eye significantly contributed to all the item and total disability indices (DIs),
measurements of the degree of visual disability as evaluated by the questionnaire; visual acuity in the worse eye
contributed to two of the seven item DIs, as well as the total DI. The R value for total DI (R ⫽ 0.87, P ⬍ 0.0001)
indicated that approximately 76% of the visual disability could be explained by these factors.
Conclusions: The Japanese glaucoma patients’ visual disability is explained primarily by retinal sensitivity in
the lower hemifield within 5° of the fixation and visual acuity in the better eye and secondarily by visual acuity in
the worse eye. Ophthalmology 2003;110:332–339 © 2003 by the American Academy of Ophthalmology.

It is estimated that 150 million people are visually disabled
in the world today,1 including approximately 307,000 people in Japan.2 According to the World Health Organization,3
the main causes of visual impairment are cataract, trachoma,
glaucoma, and chorioretinal diseases. In developing nations,
cataract and trachoma, both treatable diseases, are the main
causes of visual impairment. In developed countries, however, glaucoma and other chorioretinal or optic nerve diseases are the primary afflictions. Unfortunately, there are no
treatments available which completely avert the onset of
visual impairment caused by these diseases despite recent
medical advances.
Those with low vision confront visual disability in their
daily and social lives, leading to unavoidable deterioration
in overall quality of life. With appropriate rehabilitation
allowing these patients to use the remaining visual abilities,
this disability can be lessened to permit independent lifestyles.4

In providing rehabilitation, we first need to understand
the patient’s perception of visual disability in daily life.
Because visual disability results in changes in individual
skills and abilities, the use of questionnaires is the most
accurate and useful means by which to assess visual disability. Although multiple questionnaires5–12 have been developed in Western countries, most of these questionnaires5–11 aim to assess visual disability in patients with
visual acuity impairment. Visual acuity alone may not be an
accurate predictor of visual function status.8,13 Few questionnaires have been developed to assess visual disability in
patients with visual field impairment but with relatively
intact visual acuity; Mills and Drance12 created a questionnaire intended to assess visual disability in patients with
severe visual field impairment. Glaucoma is one of the main
causes of visual impairment, both in Japan and throughout
the world.3 Often in glaucoma, central visual acuity is
retained until the late stages of the disease, despite severe
constriction of the visual field.14 Because many of the
problems resulting from visual field impairment may differ
markedly from those caused by visual acuity impairment,
development of a reliable questionnaire aimed at this purpose is indispensable for the accurate assessment of visual
disability caused by visual field impairment. Moreover,
because most publications in Japan and other Asian countries, including newspapers, books, and magazines, are
printed with the text reading vertically, questionnaires developed in Western countries may not be appropriate for

Japanese patients. No questionnaires assessing visual disability in patients who read and write vertically have been

Although visual disability varies widely depending on
the location of the visual field impairment despite similar
remaining visual field areas,15 no report has compared the
relationship between visual field and visual disability on the
basis of the location of the visual field impairment in patients with visual field impairment.
We previously developed a preliminary questionnaire16
based on interviews with 120 glaucoma patients to assess
visual disability in Japanese patients who read and write
vertically.
After the evaluation of patients with visual field damage
who retain decent central visual acuity, we found that this
preliminary questionnaire was useful in evaluating the visual disability of patients with retinitis pigmentosa, a representative disease causing visual field impairment.17 We
were then able to exclude factors unrelated to visual impairment by analysis of principal components and determination of factors contributing to disease. The resulting revised questionnaire was found both to be internally
consistent and reliable.
Using the revised questionnaire, this survey sought to
evaluate the regions of the central visual field most important for quality of vision in patients with glaucoma.

Subjects and Methods
Subjects
We evaluated visual disability in 147 patients (75 male and 72
female) with glaucoma (69 patients with primary open-angle glaucoma [POAG], 56 patients with normal tension glaucoma [NTG],
and 22 patients with primary angle-closure glaucoma). All patients
involved in the study fulfilled the following criteria: (1) glaucoma
was the only disease leading to visual field damage and/or visual
acuity impairment; (2) patients were followed for more than 6
months at either Tokyo Teishin Hospital or University of Tokyo
Hospital; (3) visual field was evaluated using program 30-2 of the
Humphrey Field Analyzer (HFA 30-2; Carl Zeiss, Dublin, CA)
with reliable (fixation loss ⱕ 33%, false-negative or false-positive
ⱕ 33%) results; (4) patients lacked other physical impairments;
and (5) visual field damage was clinically stable with medically or
surgically controlled intraocular pressure for at least 2 years.
Clinical diagnosis of NTG was made in open-angle glaucoma
patients with a recorded intraocular pressure consistently at approximately 21 mmHg with 24-hour fluctuations.
The mean (⫾standard deviation) age of patients was 61.5 ⫾
11.9 years, ranging from 25 to 85 years. The mean of the best corrected visual acuity in the logarithm of the minimum angle of
resolution was 0.3 ⫾ 0.5 (range, ⫺0.2 to 2.0) in the eye with better
visual acuity (better eye), 0.9 ⫾ 0.8 (range, ⫺0.2 to 2.0) in the eye
with worse visual acuity (worse eye). The mean deviation (MD) of
HFA 30-2 was ⫺11.2 ⫾ 5.9 (range, ⫺31.0 to ⫹2.9) dB in the
better eye (better MD) and ⫺17.4 ⫾ 6.5 (range, ⫺32.2 to ⫺0.1)
dB in the worse eye (worse MD). Sixty-four patients worked
regularly, whereas 83 patients were currently out of work. No
significant difference was seen between either the male and female
groups or the working and nonworking groups in HFA 30-2
measurements.

Materials and Methods
Relationship between Visual Disability Assessed
by the Revised Questionnaire and Visual Acuity
Visual disability in the 147 glaucoma patients was assessed using
the revised questionnaire. The questionnaire, originally written in
Japanese, contained 30 questions addressing 7 items: legibility of
letters (letters), sentences, walking, using public transportation
(going out), dining, dressing, and additional miscellaneous activities (miscellaneous) (Table 1, excluding the questions, written
with italics). Each question had three possible responses, scored as
follows, greatly disabled, 2 points; slightly disabled, 1 point; and
not disabled, 0 point. We calculated the visual disability index (DI)
for each item (item DI: the mean score given by the questions
within each item) and their sum (total DI) for each patient.
We first assessed the relationship between patients’ visual
disability, quantified by the revised questionnaire, and visual acuity. Because visual acuity differed from eye to eye, we compared
the relationship between patients’ visual disability and two representative visual acuity measurements: visual acuity in the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution in the better eye and in
the worse eye. We then calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the above individual visual acuity measurements
and both the item and total DIs. Multiple regression analysis was
also applied using total DI as a dependent variable and the two
representative visual acuity measurements as independent variables.

Relationship between Visual Disability Assessed
by the Revised Questionnaire and Remaining
Visual Field
Within 1 month of visual disability assessment, we tested the
monocular central 30° visual field using HFA 30-2 in both eyes
with a 5-minute rest between each eye examination. Next, we
studied the relationship between the patient’s visual disability,
quantified by the revised questionnaire, and the remaining visual
field. To determine the regions within the central 30° visual field
correlated with visual disability indices, the central 30° visual field
was divided into the following subfields: the visual field within 10°
of the fixation (VF10), the visual field between 11° and 20° of the
fixation (VF11–-20), the visual field between 21° and 30° of the
fixation (VF21–-30), and the entire central 30° visual field (VF30).
Test points within the central 10° of the fixation were further
divided into six clusters (Fig 1 A–F; VFA ⫺ VFF). The average
sensitivities (dB) in the preceding subfields or clustered test points
in the binocular visual field were calculated using two independent
methods. First, the most sensitive point determined from the right
and left corresponding visual field test point was selected and
designated as the sensitivity of each corresponding visual field test
point (HFA 30-2). These values were further averaged over the
subfield or test point cluster (the highest sensitivity). Second, the
least sensitive point from the right and left corresponding visual
field test point was selected and adopted as the sensitivity of each
corresponding visual field test point. These values were then
averaged over the subfield or test point cluster (the lowest sensitivity). The better MD and worse MD of HFA 30-2 were used as
visual field measurements representing overall visual field loss.
We also calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficients for the above
22 individual visual field measurements and item and total DIs.
Multiple regression analysis was also applied using total DI as a
dependent variable and the representative visual field measurements as independent variables.

Results
Relationship between Visual Disability Assessed
by the Revised Questionnaire and Visual Acuity
Visual acuity in the better and worse eye correlated significantly
with all the disability indices (r ⫽ 0.46 to 0.74) (Table 2). Visual
acuity in the better eye demonstrated the highest correlation with
the item and total DIs (r ⫽ 0.63 to 0.74).
Using stepwise variable selection, the visual acuities in both the
better and worse eyes were found to be significant predictors of
total DI (P ⬍ 0.0001, P ⬍ 0.001). The standardized partial
regression coefficients for visual acuity in the better and worse eyes were
0.65 and 0.21, respectively. The R value determined between the predicted and actual scores of total DI was 0.78 (P ⬍ 0.0001).

Relationship between Visual Disability Assessed
by the Revised Questionnaire and Remaining
Visual Field
Regardless of the selection of either highest or lowest sensitivity in
the calculation of VF10, VF11–20, VF21–30, VF30, VFA, VFB, VFC,
VFD, VFE, and VFF, all these visual field measurements exhibited

significant correlations with the DIs (r ⫽ ⫺0.70 to ⫺0.30) (Table
3). The better and worse MDs also demonstrated significant correlations with the DIs (r ⫽ ⫺0.64 to ⫺0.44). Of these, the highest

miscellaneous. Driving, however, an important section in
questionnaires developed in Western countries, was not
included in this questionnaire. None of the patients involved
in this study complained of any inconvenience in driving. In
Japan, a driving license is not issued to a person with a
best-corrected visual acuity less than 0.7, meaning that
many of the examined subjects cannot drive by themselves.
Furthermore, in big Japanese cities such as Tokyo, many do
not or need not drive by themselves.
Because no published reliable questionnaire has been
developed to assess visual disability in patients with visual
field damage who read and write vertically, our revised
questionnaire may also be of clinical use in Taiwan and
China, where people read and write vertically.
By use of the revised questionnaire, we evaluated the
relationship between visual disability and the visual field
assessed using program HFA 30-2 and visual acuity in 147
patients at various stages of advanced glaucoma.
Among the 22 visual field measurements (VF10, VF11–20,
VF21–30, VF30, VFA, VFB, VFC, VFD, VFE, and VFF of the
highest and lowest sensitivities and better and worse MDs)
and two visual acuity measurements (visual acuity in the
better eye and visual acuity in the worse eye) made, multiple
regression analysis with stepwise variable selection determined that only the highest sensitivity VFE and visual acuity
in the better eye demonstrated significant contribution to all
the item and total DIs measurements of the degree of visual
disability as evaluated by our questionnaire. Visual acuity in
the worse eye exhibited a significant contribution to two of
the seven item DIs, as well as the total DI. Crabb et al18
reported that use of the highest sensitivity from either eye
for each corresponding test location simulated the binocular
visual field sensitivity of glaucoma patients well. NelsonQuigg et al19 also reported that either the Best Location
model (in which binocular visual field sensitivity is predicted to be the most sensitive of two visual field locations
between eyes for corresponding visual field points) or the
Binocular Summation model (in which binocular visual
field sensitivity is predicted by probability summation of the
two eyes’ sensitivities) provided the best estimates for binocular visual field sensitivity. Thus, although binocular vi-

sual field was not examined in this study, the present visual
field measurements of the highest sensitivity estimates with
reasonable accuracy the binocular visual field of patients
most essential in patients’ daily lives. The high R values for
the regression, 0.87 for total DI and between 0.72 and 0.84
for item DIs, indicate that visual disability can be explained
by the combination of the previous three parameters, primarily the first two. Thus, maintenance of both the visual
acuity and the visual field in the lower hemifield within 5°
of the fixation are important for quality of vision in Japanese
glaucoma patients.
Mills and Drance12 compared the relationship between
visual disability, as assessed by their questionnaire, and
both visual acuity and visual field, as determined by the
Esterman visual field score15 in patients with severe glaucoma; they reported that visual disability correlated more
strongly with visual field than with visual acuity. Parrish et
al20 and Gutierrez et al,21 however, reported that quality of
vision in glaucoma patients, assessed by existing questionnaires,9,11 correlated with both visual acuity and remaining
visual field as assessed by either Esterman visual field
score15 or advanced glaucoma intervention study score.22
Because these studies used different subjects and methods
to assess visual disability and visual field damage, it is
difficult to compare the results directly. Our results, however, demonstrating a significant correlation between visual
disability and both visual acuity and visual field in the lower
hemifield within 5° of the fixation is compatible with the
latter report.20,21
The Esterman visual field score was proposed as a mechanism to assess the functional visual field. The assessment
design is based on the theory that the central part of the field
is functionally more important than the periphery, with the
lower hemifield being more used than the upper.15 The
lower central field is more important for quality of vision
than any other regions evaluated in the Esterman visual field
score. Our present result with Japanese glaucoma patients
agrees with this theory, indicating that the lower central
visual field is also crucial for quality of vision for those who
write and read vertically. In addition, no previous report has
compared the relationship between visual disability and the

mean retinal sensitivity within subdivided regions of the
central visual field. This study demonstrated that the lower
hemifield within 5° of fixation is important for quality of
vision in glaucoma patients. It remains possible that the
importance of this region in our study results from the
selection of subjects, Japanese glaucoma patients who read
and write vertically. If a similar investigation would be
performed in a Western nation, other regions within the
central 30° visual field may be more crucial for good quality
vision.
In open-angle glaucoma, the lower central field often
partially remains until the very late stages of the disease,23
partially explaining why some glaucoma patients do not
consult an ophthalmologist until late in the disease. Late in
disease, the lower central field is more depressed in POAG
eyes than in NTG eyes.24 In addition, glaucomatous visual
field damage tends to be more severe within the lower
central field in myopic patients.25 According to our results,
a more aggressive treatment of Japanese POAG patients
with myopia is needed in earlier stages of disease before

Results

Appendix
We have summarized the methods by which we revised
the preliminary questionnaire and assessed the internal consistency and reliability of the revised questionnaire below.

Subjects
One hundred two patients with glaucoma (48 patients
with POAG, 42 patients with NTG, and 12 patients with
primary angle-closure glaucoma) were examined. The mean
age of patients was 62.5 ⫾ 12.7 years. The mean best corrected visual acuity by the logarithm of the minimum
angle of resolution was 0.2 ⫾ 0.4 in the better eye and 0.8
⫾ 0.8 in the worse eye. The MD of HFA 30-2 was ⫺11.9
⫾ 5.9 dB in the eye with better MD and ⫺16.8 ⫾ 6.5 dB in
the eye with worse MD.

Methods
Visual disability in 102 patients was assessed using a
preliminary questionnaire originally written in Japanese,
consisting of a total of 35 questions addressing 7 items
(Table 1).
Analysis of the principal components was used to revise
the preliminary questionnaire to exclude factors other than
visual disability.
The internal consistency of the revised questionnaire was
evaluated by calculating the item-to-total correlation and
Cronbach’s ␣ for each item addressed in the revised questionnaire.
Using the revised questionnaire, visual disability was
assessed twice at intervals of 1 month; the test–retest reproducibility of the revised questionnaire was evaluated by
using the ␬ coefficient. Reliability of the revised questionnaire was examined by calculating the mean and standard
deviation of the differences in total DI from the first (total
DI-1) and second (total DI-2) assessments. A power analysis was also performed.

338

The eigenvalues for principal components 1, 2, and 3
were 19.3, 2.4, and 1.6, with proportions of 0.6, 0.07, and
0.05, respectively. Because only principal component 1
demonstrated a high eigenvalue and a high proportion
among these components, we suggest the existence of single
principal component. All the questions included in the preliminary questionnaire exhibited high principal component
weight for principal component 1 (0.54 to 0.85 for principal
component 1, ⫺0.30 to 0.57 for principal component 2,
⫺0.34 to 0.38 for principal component 3). Five questions
(written in italics in Table 1), however, also demonstrated a
high principal component weight for principal component 2
(0.51 to 0.55), suggesting the possibility of including a
factor other than visual disability. These five questions were
excluded from the preliminary questionnaire.
In the revised questionnaire, the item-to-total correlation
ranged from 0.81 to 0.91, whereas Cronbach’s ␣ varied
between 0.85 and 0.88.
The ␬ coefficient was 1.0 (standard error, 0.09 to 0.13)
for 16 questions and 0.9 (standard error, 0.10 to 0.13) for 14
questions. The mean score of total DI-1 and total DI-2 were
4.05 ⫾ 3.84 and 4.06 ⫾ 3.85, respectively. The difference
between total DI-1 and total DI-2 was ⫺0.01 ⫾ 0.09 (range,
⫺0.18 to 0.17) (Fig 3).26 The sample size of 100 patients
should allow the detection of a 1.54 difference in total DI-1
with ␣ ⫽ 0.05 and ␤ ⫽ 0.20.27

Conclusion
The internal consistency and reliability of the revised
questionnaire were found to be satisfactory.
