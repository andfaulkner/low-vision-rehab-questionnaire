Introduction
Over the last decades, Cerebral Visual Impairment (CVI) has become a major issue in
children with neurodisabilities.1 Since its description in the 1980’s, the substantive definition
of CVI has not undergone many changes but there remain many controversies.2, 3 Ongoing
issues have been raised and are yet to be resolved. First of all, the definitions available are

CR
IP
T

variable and broad and may encompass many subtypes of basic and higher vision including
differing degrees of acuity loss and visual perception deficits. On the other hand, the
definitions available are not always clear enough so that conditions affecting the eye itself
might or might not be included. Lastly, there is a difference in terminology between the US

AN
US

and the European countries with a favor in the latter for the term “Cerebral”. As a significant
proportion of children presenting with CVI have periventricular white matter disease,
European Centers prefer the term ”cerebral” to take into account cortical, sub-cortical, and
white matter structures.4, 5 A recent systematic review investigating the different definitions of
CVI found fifty one papers that met inclusion criteria. The most commonly appearing themes

M

were ‘visual impairment’, ‘retro-chiasmatic pathway damage’ and ‘normal/near normal eye

ED

health’. The most consensual definitions identified in this review may not be the most
empirically useful definition for describing childhood CVI and the authors proposed that a

PT

way forward was to use the definition of CVI as ‘a verifiable visual dysfunction which cannot
be attributed to disorders of the anterior visual pathways or any potentially co-occurring

CE

ocular impairment’.’ This would be sufficiently broad and inclusive of primary cerebral or
posterior visual pathway origins of visual dysfunction, whilst also requiring a systematic and

AC

explicit means of ‘verifying’ the disorder through appropriate clinical assessment methods.2

Whereas the definition of CVI is broad, the clinical spectrum is even broader and so is the
array of underlying conditions causing CVI, which can be acquired or congenital, occurring in
the pre-, peri-, or postnatal period.6–8 Many conditions have been associated with CVI, such
as cerebral malformations, hydrocephalus, central nervous system infections, epilepsy, head
trauma, metabolic and neurodegenerative diseases, or intoxication with drugs or poisons.9

3


[page 5]
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
However, cryptogenic cases have also been described.10 Still, the most frequent conditions
linked with CVI are periventricular white matter disease, typically occurring in preterm infants,
and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy around birth, both of which can lead to cerebral palsy
(CP). In children with CP, up to 70 % have basic and higher visual dysfunction including
visual perceptual problems.11,12 The first publications on visual dysfunction in CP described

CR
IP
T

the children as having object recognition difficulties.13,14 However, recent studies have shown
that children with CP also suffer from visuospatial deficits impacting on their locomotion.15

As severe visual impairment of any etiology has an important impact on other developmental
domains including cognition, early diagnosis is of utmost importance to prevent cumulative

AN
US

cognitive, motor, socio-emotional, and educational deficits.16 The greatest delays are found in
infants and young children with profound vision loss and therefore early infancy vision

M

promotion is recommended for those with early severe vision reduction.17

An ophthalmological, neuropediatric, developmental, vision and visuomotor assessment is

ED

required, in addition to interviewing the parent and other professionals involved in the child’s
medical and educational care. This assessment will underpin the identification and diagnosis

PT

of whether the child has CVI or not, identify the child’s strengths and needs and provide
important information for the child’s care, habilitation and education plan. As CVI has so

CE

many aspects, habilitation will have to be multidimensional and individually tailored.

AC

An optimal habilitation plan thus needs a thorough systematic, multidimensional, and
multidisciplinary assessment. The importance of a systematic means of screening for and
assessing CVI has been emphasized in the recent systematic review.2 A number of different
European pediatric centers, including London (UK), Brescia (Italy), and Leuven (Belgium),
have been developing multidisciplinary methods of assessment in the clinic and research
context drawing on ophthalmology, neurology and neurodisability models of care. In the
following sections of the paper we consider our assessment models in the context of clinical

Need for early identification
Early identification of CVI is critical because it can lead firstly to early detection of infants at
risk of brain damage and, secondly, to implement intervention in order to gauge plasticity and

AN
US

recovery from perinatal brain damage early in life through measured changes over time.18

Developmental trajectories and outcomes of CVI

An important issue to consider in the screening and/or assessment of CVI in children is the
developing brain and related developmental trajectories in typically developing children.

M

Along with the maturation of lower visual functions (with visual acuity reaching an adult level
between three and five years of age), various higher level visual functions complete their

ED

development at differing and specific ages. For example, already from the age of six weeks,
recognition of faces is based solely on the internal configuration of the face.19 At 3 months of

PT

age, infants show preferential attention for upright human movement, which is crucial for the
ability to recognize people and to make social contacts.20 Meanwhile, within the first 16

CE

months, form- and motion-processing abilities start to develop but will not be completed
before the age of 4 years.21 Many higher vision abilities continue to develop throughout

AC

childhood, such as

complex form-ground discrimination and visual attention, which

continues to mature until school age.22 These higher vision abilities develop alongside other
cognitive abilities, with higher visual perceptual abilities developing in accordance with brain
maturation and developmental age.23

So, in contrast to adults where a lesion in the brain almost certainly leads to permanent
deficits, in children there is hope for improving outcomes and even recovery in some cases

as injured or compensatory neural networks develop over time.24, 25 For example, whereas in
the acute phase following a traumatic event, children often seem to be cortically blind,
functional improvement/recovery has been reported after variable time periods.26

However, in most patients with CVI normal visual function will not be regained, as the vast

Value of neuroimaging

ED

Areas of brain damage may be focal or widespread and variable and the MRI brain scan is
not of diagnostic value in itself. A correlation between neurovisual and neuroradiological

PT

findings is not always present. In fact, a normal MRI does not exclude the presence of CVI. 5

CE

Many studies have reported an association between visuo-spatial dysfunction and occipitoparietal periventricular white matter lesions but no significant association between visuo-

AC

is some agreement among investigators that the lack of correlation between higher visual
skills and observed morphological brain anomalies may be explained by the fact that
conventional MRI techniques do not reveal macro-structural injuries within the visual
pathways.5, 30 In this direction, the development of new neuroimaging advanced techniques
such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) has proven to be highly useful not only as a method to

Heterogeneity of the clinical picture
Children with CVI present a variable symptom complex. For example, children can exhibit

CR
IP
T

severe CVI impairment with complete loss of visual awareness, whereas children with milder
CVI may only exhibit visual perceptual problems like the inability to recognize faces or to
visually identify objects.22 This depends on the timing, location and extent of the causative
lesion itself. Ortibus et al. have shown that more extensive white matter damage is

AN
US

associated with more important object recognition difficulties, coined by Dutton as ‘dorsal stream dysfunction plus’.5, 34 In more recent work, several groups have shown that global
lesion extent and specific thalamus involvement, measured by a semi-quantitative scale,

M

define the clinical picture. 35, 36

ED

Traditionally, clinical symptoms of CVI are categorized according to the proposed underlying
neuroanatomical distribution of the visual functions as first described in adults. 37 As such,

PT

some symptoms could be related to primary visual pathways, some symptoms have been
more related to putative ventral stream deficits and other to dysfunctions of the dorsal

CE

stream. For an overview of clinical symptoms, we refer to excellent overviews such as those
of Dutton et al. and Fazzi.30, 38 Typically not all symptoms occur at the same time and they

AC

can evolve over time.9,39 This could be due to recovery and plasticity mechanisms. In
addition, clinical experience teaches that the child may also acquire compensation strategies
for the visual deficits. A classic example is the dependence on color for recognizing objects
or finding pictures on communication charts by remembering their location. Lastly, as
described in congenitally blind people, the other senses may become more superior and
non-visual perceptual processing may actively recruit the occipital areas.40

Multidisciplinary approach for CVI: the European model
A first crucial step in early identification of CVI is consideration of the child’s pediatric medical
history, including prenatal, neonatal and early post-natal history, and later traumatic
episodes, which will alert one to the risk of congenital or later CVI. The majority of tools used
to identify CVI were not originally developed to diagnose this condition. Both clinically and in

CR
IP
T

research, groups of assessments are often used together to address different parts of the
diagnostic process, which will draw on different disciplines including pediatric ophthalmology,
optometry and orthoptics, developmental pediatrics, neuroimaging, visual electrophysiology,
clinical neuropsychology, and occupational therapy. These may also be supplemented by

AN
US

education. Individually, such assessments are unlikely to reach sufficient specificity or
sensitivity to accurately differentiate between CVI and other conditions. However, when
taken together and combined into a final integrated formulation, the multidisciplinary team
and disciplines using a combination of assessment methods may successfully identify CVI in

M

the childhood population at different ages and levels of vision and cognitive ability.41 This
combined approach is however only found in a few pediatric centers, such as the

ED

Developmental Vision Clinic of Great Ormond Street Hospital, London (UK), the CVI clinic in
Leuven (Belgium) and the Center of Child Neurophthalmology at the Unit of Child Neurology

PT

and Psychiatry, ASST Spedali Civili of Brescia (Italy), where a multidisciplinary pediatric

CE

neurodisability/developmental vision clinic team works alongside pediatric ophthalmology.

Screening questionnaires
Several questionnaires have been developed for screening purposes, to obtain an initial idea
of whether children are at risk for CVI, depending on their clinical characteristics.42–46 Some
of them have been developed for specific age ranges, others for specific risk groups such as
children with CP or for a specific geographical population. Although their clinical screening
value is recognized, not many of these questionnaires have been studied for reliability or
validity. Studies of the Preverbal Visual Assessment (PreVias) for example show the capacity
of the questionnaire to differentiate typically developing children from those with slower

8


[page 10]
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
developing visual abilities.47 However, it is not possible to specifically identify the children
with CVI from other conditions associated with slower maturing of vision abilities, for example
as in prematurity. Ortibus et al. have validated their questionnaire in a sample of children
seen at a CVI clinic and tested with an object recognition battery. Although relatively good
sensitivity and specificity of object recognition could be found based on 7 specific items, this

CR
IP
T

set of questions could not reliably give information on whether a child is also at risk for visuomotor or visuo-spatial difficulties.42 Moreover, some of the items are not suitable for children
with a motor disability, which is why Salavati and colleagues developed a questionnaire
tailored to this population. Validity studies were performed, however, the criteria for CVI

AN
US

diagnosis in their population were not so clear.43 Philip et al. meanwhile rephrased some of
the questions of an inventory developed by the group of Dutton, so as to fit the specific
conditions of India.46 Taken together, no one questionnaire seems reliable and valid enough
at the moment to initially detect or screen children at risk for CVI and a combination of

M

questionnaires and/or addition of information from parents and other caregivers and pediatric
clinicians involved with the child is likely to be necessary. Further research and development

ED

Cognitive/developmental assessment

CE

Before progressing to assess higher visual perceptual abilities, it is necessary to evaluate the
child’s general cognitive and adaptive behavioral levels using standard assessment tools. If

AC

the child has very low visual acuity then it is advisable to use the only available semistandardized scales for children with visual impairment (the Reynell Zinkin Scales for young
children with visual handicap) for under 5s or the verbal subscales of cognitive schedules for
school aged children so that the cognitive assessment is not compromised by vision
reduction.48 Children with better vision can be assessed on infancy and childhood
developmental assessment scales that include verbal and non-verbal scales. This will

Assessment of vision deficits

Children with CVI present with specific difficulties in visual behavior or function. Although

M

they have a normal or quite normal eye examination, they show poor visual reactions and
poor visual attention. The presence of visual latency, a limited visual attention span and

ED

extremely shortened gaze behavior often result in misjudgment of these children as
inattentive, incapable or poorly motivated.49, 50 Other important features of CVI are

PT

oculomotor disorders such as nystagmus, unstable fixation, discontinuous smooth pursuit,
and/or abnormal saccades. Also, they may show reduced visual acuity, poor depth

CE

perception and visual field restriction.51 This could explain their characteristic head turn when
looking at or reaching for an object. In addition, they tend to use peripheral vision to look at

AC

an object and appear not to use their central vision.38 Also, children with CVI often lean
forward to bring their eyes close to an object to reduce the ‘crowding’ effect or to improve
resolution. This way, the amount of ‘noise’ in the background is reduced. However, this
phenomenon is often misinterpreted as task rejection or as rebellious misbehavior. 52
Paradoxically, in nearly one-third of the children photophobia (intolerance of light) is present,
however gazing at lights is very characteristic, particularly in those with additional complex
disability.6 Although perception of movement and color are frequently preserved in children

10

Typically, they are better off with higher contrasts and they do have problems with perception
of motion coherence as shown in many studies in preterm infants.21, 53 Vision for figure ground discrimination and crowding or simultanagnosia appears to be particularly

Neuropsychological assessment of visual perception

The value of a standardized neuropsychological assessment of visual perception abilities is
that it permits comparison with the test population norms (in the test manual), or reported
typically developing community populations. This leads to identifying whether the child is in
line with or behind normative (developmental) age expectations. Also, longitudinal follow up

can demonstrate changes in the individual child’s trajectories. Based on available tests,
evaluation can be successfully performed in children from ages of 2-5 years to adulthood.

Specific instruments have been developed for specific visual functions, such as the line
bisection task to detect hemi-neglect or the embedded figure test to specifically test figure-

manipulation, grasping and visual motor integration and measuring interrelated motor
abilities.81 The Movement Assessment Battery for Children (Movement ABC-2) is now

Visual function and functional vision

Assessing visual function describes how the child’s eyes and his or her basic and higher
visual systems function. It sheds light on the ability to process visual stimuli of a particular
dimension, e.g. form, colour or motion.1 Functional vision on the other hand describes how a
person functions with his or her visual abilities and, more importantly, looks at how vision
determines function in everyday life.88 As such, it is important to consider the impact of the

CR
IP
T

child’s vision on communication and social relations, orientation and mobility, daily life
activities and for sustained near vision tasks. Communication in daily life not only happens
between person to person but also in busy environments or from a greater distance. For
instance, difficulties with crowding will particularly impact the child’s ability to find their parent

AN
US

or caregiver or friends in a busy environment, affecting their social relating at school. The
final consideration is how to assess the needs of a child with a multiple disability, in whom
standardized assessment may not be feasible. In this group, we rely considerably on history taking and information gathering from parents and educational and health professionals who
have had a sustained relationship with the child. In these cases, a detailed observation of the

M

child’s vision, general play and social interactions in the clinic or everyday environment, is

ED

crucial, using standardised observational frameworks where possible. Observation in optimal
and less optimal conditions and videotaping, will enable giving detailed feedback to parents,

PT

focussing on the family’s and educator’s concerns.

Comorbidities in children with CVI
As most of the children with CVI have a brain lesion, comorbidities are markedly present. In

AC

addition to the well-known association between CP and CVI, many children present with
uneven cognitive or learning profiles, intellectual disability, attentional difficulties/ ADHD and
also autistic spectrum disorder (ASD) and characteristics of developmental coordination
disorder (DCD).87 There is ongoing research interest in whether this raised tendency is a
