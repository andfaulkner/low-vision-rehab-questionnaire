Long-term exposure to fine particulate matter
and its constituents is related to self-reported
visual impairment among middle-aged
and older adults: a longitudinal study in China
Songtao Wang1†, Xueying Li2†, Xiufen Liu1, Yibo Wang1, Dandan Zhou3* and Chengwei Lu1*
Abstract
Background Visual impairment (VI) poses an increasing global health burden, yet the long-term effects of fine
particulate matter (PM2.5) constituents on VI remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate associations between long term exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents and the risk of VI.
Methods We used data from the nationally representative China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS,
2011–2018) including adults aged ≥ 45 years. Long-term exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents (SO42–, NO3–, NH4+,
organic matter (OM), and black carbon (BC)) was obtained from the Tracking Air Pollution in China dataset and linked
to participants’ residential cities. The 3-year moving average concentration was used as the primary exposure metric
to capture cumulative effects, while the 1-year average concentration was applied for sensitivity analysis. VI was
defined as self-reported fair or poor vision. Associations were estimated using time-dependent Cox models, with
restricted cubic spline (RCS), stratified and interaction analyses to assess nonlinear and subgroup effects.
Results Over 27,443 person-years of follow-up, 3,736 participants developed VI. Per interquartile range (IQR) increase
in 3-year moving average concentrations, higher risks of VI were observed for PM2.5 (HR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.03–1.15),
SO42– (HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.05–1.22), NO3– (HR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.13), NH4+ (HR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.17), OM
(HR = 95% CI: 1.15, 1.07–1.24), and BC (HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.14–1.34). RCS analysis revealed non-linear associations
for PM2.5, NO3–, NH4+, and OM, whereas SO42– and BC showed approximately linear trends. Associations were more
pronounced among older adults (> 60 years old), smokers, and those with hypertension.
Conclusion Long-term exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents significantly increases the risk of VI among middle aged and older adults in China, highlighting the need for targeted environmental health policies.
Keywords Black carbon, Fine particulate matter, Time-dependent Cox models, Visual impairment

Introduction
Visual impairment (VI) has emerged as a pressing global
public health issue, imposing considerable social and
health burdens worldwide. According to data from the
Global Burden of Disease (GBD) studies, the number of
individuals affected by moderate and severe VI increased
by 133.67% and 147.14%, respectively, between 1990 and
2019. Projection indicated that by 2050, approximately
474 million people worldwide will experience moderate to-severe VI [1, 2]. The burden of VI extends beyond
visual loss itself, encompassing a wide range of physical,
psychological, and socioeconomic consequences that
substantially affect quality of life and overall well-being.
Individuals with VI typically engage in less physical
activity—a key determinant of overall health and longevity—and are at higher risk of falls due to impaired mobility and daily functioning [3, 4]. Moreover, VI is closely
linked to social isolation, cognitive decline, depression,
and anxiety [5–8]. Importantly, VI may not only result
from ocular diseases but also from environmental exposures, notably air pollution.
Accumulating evidence suggests that air pollution
exposure contributes to the development of various ocular diseases. Previous studies have demonstrated that
exposure to ambient particulate matter (PM) can induce
ocular surface disorders, including conjunctivitis, keratitis, and dry eye disease [9–11]. Of particular concern is
fine particulate matter (PM2.5), characterized by its small
aerodynamic diameter and large surface area. Recent
studies have associated PM2.5 exposure with cataract,
glaucoma, age-related macular degeneration (AMD),
and retinal or choroidal injury [12–15]. Mechanistically,
PM2.5 may impair ocular tissues through neurotoxicity,
cellular pyroptosis, and disruption of retinal pigment epithelial tight junctions, thereby predisposing individuals
to glaucoma, optic atrophy, and other retinal diseases [12,
16, 17].
Despite these findings, evidence linking PM2.5 exposure
to VI remains limited. Moreover, the chemical constituents of PM2.5—such as sulfates (SO42−), nitrates (NO3–),
ammonium (NH4+), organic matter (OM), and black
carbon (BC)—vary substantially in biological toxicity
and may differentially affect ocular health. While prior
research has explored associations between PM2.5 and VI
in children, dose–response patterns, and vulnerable subpopulations among adults remain poorly characterized.
This study therefore aims to comprehensively investigate
the associations between long-term exposure to PM2.5
and its constituents and the risk of VI.
Methods
Study design and population

This prospective cohort study utilized data from the
China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study

Page 2 of 10

(CHARLS), a nationally representative survey of adults
aged 45 years and older. CHARLS employed a multistage probability sampling strategy covering both urban
and rural communities across 28 provinces in mainland
China [18]. The CHARLS was initiated in 2011 and subsequently followed up in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The
CHARLS was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review
Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052–11015),
and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Due to the lack of VI information in 2020, this
study collected extensive sociodemographic, behavioral,
and health information at baseline (2011) and during
three follow-up waves (2013, 2015, and 2018).
Participants were included if they: (1) were
aged ≥ 45 years at baseline; (2) had VI information; and
(3) completed at least one follow-up interview. Participants with missing PM2.5 exposure estimates, missing
key covariates (age), missing baseline VI information, or
prevalent VI at baseline were excluded to ensure clear
temporal ordering between exposure and outcome. After
applying inclusion criteria, 11,831 participants were
included, contributing 27,443 person-years of follow-up
(Fig. 1).
Exposure assessment

1-year and 3-year moving average concentrations of
PM2.5 and its constituents (SO42−, NO3–, NH4+, OM, and
BC) were obtained from the Tracking Air Pollution in
China (TAP) dataset (http://tapdata.org) [19, 20].
TAP integrates multiple data sources, including satellite remote sensing, ground-based monitoring, meteorological reanalysis, and land-use information, and applies
ensemble machine learning algorithms (random forest
and deep neural networks) to generate 1 km × 1 km gridded annual average estimates of pollutant concentrations across China. We subsequently converted the grid
coordinates in the TAP dataset into actual residential
addresses and calculated the annual average pollutant
concentrations at city level.
Residential information in CHARLS was available
only at the city level to protect participant privacy. For
each participant, the 3-year moving average concentration, calculated as the mean of the current year and the
two preceding years (e.g., 2009–2011 for the 2011–2013
interval), was used as the primary exposure metric. The
3-year moving average concentration smoothed interannual fluctuations, reflects cumulative exposure burden,
and better captures the biological latency of chronic ocular effects.
To assess robustness, 1-year average concentrations
before the follow-up interval (e.g., 2010–2011 for the
2011–2013 interval) were also analyzed. Overall, pollutant data encompassed 28 provinces and 125 cities across
China.

Outcome ascertainment

The outcome was VI, determined from self-reported
vision assessments in CHARLS. Participants were asked
to rate their distance and near vision as “excellent”, “very
good”, “good”, “fair”, or “poor”. Consistent with previous
studies, responses of “fair” or “poor” were classified as VI
[21–23]. The event time was defined as the wave in which
a participant first self-reported VI or lost follow-up.
Covariates

Covariates were selected based on the previous studies
[13, 24, 25]. Covariates included age, sex, marital status
(married or other), residence (urban or rural), education
level (less than primary, primary, middle, or high school
and above), personal annual expenditure (CNY) (0–2,915,
2,916–4,807, 4,808–7,343, 7,344–12,108, ≥ 12,109), medical insurance (yes or no), smoking (yes or no), drinking (yes or no), hypertension(yes or no), diabetes(yes or
no), dyslipidemia(yes or no), heart disease(yes or no),
stroke(yes or no), body mass index (BMI, kg/m^2), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP, mmHg),
total cholesterol (TC, mg/dL), triglycerides (TG, mg/
dL), random blood glucose (GLU, mg/dL), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c, %). Additionally, province of

residence, as a fixed effect to account for regional variations, was incorporated.
Statistical analysis

This study used multiple imputation to handle missing
data. Baseline characteristics were compared between
participants with and without VI. Continuous variables
were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges
(IQRs) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum or
Kruskal–Wallis tests, while categorical variables were
expressed as counts (percentages) and compared using
χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. Associations between long term PM2.5 exposure and the risk of VI were evaluated
using time-dependent Cox models. Because PM2.5 and its
chemical constituents were highly correlated with each
other, single-pollutant models were constructed to avoid
multicollinearity (Table S1 in Supplementary material).
To ensure comparability across pollutants with different concentration ranges, hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated IQR increase in PM₂.₅ and each constituent. Three
sequential models were developed: Model 1 adjusted for
age, sex, marital status, residence, education level, smoking status, drinking status, and province of residence;
Model 2 further adjusted for hypertension, diabetes,

dyslipidemia, heart disease, stroke, personal annual
expenditure, and BMI; Model 3 additionally incorporated
SBP, DBP, TC, TG, GLU, and HbA1c. To examine nonlinear dose–response relationships, restricted cubic spline
(RCS) model with four knots was fitted. Stratified Cox
models were applied to assess subgroup-specific associations, and interaction analyses were conducted between
air pollutants and potential effect modifiers. All analyses
were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with
the “survival” package. Statistical significance was defined
as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Results
Baseline characteristics of participants

At baseline, 11,831 participants aged ≥ 45 years without
VI were included, among whom 3,736 (31.6%) reported
VI during follow-up (Table S2 in Supplementary Material). The median age of participants was 58 years old, and
54.2% were female. Participants who reported VI were
more likely to be reside in urban areas, and have a higher
educational level (P < 0.001). Participants with VI showed
lower prevalence of diabetes and hypertension, but were
more often current smokers and drinkers (P < 0.01). No
significant differences were observed in dyslipidemia
and medical insurance coverage. In addition, those who
developed VI had slightly higher BMI and HbA1c levels
(P < 0.001), while lipid factors and blood pressure were
similar between two groups. Regarding exposure levels,
participants with VI were exposed to slightly lower 1-year
average PM2.5 but higher 1-year average SO42− concentrations at baseline, with consistent patterns for 3-year moving averages (P < 0.05).
The relationship between PM2.5 and VI

In the time-dependent Cox models, higher 3-year moving average concentrations of PM2.5 and its constituents
were consistently associated with an increased risk of
VI (Table 1). For each interquartile range (IQR) increase
in PM2.5, the fully adjusted HR for VI was 1.09 (95%
CI: 1.03–1.15, P = 0.003). The associations were more

pronounced for PM2.5 constituents, particularly SO42−
(HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 1.05–1.22), NH4+ (HR = 1.09, 95%
CI: 1.02–1.17), and OM (HR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.07–1.24).
A modest but significant effect was also observed for
NO3– (HR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.00–1.13). Among all constituents, BC exhibited the strongest association with VI
(HR = 1.24, 95% CI: 1.14–1.34), highlighting its potential
role as a particularly toxic fraction of PM2.5.
RCS analysis of PM2.5 and its constituents with VI risk

RCS analysis revealed significant dose–response relationships between the 3-year moving average concentrations
of PM2.5 and its constituents and the risk of VI (Fig. 2).
The overall associations were statistically significant
for all pollutants (P for overall < 0.05). A nonlinear pattern was observed for PM2.5 (P for nonlinearity < 0.001),
NO3– (P = 0.004), NH4+ (P = 0.042), and OM (P < 0.001).
In contrast, the relationships for SO42– (P for nonlinearity = 0.204) and BC (P for nonlinearity = 0.253) appeared
approximately linear, with a gradual rise in risk across the
exposure range.
Categorical analysis of 3-year average PM2.5 and its
constituents and VI risk

To further validate the nonlinear exposure–response patterns indicated by the RCS analysis, pollutant concentrations were categorized into tertiles, and HRs for each
tertile were estimated using time-dependent Cox models
(Table 2). For most PM2.5 constituents, a nonlinear trend
was observed: compared with the lowest tertile (T1), the
second tertile (T2) generally showed slightly reduced or
nonsignificant risks, whereas the third tertile (T3) was
associated with elevated VI risk. The cutoff values for
tertiles were presented in Table S3 in Supplementary
Material.
Specifically, higher risks of VI were found in the top
tertile of SO42– (HR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08–1.48, P = 0.005),
NH4+ (HR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.11–1.53, P = 0.001), and BC
(HR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.16–1.55, P < 0.001), while the corresponding T2 levels were below unity but not statistically significant. These results support the nonlinear

Sensitivity analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, we used the 1-year average concentrations to evaluate the robustness of the associations.

The findings were largely consistent with the main analyses, supporting the stability of the results.
Per IQR increase, higher 1-year average concentration of PM2.5 (IQR = 19.83 µg/m3) were significantly
associated with a higher risk of VI (HR = 1.09, 95% CI
1.04–1.15). Similar associations were observed for its
constituents, including SO42– (4.50 µg/m3; HR = 1.12,
95% CI 1.05–1.20), NO3– (5.73 µg/m3; HR = 1.07, 95% CI
1.01–1.14), NH4+ (3.92 µg/m3; HR = 1.09, 95% CI 1.02–
1.17), OM (5.69 µg/m3; HR = 1.15, 95% CI 1.07–1.24), and
BC (1.07 µg/m3; HR = 1.21, 95% CI 1.12–1.30) (Table 4).
RCS analysis indicated significant nonlinear dose–
response relationships for PM2.5, SO42–, NO3–, and OM
(P for nonlinearity < 0.05). These pollutants showed an
initial flattening or mild decline in VI risk at lower concentrations, followed by a pronounced increase in risk at
higher exposure levels. In contrast, NH4+ and BC exhibited approximately linear dose–response patterns, with
risk rising steadily across the exposure range (Figure S1
in Supplementary Material).
Consistent with the RCS results, tertile analysis demonstrated that participants in the highest tertile had significantly greater risks of VI for PM2.5 (HR = 1.16, 95%
CI 1.02–1.32) and SO42– (HR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.05–1.44),
whereas associations for NO3–, NH4+, OM, and BC were
weaker and did not reach statistical significance (Table S6
in Supplementary Material).

Discussion
This study, based on a nationally representative longitudinal cohort and incorporating high-resolution air
pollution exposure data, systematically evaluated the
association between PM2.5 and its constituents––including SO42–, NO3–, NH4+, OM, and BC––and the risk of VI.
Using time-dependent Cox models, we found that higher
ambient PM2.5 concentrations were significantly associated with increased risk of VI. Notably, BC and OM
exhibited the strongest risk effects, with nonlinear exposure–response relationships. These associations were
particularly pronounced among older adults, urban residents, and individuals with lower educational attainment.
These results are consistent with those of previous
research. For instance, a large cross-sectional study found
that higher levels of PM2.5 and its constituents were associated with increased risk of VI in school-aged children,
with BC showing the most significant effect. The same
study reported nonlinear exposure–response relationships for SO42–, NO3–, NH4+, OM, and BC [25].

VI is a result of multiple ocular diseases, including
corneal disorders, cataracts, glaucoma, myopia, and
age-related macular degeneration. Numerous epidemiological and experimental studies have suggested links
between air pollution and these ocular conditions. For
example, Mokhtar et al. reported that long-term PM2.5
exposure was associated with decreased corneal nerve
fiber density, total nerve length, and fractal dimension,
indicating corneal neurodegeneration [26]. Additionally,
high PM2.5 exposure has been linked to corneal epithelial
defects, thinning of the epithelial layer, and limbal stem
cell loss [27]. Li et al. demonstrated that each 10 µg/m3
increase in PM2.5 concentration in the 1, 2, and 3 years
before follow-up was associated with HRs of 1.165, 1.138,
and 1.091, respectively, for age-related cataracts [13]. A
retrospective cohort study in Taiwan involving 97,306
children found that children in the highest quartile of
PM2.5 exposure (> 41.2 µg/m3) had significantly increased
risk of developing myopia compared to those in the lowest quartile (< 29.5 µg/m3, HR = 1.76) [28]. Additionally,
elevated PM2.5 levels were associated with an increased
risk of glaucoma, characterized by reduced macular
ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer thickness, with evidence of a dose–response relationship [16]. Animal studies further support these findings; acute PM2.5 exposure
induced choroidal vessel dilation and disruption of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) tight junctions, increasing
the risk of retinal edema. Prolonged exposure led to persistent choroidal inflammation and AMD-like degenerative changes in the retina [12].
This study also provides novel insights into the differential toxicity of specific PM2.5 constituents. Among
them, BC and OM demonstrated the most substantial
associations with VI. BC is one of the smallest and most
stable carbonaceous fractions of PM2.5, primarily emitted from combustion sources such as vehicle exhaust and
agricultural burning. Due to its ultrafine nature, BC can
penetrate the ocular surface and trigger local inflammatory responses. Experimental studies have shown that
BC exposure leads to corneal epithelial cell shedding,
inflammatory cell infiltration, and ocular surface microenvironmental imbalance [29]. At the cellular level, BC
induces apoptosis in corneal epithelial cells and activates
the NLRP3 inflammasome, resulting in increased expression of inflammatory mediators such as IL-1β, NLRP3,
caspase-1, and ASC [30]. Systemically, BC exposure may
activate mast cells and amplify oxidative stress and systemic inflammation [31]. A study incorporating human
genetic data found that the association between BC and
elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) was stronger among
individuals with high oxidative stress genetic risk scores,
suggesting potential gene–environment interactions [32].
OM is one of the most chemically complex PM2.5 fractions, comprising polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons

Page 8 of 10

and volatile organic compounds. Due to their lipophilic
nature, these compounds can penetrate the eye via the
cornea or sclera and elicit toxicological responses. Magnuson et al. demonstrated in a zebrafish model that PAH
exposure downregulated genes involved in ocular development and phototransduction, resulting in impaired
neural development, increased apoptosis, and reduced
optomotor responses [33]. Volatile organic compounds
are also known to cause eye irritation and are a common
cause of ocular symptoms in high-exposure environments [34].
In contrast to BC and OM, the inorganic secondary
aerosol constituents of PM2.5, SO42–, NO3–, and NH4+—
are formed from precursor gases such as sulfur dioxide,
nitrogen oxides, and ammonia through atmospheric
chemical reactions. Although these constituents are
less chemically reactive, animal studies have shown that
exposure to these ions increases malondialdehyde levels
and lipid peroxidation in the liver, suggesting elevated
systemic oxidative stress [35], which may contribute to
ocular inflammation.

Conclusion
This study evaluated the independent associations
between long-term exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents and the risk of VI in a nationally representative adult
cohort. The results identified BC, OM, and SO42– as the
constituents most strongly associated with visual risk,
providing important directions for future mechanistic
research.
Strength and limitation
This study examined the associations between long-term
exposure to PM2.5 and its constituents and the risk of VI
in a nationally representative cohort. It identified specific
components—particularly BC, OM, and SO42–—most
strongly linked to VI.
Several limitations should be noted. VI was self reported rather than clinically measured, which may
cause non-differential misclassification. Such random
error would generally bias the estimated associations
toward the null, leading to an underestimation of the
true effects. Residential information was available only at
the city level, and exposure differences from living near
major industrial areas or busy roads could not be captured. Because VI was assessed every 2–3 years, exact
onset times could not be determined, and some temporal
misalignment may remain.
Despite these limitations, our findings provide population-based evidence that long-term PM2.5 exposure and
its components may adversely affect visual health.
