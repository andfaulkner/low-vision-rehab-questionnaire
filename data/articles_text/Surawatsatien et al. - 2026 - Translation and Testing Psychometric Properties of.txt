•

We addressed the lack of validated Thai versions of the vision bolton EQ-5D by translating, culturally adapting, and psychometrically

Abstract
Objectives
This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric
properties of the Thai versions of the vision bolt-on EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L,
comparing them with the standard EQ-5D-3L, EQ-5D-5L, and EQ-VAS in individuals
with varying degrees of visual impairment.

Methods
Following EuroQol Translation Guidelines, the vision bolt-on items were translated
through forward-backward processes and underwent cognitive debriefing. A total of
301 participants were interviewed at baseline, with 275 completing a 2-week follow up. Known-groups validity was assessed based on visual acuity classifications and

Results
Both versions of the vision bolt-on had no missing data and demonstrated moderate
to good test-retest reliability (ICC range 0.62-0.64). They effectively differentiated
quality-of-life scores across varying degrees of vision impairment, showing reduced
ceiling effects (5%-9%) compared with the standard EQ-5D. Correlations with EQ-VAS
were moderate (rho 0.40-0.45). When participants reported improved vision, the
vision bolt-on EQ-5D-5L exhibited the highest responsiveness, whereas all
instruments were generally less responsive for worsening vision.

Conclusions
The Thai versions of the vision bolt-on EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L demonstrated
acceptable reliability, validity, and moderate responsiveness for capturing vision related health changes, with the 5L version showing slightly greater sensitivity. These
findings support the usefulness of adding a vision dimension to EQ-5D for Thai speaking populations, facilitating more precise evaluations of interventions targeting
visual health.

Introduction
Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) measurement is pivotal in evaluating
healthcare interventions, informing policy decisions, and understanding patient
outcomes in chronic and disabling conditions. The EQ-5D, developed by the EuroQol
Group, is a widely used generic instrument for assessing HRQoL because of its
brevity, cross-cultural applicability, and utility in economic evaluations.1 The original
EQ-5D-3L and its expanded counterpart, the EQ-5D-5L, which offers improved
sensitivity with 5 response levels per dimension, have been validated across diverse
populations.2,3 However, the standard EQ-5D’s 5 core dimensions—mobility, self-care,
usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression—may inadequately capture
the burden of specific health issues, such as visual impairment, which profoundly
impacts daily functioning and psychosocial well-being.4 This limitation is highlighted
in a recent multicenter Thai study on patients with eye diseases, which found that
the standard EQ-5D-5L showed a significant response only when visual impairment
became severe, failing to detect the impact of mild or moderate impairment.
Furthermore, the same study found that the Health Utility Index 3, an instrument
that includes a vision domain, demonstrated a stronger correlation with a vision specific quality of life tool than the standard EQ-5D-5L did.5

Vision-related bolt-ons, for instance, aim to quantify the unique challenges faced by
individuals with visual impairment.7 This additional item asks respondents to rate
their vision-related difficulties using the same 3- or 5-level response structure as the
core EQ-5D dimensions and has been proven to increase responsiveness and
discriminatory power of the questionnaire.8,9
In Thailand, visual impairment continues to pose a significant public health
challenge, affecting >2% of the population. Conditions such as cataracts, diabetic
retinopathy, and glaucoma notably reduce individuals’ quality of life (QoL).10 Despite
the availability of the Thai EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L,11 no validated vision-specific
bolt-on exists for these tools. Cross-cultural adaptation requires meticulous
translation, cognitive debriefing, and psychometric validation to ensure linguistic and
conceptual equivalence.12,13 Furthermore, comparing the performance of the 3L and
5L versions with a vision bolt-on could clarify whether the enhanced granularity of
the 5L improves sensitivity in detecting vision-related QoL changes. To our
knowledge, no psychometric evaluation has been conducted on non-English versions
of vision-related QoL questionnaires.
This study aims to translate and evaluate the psychometric properties of Thai
versions of the vision bolt-on EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L questionnaires and to compare
the properties with the original translated EQ-5D questionnaires. Specifically, we
assessed their reliability (test-retest stability), constructed validity (convergent and
known-groups), and responsiveness in a cohort of Thai adults with varying degrees of
visual impairment. By addressing this gap, our findings will inform the use of these
adapted tools in clinical practice, health economic evaluations, and population
studies targeting vision-related QoL in Thailand.

Methods
This prospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board
of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University (COA No. 1023/2023). The study
was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants
received a comprehensive explanation of the study, and informed consent was
obtained before enrollment.

Translation of the Vision Bolt-On EQ-5D-3L and the Vision Bolt-On EQ-5D5L
Permission for use and translation of the vision bolt-on EQ-5D-3L and the vision bolton EQ-5D-5L was granted without charge by the EuroQol Research Foundation. The
translation process followed the required EuroQol Translation Guidelines.14 The
process consists of forward translation, backward translation, cognitive debriefing,
and submission for approval from the EuroQol. Two independent bilingual experts
carried out the forward translation of the vision bolt-on component, referencing the
legacy Thai EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L versions. Subsequently, the translators and
investigators conferred to produce a consensus version. The backward translation
was done by 2 independent translators, and the reconciled version was compared to
the original English version of the questionnaires to produce the second consensus

Patients and Procedures
A sample size was determined using G∗Power version 3.1.9.7 (Heinrich-HeineUniversität Düsseldorf, Germany),15 assuming a moderate effect size for test-retest
reliability, known-groups validity, convergent validity, and responsiveness, with 90%
power and a 0.05 significance level. The largest calculated sample size based on
known-groups validity and a projected 30% attrition rate was 300. The participant
was conveniently sampled from the Ophthalmology Clinic at the King Chulalongkorn
Memorial Hospital from February 1 to March 31, 2024. Participants were categorized
into 5 groups on the basis of the WHO International Classification of Diseases 11
(ICD-11) criteria for vision impairment.16 Those with no vision impairment had a
best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of at least 20/40 in the better eye. Mild vision
impairment was defined as a BCVA worse than 20/40 but equal to or better than
20/70, while moderate vision impairment included individuals with a BCVA worse
than 20/70 but equal to or better than 20/200. Severe vision impairment was
classified as a BCVA worse than 20/200 but equal to or better than 20/400. Finally,
blindness was defined as a BCVA worse than 20/400 in the better eye.
Participants were asked to complete the translated questionnaires in the following
sequence: (1) EQ-5D-5L; (2) vision bolt-on EQ-5D-5L; (3) participants' demographic
data, including age, sex, educational level, and past ophthalmic and systemic
diseases; (4) EQ–visual analog scale (VAS); (5) EQ-5D-3L; and (6) vision bolt-on EQ5D-3L. If participants were unable to read the questionnaires, interviewers would
read them to the participants without any interpretation. Two weeks after the first
interview, participants would be contacted by the same interviewer by means of a
video call. Participants were asked to compare their current eye health perception
with their initial assessment using the following options: (1) much better than the
first assessment, (2) somewhat better than the first assessment, (3) about the same
as the first assessment, (4) somewhat worse than the first assessment, and (5) much
worse than the first assessment. Patients who reported no change in eye health3 were
included in the test-retest analysis. Those who indicated an improvement1,2 or a
decline in eye health4,5 were categorized into the improvement and worsening
groups, respectively, for the responsiveness assessment.
The utility scores of the EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L were calculated using Thai value
sets,17,18 whereas the utility scores of the vision bolt-on EQ-5D-3L and vision bolt-on
EQ-5D-5L were calculated using available value sets from other countries.7,19

Data Analyses
Practicality was assessed based on the time required to complete the questionnaire,
recorded by the interviewers. The ceiling effect was analyzed on the basis of the
percentage of participants who achieved the highest score on the questionnaire. If
this percentage was <15%, the questionnaire was considered capable of distinguishing
between individuals with different health conditions.20 Test-retest reliability was
evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), calculated with the two-way
mixed and single-measure method. ICC values were interpreted as follows: <0.40

Known-groups validity was assessed by evaluating differences in QoL between
groups with and without the characteristic of interest. Statistical analysis was
performed using the Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis test. For the Mann-Whitney
U test, effect sizes were categorized as small (<0.3), medium (0.3-0.5), and large
(>0.5).23 For the Kruskal-Wallis test, effect sizes were classified as small (0.01-0.059),
medium (0.06-0.13), and large (>0.13).23 Convergent validity was evaluated by
comparing the relationship between instruments measuring similar health concepts
using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. The strength of the correlation was
determined using established criteria as follows: weak or no correlation (r < 0.25),
moderate (0.25 ≤ r < 0.50), moderate to strong (0.50 ≤ r < 0.75), and strong (r ≥
0.75).24 Responsiveness refers to the ability of the questionnaires to detect changes in
eye health status over time. This was assessed using scores from patients who
perceived their eye health status as improved or worsened at follow-up.
Responsiveness was evaluated by calculating the standardized effect size to compare
QoL scores between the 2 time points. A value of 0.2 indicated low sensitivity to eye
health change, 0.5 represented moderate sensitivity, and 0.8 suggested high
sensitivity to eye health change.24 All statistical analyses were done using IBM SPSS
version 29. P < .050 were considered statistically significant.

Results
The study initially recruited 421 participants, but 120 individuals declined to
participate, resulting in a refusal rate of 28.5%. This reduced the number of
participants to 301 for the first interview. Approximately 46% of participants selfcompleted the questionnaires, whereas the remaining responses were read aloud by
researchers without any interpretation. Following the first interview, 26 participants
were unable to complete the second interview, with 3 declining to be interviewed
and 23 being unreachable. Ultimately, 275 participants completed the second
interview. Baseline characteristics of the participants and participant flow chart are
presented in Appendix 1 in Supplemental Materials found at
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.vhri.2025.101498 . Most of the participants were women
and were between 61 and 70 years old. Around 53% of participants had a bachelor’s
degree or higher. Retinal diseases were the most common concurrent ocular disease
among participants, and >80% of participants had no vision impairment with the
better-seeing eye. Most participants (78.1%) had at least 1 systemic comorbidity. EQ5D-5L and its vision bolt-on variant showed higher mean scores than their EQ-5D-3L
counterparts (Table 1).

In the known-group validity assessment, no statistically significant differences in
mean scores were observed across different sex and age groups in any of the
questionnaires. Although significant differences were observed in education levels for
EQ-5D-3L, EQ-5D-5L, and vision bolt-on EQ-5D-3L, the effect sizes were small. All
questionnaires were sensitive to different numbers of comorbidities and the level of
vision impairment in the better-seeing eye. The vision bolt-on domains demonstrated
stronger effect sizes among the vision impairment categories, particularly when
distinguishing on the basis of the visual acuity of the worse-seeing eye (Table 4). In
terms of convergent validity, both EQ-5D-5L and vision bolt-on EQ-5D-5L exhibited
similar correlations with EQ-VAS (Spearman’s rho = 0.404 and 0.402, respectively),
whereas the vision bolt-on EQ-5D-3L demonstrated a slightly stronger correlation
with EQ-VAS compared to the EQ-5D-3L (Spearman’s rho = 0.442 vs 0.405) (Table 5).
All questionnaires showed moderate responsiveness to eye health status in the
improvement group (Table 6). The instruments were generally less responsive to
worsening health. The vision bolt-on EQ-5D-5L demonstrated the strongest
responsiveness to both eye health improvements and deteriorations (standardized
effect size = 0.486 and 0.659, respectively).

Discussion
This study investigated the psychometric properties of the Thai versions of the vision
bolt-on EQ-5D-3L and vision bolt-on EQ-5D-5L, comparing them with the standard
EQ-5D-3L, EQ-5D-5L, and EQ-VAS. Overall, our findings suggest that adding the vision
dimension offers enhanced sensitivity to vision-related health states. This is
consistent with previous work on vision bolt-ons in other settings8,9 and aligns with
a broader research strategy of using task-specific bolt-ons for particular populations,
such as adding a "respiratory" dimension for patients with chronic obstructive
pulmonary disease25 or "interpersonal relationships" for the Thai population.26
Below, we discuss our key results in the context of previous research on EQ-5D
instruments and their adaptations.
In terms of practicality, our study demonstrated no missing data and a 0% refusal rate
among participants who agreed to take part, indicating that the questionnaires were
acceptable and feasible in this Thai population. The short completion times for the
bolt-on items (a mean of 18-24 seconds) also support their practicality. These
findings align with the general usability of the EQ-5D, which has been widely
reported as brief and user-friendly.2 Both instruments showed moderate to good test retest reliability, with ICC values ranging from 0.62 to 0.64 and weighted kappa
values from 0.59 to 0.66, which were similar to their original counterparts. The ICC
values in this study aligned with the results from other psychometric studies of the
EQ-5D conducted in similar Thai populations.11,27 When placed in this context, these
values are shown to be consistent and acceptable; for instance, a study by
Pattanaphesaj et al27 in Thai diabetes patients found a nearly identical ICC of 0.64 for
the EQ-5D-3L index. Although some guidelines recommend ICC values above 0.70 for
excellent reliability,28 questionnaires capturing broad aspects of health with less
number of items might yield lower ICC values.29 Our results are also consistent with
similar validation studies of the EQ-5D-5L, which also reported ICCs in the moderate to-good range.30 Notably, the vision bolt-on EQ-5D-5L showed slightly higher ICC
values than the 3L version, suggesting that the additional scales may improve test retest stability in capturing changes or consistency in vision-related health.
In line with our expectations regarding known-groups validity, participants with
comorbidities and more severe vision impairment reported significantly lower QoL
scores, indicating that both standard EQ-5D and vision bolt-on versions can
differentiate between health states. These findings align with previous research
indicating that generic instruments effectively capture major health decrements,7
whereas the bolt-on additions can further distinguish the severity of visual
impairment, as previously demonstrated in the original English version.9 However,
there were no significant differences by sex or age, possibly reflecting the cross sectional nature of our data and the relatively narrow age distribution of participants.
A notable finding was the significant differences by educational level (albeit with
small effect sizes), suggesting that social determinants of health can influence self reported QoL.31 Correlations between EQ-5D versions and their vision bolt-ons were
high (Spearman’s rho 0.84-0.87), indicating that the bolt-on dimension is well
integrated into the overall health construct with good convergent validity.
Correlations with EQ-VAS were moderate (0.40-0.45), which aligns with previous
studies noting that a single, overall self-rating (the VAS) may not fully capture the

Concerning responsiveness, when participants reported feeling “better,” the vision
bolt-on EQ-5D-5L showed the largest standardized effect size, indicating moderate
sensitivity to improvements in health. This aligns with previous research
demonstrating that the vision bolt-on dimensions can enhance the sensitivity of the

declines in eye health may be harder to detect in a short follow-up period. This
asymmetrical responsiveness, in which health outcome instruments in
ophthalmology can often detect improvement more robustly than they can detect
decline, aligns with findings in other research.33,34 Future longitudinal studies with

ceiling effects (35%) than the EQ-5D-5L (23%)—a pattern consistently reported in the
literature.2,35 The vision bolt-on versions further reduced ceiling effects (5%-9%),
which suggests that adding a vision dimension can help discriminate mild or
moderate health problems that might otherwise go unnoticed on the standard
instrument. This finding highlights the benefit of a bolt-on domain where specific
aspects of functioning, such as vision, are critically relevant.
Our work correlates with the previous research by Gandhi et al8 and Luo et al,9 which
demonstrated that including a vision dimension could enhance both the
discriminative ability and responsiveness of EQ-5D in populations with eye
conditions. Similarly, Herdman et al2 and Janssen et al35 reported that the EQ-5D-5L
version generally improves measurement precision and reduces ceiling effects
compared to the 3L. We extend these findings to a Thai-speaking population,
confirming that the vision bolt-on is feasible and reliable in a non-English setting—a
gap previously identified in the literature. The bolt-ons also enhance sensitivity
without significantly increasing the response time of the questionnaires. Our results
suggest that the Thai vision bolt-on can capture meaningful differences in vision related QoL while retaining the advantages of a generic, preference-based measure.
This study has several notable strengths. First, it employed robust translation and
validation procedures, including forward-backward translation, and a cognitive
debriefing process, ensuring linguistic and conceptual equivalence of the Thai vision
bolt-on versions. Second, the large sample size and full data completion rate
underscore the feasibility and acceptability of these instruments for Thai
participants. Third, including both 3L and 5L versions alongside the vision bolt-on
items offers comprehensive insights into how each variant performs in terms of
reliability, validity, responsiveness, and practicality. However, this study has several
notable limitations. First, the sample was recruited via convenience sampling from a
single tertiary hospital, resulting in a highly educated cohort that was also
predominantly composed of individuals with no vision impairment in their better seeing eye (over 80%). Consequently, the very small subgroups for severe impairment
(N = 1) and blindness (N = 12) limit the generalizability of our findings to more
severely impaired populations and warrant further validation in such cohorts.

Conclusions
In conclusion, the Thai versions of the vision bolt-on EQ-5D-3L and EQ-5D-5L
demonstrated acceptable reliability, strong known-group validity, and moderate
responsiveness, particularly for detecting improvements in vision-related health. The
instrument's responsiveness to worsening vision could not be confirmed, likely
because of the small sample size of this subgroup. However, the 5L version seemed
more sensitive overall, reducing ceiling effects to a greater extent and showing a
higher effect size when participants reported feeling better, suggesting it may be
more suitable for capturing subtler differences in vision-related QoL. These findings
underscore the practical and clinical value of adding a vision dimension to EQ-5D
instruments in Thai-speaking populations, enabling more precise evaluations of
interventions aimed at visual health. Future longitudinal research is recommended to
further confirm its performance in detecting health declines and to broaden its use.
