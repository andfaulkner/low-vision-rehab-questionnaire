Identifying low vision rehabilitation priorities
using a conjoint analysis approach

Abstract. To identify the relative importance of a range of activities amongst patients with age related macular degeneration (AMD) and to explore the visual function and clinical characteristics
associated with the different priorities for low vision rehabilitation. A prospective sample of 122
patients with AMD attending the low vision clinics at Edinburgh Eye Hospital were given several
tests including: binocular visual acuity (logMAR), binocular contrast sensitivity (Pelli–Robson),
quality of life (QoL) NEI-VF 25 questionnaire and utilities were determined using a time trade-off
task (TTO) and a conjoint analysis task. Clinical grading of AMD severity was according to the
international classification system. The relative importance of five different activities, identified by
factor analysis in a previous study, was assessed in a paired comparison paradigm using discretechoice conjoint analysis. The activities were: reading, recognising faces, getting about outside,
problems associated with glare, and difficulties associated with performing household tasks. Each
activity was represented at three levels of difficulty (none, a few, and a lot). Utilities derived from
conjoint analysis were compared with TTO utilities. A factor analysis showed that AMD severity and
basic measures of visual function were only moderately related to TTO utilities but independent of
conjoint utilities. Analyses of individual patient utilities showed two distinct patient clusters; those
for whom reading was of greatest importance and those for whom getting about outside was of
greatest importance. Conjoint analysis offers the potential of identifying individual patient priorities
for low vision rehabilitation. D 2005 Published by Elsevier B.V.

1. Introduction
Establishing priorities for rehabilitation is essential for two reasons, firstly to help
determine what is important to the individual patient in terms of their rehabilitation needs
and secondly, to provide an estimate of the value of the service as a whole to inform health
care managers’ decisions about its utility and cost effectiveness of the service in the context
of other health care services competing for limited resources. There are many approaches to
measuring importance or utility and most can be grouped into one of two categories; these
are (a) direct magnitude estimation or rating scales and (b) choice-based methods, the latter
of which include procedures such as standard gamble, time trade-off (TTO) and conjoint
analysis. In the UK, the National Institute for Clinical Excellence (NICE) has been
established to appraise the cost-effectiveness of health care interventions, including
medicines, medical devices, and medical procedures [1] and eventually, no doubt, this
will include an evaluation of the impact of low vision services. The recommendations of
NICE are that the value or utility of health effects be assessed using choice-based methods
and they specifically exclude the use of rating scales. Presumably, this recommendation of
NICE assumes that the different choice-based methods will provide equivalence of utilities.
2. Purpose
One of the purposes of the present study was to compare and assess the equivalence of
TTO and conjoint analysis for deriving utilities associated with vision-related quality of
life (QoL) on the same cohort of patients with age-related macular degeneration (AMD).
Comparisons of these utilities were also made with clinical grade of AMD, basic measures
of visual function and standardised vision-related QoL questionnaire data.
3. Methods
A more detailed description of the study is given elsewhere [2]. The study comprised a
prospective sample of 122 patients attending the low vision clinics at Edinburgh Eye
Hospital. AMD was graded using the international classification system [3]. The four
monocular grades were: mild dry, moderate dry, late dry (geographic atrophy affecting the
inner macular) and wet (including disciform scar). Three binocular categories of AMD
severity were derived from the monocular grades: bmildQ = mild in both eyes OR mild in
one eye and moderate in the other eye, bmoderateQ = moderate in both eyes OR mild in one
eye and severe in the other eye, bsevereQ = late dry or wet AMD in both eyes. The visual
function tests included: monocular and binocular best corrected distance and near visual
acuity (logMAR) and contrast sensitivity (Pelli–Robson). QoL questionnaires included the
NEI VF-25, a life-style questionnaire and the two choice-based methods for determining
utility were TTO and conjoint analysis.

4.2. Conjoint analysis
This method of assessing the relative importance of different attributes (e.g. daily
activities) has a widely established use for evaluating health state preferences [4]. Patients
were asked to consider themselves in one of two different states as a consequence of their
AMD. These two states were described by paired profiles of five independent attributes of
daily living identified from a previous study [5]. The profiles comprised different
combinations of the five attributes described in terms of one of three levels of perceived
difficulty (bno difficultyQ, ba few difficultiesQ, and ba lot of difficultiesQ). An example
paired-comparison is shown in Table 1. A fractional factorial replicate of this 35 design
involved each patient responding to 15 paired-comparisons in which they were required to
state which person, in their opinion, was worse off.
Two questionnaires were used depending on whether a patient could, or could not, read
N36 comfortably. For those who could read 36-point print a 5-attribute questionnaire was
used, whereas for those who were unable to read 36-point print, the questionnaire was
reduced to only 3 attributes (i.e. recognising faces, getting about outside, pouring liquids
and household chores) and relied on temporal rather than spatial comparisons.
5. Patients
The mean age of the patients was 77.6 years (S.D. = 6.8) and, using the binocular grade
of AMD severity, 16.4% were classified as bmildQ, 33.3% as bmoderateQ and 50.3% as
bsevereQ. The mean binocular distance logMAR visual acuity was 0.50 (S.D. = 0.43), mean
binocular near logMAR visual acuity 0.72 (S.D. = 0.47) and mean binocular Pelli–Robson
contrast sensitivity was 1.15 (S.D. = 0.43).

6. Results
6.1. Time trade-off (TTO) utilities
The mean age of respondents’ choice for one of the three life-expectancy groups of 30, 20 or 10
years was 68.5 years, 76.8 years and 81.5 years, respectively. While this is an indication of the
validity of their choice, the frequency distribution of the percentage years a person would trade
showed a definite quantisation for proportions of 0%, 25%, 50% and 75%. About half were not
prepared to trade any of their estimated remaining life while 1 in 4 were prepared to trade 50% of
their expected remaining years.
An ANOVA showed no significant difference across the three life expectance groups in terms of
binocular distance and near visual acuity, although the group expecting to live the longest had
significantly better contrast sensitivity than the other two groups ( F = 3.7, df = 2, p = 0.2). Using all
respondents, binocular classification of AMD severity significantly affected TTO utilities ( F = 19.8,
df = 2, p b 0.001) with a mean utility of 0.96 for mild AMD, 0.92 for moderate AMD and 0.69 for
severe AMD. In other words, people with severe AMD were prepared to trade more of their
remaining expected life for perfect vision than were those patients with mild or moderate AMD. Post
hoc analyses showed no significant difference between mean utilities for mild and moderate AMD
but a significant difference between moderate and severe AMD ( p b 0.001).
The proportion of patients who refused to trade any of their estimated remaining life (non-traders)
was highest amongst those with mild binocular AMD severity (88%) and least amongst those with
severe binocular AMD severity (30%). Correlations between TTO utilities, binocular visual function
(distance VA, near VA, contrast sensitivity), AMD severity in the better eye, worse eye and binocular
AMD severity classification were low, ranging from r = 0.27 to r = 0.40 (all p b 0.01) when all
patients were included in the analyses. When the non-traders were removed from the analyses, none
of these correlations remained significant at p = 0.01, suggesting that TTO utilities, amongst those
prepared to trade, were unrelated to visual function and clinical status.
6.2. Conjoint analysis utilities
In these analyses, utilities for each attribute are determined by the differences in importance
between the assigned values of bno difficultiesQ, ba few difficultiesQ and ba lot of difficultiesQ.
From the 5-attribute conjoint task, outdoor mobility and reading were the most important. Using
logit analysis, utilities were computed for each attribute in terms of the preference difference
between the levels of bno difficultiesQ and ba lot of difficulties.Q Setting the utility for the most
important attribute of bgetting about outside aloneQ as unity, the relative utilities of the other
attributes were: reading = 0.72, pouring liquids = 0.66, recognising faces = 0.52 and problems with
glare = 0.28. Thus, for the sample, reading was 72% as important as getting about outside alone,
while recognising faces was only about half as important. There was a similar relative importance
for the common variables in the 3-attribute task completed by patients with poorer visual acuity.
Using Bonferroni correction for p b 0.01, none of the utilities generated from conjoint analysis
were significantly related to measures of visual function (i.e. binocular distance or near visual
acuity, or binocular contrast sensitivity) or the degree of AMD severity, and neither was there any
relationship between age or gender and conjoint utilities.
Binary logistic regression analyses identified no predictors of stated priorities from clinical status,
visual function or QoL difficulties. However, a factor analysis of the visual function data, AMD
grading, TTO utilities, conjoint utilities and a self-rating of general health demonstrated four
principal components which accounted for 71% of the variance. The first component was
characterised by aspects of visual function and AMD grade, which were only moderately related to
TTO utilities. The next three components characterised separate independent dimensions of the

conjoint utilities; one related to priorities for reading and seeing detail, another related to priorities

It is possible, by means of a Hierarchical Bayesian analysis, to derive individual patient utility
profiles across the five stated conjoint attributes. Factor analysis of these individual utilities shows
that there were two principal groups of patients’ priorities and similar findings have recently been
found in a sample of patients with vision loss from glaucoma. About one-third of the AMD sample
had reading as their principal priority and about one-third had outdoor mobility as their principal
priority.

7. Conclusion
There are limitations to the use of TTO, with many patients (i.e. mostly those with mild
AMD and better visual acuity) refusing to trade. Utilities derived from TTO are only
moderately related to aspects of visual function and the severity of AMD, and are also
independent of conjoint analysis utilities. TTO and conjoint analysis tasks are clearly
measuring different properties of importance. Conjoint analysis, by its contextual framing
of options, more closely approximates an individual’s practical decision making.
Factor analysis of conjoint utilities reveals two principal clusters of patients in terms of
utilities and these require radically different rehabilitation approaches. Patients whose
priority is for getting about outdoors alone will initially require mobility training, and
patients whose priority is for reading are more likely to benefit initially from optical low
vision devices. The findings emphasise the need for an integrated approach to low vision
rehabilitation; to address the wrong priority for an individual is not only wasteful of
resources but is likely to have a negative impact on the patient’s psychological state.
The findings also demonstrate that priorities for rehabilitation cannot be predicted from
clinical state, visual function or rated QoL difficulties. While rehabilitation preferences are
not synonymous with need, preferences need no inferences. Conjoint analysis offers the
opportunity of deriving individual preferences, although there is a need for more work to
identify the best method of assessing importance in rehabilitation. Only by this means will
it be possible to determine the appropriate priorities to address for an individual patient
and derive the cost-effectiveness of low vision services as a whole.
