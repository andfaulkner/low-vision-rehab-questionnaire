ABSTRACT

ARTICLE HISTORY

Purpose: To investigate the factors affecting the reading speed of patients with central scotoma due to
age-related macular degeneration (AMD).
Materials and Methods: We included 63 eyes of 63 patients with AMD who applied to our low vision
clinic between August 2018 and September 2019 in this prospective study. We evaluated sociodemographic characteristics, eye examination findings and Minnesota Low Vision Reading Test
(MNREAD) results. We used the MAIA microperimeter device to evaluate the properties of the preferred
retinal locus for fixation (PRL) of the patients. Evaluations included the assessment of the effects of all
parameters on reading speed.
Results: The PRL was most commonly in the nasal (31%) and superior (26%) quadrants. Twenty-nine
percent of the cases preferred the left visual field. PRL localization had no effect on reading speed,
whereas, fixation stability, educational status, presence of foveal absolute scotoma, reading acuity and
duration of reading interruption were found to have the most significant effects. Multiple regression
analysis showed that reading speed decreased by 67 units in the presence of unstable fixation, by 17 units
in the presence of foveal absolute scotoma, by 3 units with every 0.1 increase in logMAR value, and by 1.7
units with every 1-year increase in reading interruption. Additionally, being a university graduate was
associated with an increased reading speed (by 18 units)
Conclusion: Increased reading performance is one of the factors that can improve quality of life. The
factors found to affect the reading speed in the current study may guide the rehabilitation process in low
vision patients.

Introduction
Decreased reading ability is the most common reason leading to
patients’ application to low vision centers.1,2 Reading is
a complex activity that is affected by many factors, including
oculomotor control,3,4 decreased fixation stability,5–8 narrowed
visual field,9–13 comprehension speed,14 near visual acuity,3 contrast sensitivity,3,7,15 print size3 and age.16 Therefore, the reading
performance of a low vision patient is not always sufficient, even
when the patient has good near visual acuity.9,17,18
In patients with age-related macular degeneration (AMD),
the real fovea cannot perform its normal function; thus causing
a shift of vision to a healthier area of the retina, defined as the
preferred retinal locus (PRL).10,19–22
The microperimetry device has been used to evaluate the
functional state of the macula and the fixation characteristics in
these patients.23,24 Fixation stability is an important factor that
affects reading performance greatly, especially in the presence
of central scotoma. This has been proven by various studies
that have demonstrated a strong positive correlation between
reading speed and fixation stability.5–8 Although many studies
have explored fixation stability and its effect on reading speed,
there are only a limited number of studies investigating the
effect of PRL location, distance to fovea and its relationship
with scotoma and visual field on reading speed. Information

As mentioned before, there are a number of unrelated
factors that contribute to a person’s reading speed. Although
some studies have explored the influence of various parameters
on reading speed there are very few studies that have assessed
the actual effects of these factors in a systematic manner that
would enable direct comparisons with other studies and also
between the parameters themselves. Therefore, in this study,
we aimed to investigate the impact of microperimetric parameters, and sociodemographic and clinical characteristics on
reading performance in patients with central scotoma due to
AMD, and to determine the relationships between these parameters and reading speed. We obtained and reviewed these
results with the intention that they could guide healthcare
professionals performing near-sight rehabilitation.

Materials and methods
The ethical committee of Ankara University approved this
prospective study. All steps and procedures were in accordance
with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. All individuals
that participated in the study provided informed consent.

We enrolled a total of 63 eyes diagnosed with AMD-related
central vision loss from a group of 63 patients who had applied
for visual rehabilitation to the Low Vision Rehabilitation
Center of Ankara University Faculty of Medicine, between
August 2018 and September 2019. The diagnoses of patients
were either atrophic AMD or stable exudative AMD. We
included patients who were older than 55 years, were at least
primary school graduates, spoke Turkish as their native language, and did not have neurological diseases or mental disabilities that prevented them from reading or performing the
MNREAD test. Additionally, only those with best-corrected
visual acuity (BCVA) of between logMAR 1.3 and logMAR
0.5 were included in the patient group. Since visual acuity
below 0.5 logMAR did not significantly affect reading performance, patients with BCVA better than 0.5 logMAR were not
included in the study. Finally, patients who were illiterate,
those that had previously undergone visual rehabilitation,
and patients who used low-vision aids and/or had trained
retinal locus (TRL) were excluded from the study.
We conducted detailed examinations in each patient,
including BCVA measurement, biomicroscopic and fundus
examination, low vision examination, contrast sensitivity test,
lesion size, and quality of life scoring. Additionally, we asked
patients about their reading habits prior to the disease and
determined the time at which they had significantly lost their
reading habit (being unable to continue their usual reading
habits, or the inability to read books or newspapers). We
recorded the time that had elapsed since the loss of reading
habit (in years) and this duration was defined as ‘reading
interruption’. This data was obtained from patients and their
relatives.
The BCVA was measured according to the Early Treatment
Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) chart and recorded as
logMAR. Reading acuity was measured with the MNREAD test
and recorded with ‘M’ value. The CSV-1000 test was used to
test contrast sensitivity. The CSV-1000 test is traditionally
applied with spatial fluorescence varying from 3, 6, 12 and 18
cpd (cycles per degree) values and grating from 2.5 meters.26
However, since all subjects were patients with severe low vision, we performed the test by halving the standard test
distance and cpd values. We used the “Fundus autofluorescence (FAF)” method to calculate lesion size via the
“Image Finder” program and recorded results in mm2. The
quality of life scale applied to patients was the Turkishvalidated version of the NEI-VFQ-25.27 The same technician
(B.S.) conducted all evaluations with this questionnaire. From
this scale, we recorded and evaluated the general score and the
near-vision activities subscale score.
The Turkish version of the MNREAD reading test
(MNREAD-TR) was the test used to determine reading performance. Idil et al. carried out the Turkish language reliability and validity study of this test.28 This test is comprised
of cards with print sizes representing logMAR 1.3 (8 M) to
logMAR (−0.5) (0.12 M) (1 M = logMAR 0.4). The smallest
print size that a person could read without making
a significant mistake was used to calculate reading acuity
(RA). Critical print size (CPS) was the smallest print size
that could be read with maximum reading speed. Maximum

reading speed (MRS) was the reading speed of a patient when
unrestricted by print size. We used word per minute (wpm)
values to depict the results of reading speed tests. The reading accessibility index (ACC) is calculated by dividing the
reading speed obtained in the first 10 sentences of the
MNREAD card by 200. These sentences represent the most
frequently used print sizes in daily life. Since normal reading
speed corresponds to 200 wpm, the normal ACC value is “1”.
ACC is a good indicator of reading speed in central
scotoma.5
We used the MAIA Microperimetry device to evaluate fixation. P1 and P2 values showing the percentage of fixation
points in a circle with a radius of 1° and 2° were used to analyze
fixation stability. Definitions were as follows: ‘stable fixation’
when P1 was higher than 75%, ‘relative unstable fixation’ when
P1 < 75% and P2 > 75%, and ‘unstable fixation’ when P1 and P2
were both lower than 75%.29 The bivariate contour ellipse area
(BCEA) was the elliptical area associated with the major and
minor axes covered by fixative eye movements. The retinal
sensitivity results range between 0 and 36 dB.
Macular Integrity Index (MII) is the assessment of macular
integrity based on comparisons with age-matched responses.
Categorization of MII values were as follows: a value under
40% was normal, 40–60% was suspicious and over 60% was
abnormal.
We recorded patients’ retinal sensitivity, MII, P1, P2, fixation stability, BCEA 63 and BCEA 95 values automatically, as
they are part of the routine microperimetry analysis (Figure 1).
We performed some specific measurements for the study, in
addition to the routine data obtained from the microperimetry
analysis. We printed the grid-shaped screenshot showing the
localization of the PRL on A4 paper and determined the placement of the PRL and calculated scotoma dimensions and the
distance of the PRL to the fovea and the edge of the lesion.
A single ophthalmologist (D.A.) performed all calculations in
order to prevent inter-operator variance. The operator measured the distance between the PRL and Fovea in millimeters
and calculated degree-equivalents. In cases with uncertain
fovea location, we estimated and marked the fovea location
by calculating from the middle of the optic disc to 15.5°
horizontally (towards the temporal) and 1.3° towards the
inferior.30 We classified the location of the PRL as superior,
inferior, temporal, nasal or central (central 4°), with respect to
the center of the fovea31 (Figure 2).
We used the IBM SPSS Statistics Version 20.0 package
program for all statistical analyses. We defined the threshold
for statistical significance as p < .05 in all tests. The Shapiro
Wilk test was used to test the normality of the distribution of
numerical measurements. The independent samples t-test and
the Mann–Whitney U test were used in the comparison of
numerical measurements among two groups, with respect to
the normality of distribution. We used the One-Way Variance
Analysis or the Kruskal Wallis test for general comparisons of
numerical measurements in more than two groups – again
according to the normality of distribution. The Bonferroni,
Scheffe or Tamhane methods were used for post-hoc corrections after identification of significant differences in >2 group
comparisons – depending on variance homogeneity.

Results
The study included a total of 63 patients with advanced AMD and
low vision, aged between 58 and 93 years. The eye with better
visual acuity was the left eye in 36 patients and the right eye in 27
patients. The percentage of patients with atrophic AMD (48%)
and exudative AMD (52%) were similar. Patients with undergraduate education had higher MRS values (p = .008).
Additionally, MRS values were positively correlated with total
score (p = .050) and near-vision activities subscale score
(p = .027) of the quality-of-life questionnaire, and negatively
correlated with duration of reading interruption (p = .014). The
sociodemographic characteristics of the cases are shown in
Table 1.

The mean distant BCVA value was 0.66 ± 0.2 logMAR,
while the mean near BCVA was 2.63 ± 2.46 M. The mean
lesion size was 7.162 ± 4.671 mm2 (min–max: 1.110–
24.568 mm2). The mean contrast sensitivity results/values at
low frequencies (1.5 cpd and 3 cpd) were 12.3 ± 11.2 dB and
21.0 ± 15.1 dB, respectively. For the medium frequencies (6 cpd
and 9 cpd), the results were 10.62 ± 9.49 dB and 4.46 ± 3.93 dB,
respectively.

and reading speed were assessed, we found negative correlations between MRS values and both the distant and near
acuities (p = .002 and p = .003, respectively). MRS was found

Discussion
We investigated factors that affect the reading performance of
patients with AMD and determined that there were relationships between reading speed and parameters, such as fixation
stability, presence of foveal absolute scotoma, reading acuity,
education level, and duration of reading interruption. The
factor that decreased reading speed the most was found to be
the presence of unstable fixation. This result shows the importance of fixation stability in increasing reading speed in near
visual rehabilitation, as it is apparent that near visual acuity is
not sufficient for the evaluation of reading performance.
In addition to the parameters obtained from microperimetry analysis and the automated calculation results, we also
evaluated other factors that may affect reading speed, such as
sociodemographic characteristics, eye examination findings,
contrast sensitivity and MNREAD parameters. We also conducted multiple linear regression analysis to identify factors
that were independently associated with reading speed. To our
knowledge, this study is the only study in the literature that
focused on this rarely researched subject via the measurement
of an extensive set of parameters with a study group that had
a relatively high number of patients.

Previous studies have reported that reading speed should be
at least 80 wpm for fluent reading and 40 wpm is only sufficient
for spot reading.11 Reading speed is significantly lower in
patients with central scotoma compared to the normal population. Complete loss of reading ability is common in these
patients. In our study, the MRS of the patients ranged between
19 and 160 wpm, while MRS was lower than 80 wpm in 42
(66%) and lower than 40 wpm in 17 (27%) patients.
The strong relationship between fixation stability and reading speed has been demonstrated in many studies.5,7,8
Crossland et al. reported that fixation stability was responsible
for around 54% of the change in reading speed in patients with
AMD and juvenile macular degeneration. Besides this, they
also reported that the decrease in reading speed was not associated with fixation instability alone.6 In our study, in accordance with the literature, a statistically significant relationship
was found between fixation stability and MRS, but unstable
fixation was not found to be the only factor that reduced
reading speed. Having foveal absolute scotoma and duration
of reading interruption also influenced reading speed. The
reading speed was also affected by the education level of the
patients.
In the presence of central scotoma, visual span narrows;
therefore, patients often rely on guessing words in order to be
able to read,13,14 which increases the importance of visual
abilities. We evaluated the influence of reading interruption
on the speed of reading because we considered this factor as
a confounding variable that would alter reading speed due to
its possible adverse effects on cognitive functions. According to
our analysis, each year of reading interruption decreased reading speed by 1.7 units. If there are periods when the patient
does not read, it is very likely that his/her visual skills, such as
visual memory and visual completion ability, will deteriorate.”
Various studies have shown that scotoma size affects reading speed.11,13,14 Scotoma size is especially important in terms
of identifying and perceiving a word that is being read. In
central scotoma, individuals can perform normal reading in
the absence of foveal absolute scotoma with the presence of 2°–
5° of the field of view in the horizontal plane (2° to the right and
left of fixation).32 In their study, Whittaker et al.11 have
reported that field of view (number of letters visible) and
scotoma size were important for reading, and that fluent reading was not possible in the presence of scotoma size greater
than 22°. Ergun et al.13 found a negative correlation between
absolute scotoma size and reading speed (r = −0.48, p = .023).
We also found a negative correlation between absolute scotoma size and MRS (r = −0.422, p = .001). Various factors, such
as scotoma location, fixation stability and other parameters,
could explain the relatively lower correlation between these
parameters (relative to other correlations) in the present
study. Absolute scotoma size ranged between 0° and 62° in
our study, and it was greater than 22° in 16 cases (25%). When
we evaluated the reading speed of these cases, we found that
MRS values were lower than 80 wpm in 14 (87.5%) of these
individuals, indicating that they could not read fluently.
The presence of foveal absolute scotoma also decreases
reading performance.32 A statistically significant relationship
was found between reading speed and foveal absolute scotoma
in this study. The multiple regression analysis revealed that the

presence of foveal scotoma caused a decrease of 17 units in
MRS value. This is largely due to the fact that the fovea plays
a critical role in visual acuity, as it is the area with the highest
retinal resolution.33
It has been reported that 77–100% of low-vision patients with
poor central vision experience adaptations through various
mechanisms. In most cases, this adaptation is the development
of a PRL.10,24,34,35 In the current study, 98% of the cases had
developed a PRL; 84% of these were extrafoveal and 16% were
foveal. However, foveal fixation is not sufficient for good reading
speed. When there is retinal sensitivity loss in the area surrounding the fovea, visual acuity is significantly reduced, even when
fixation is central.36 The significant relationship between retinal
sensitivity loss and reading speed has been shown in our study.
There are studies reporting that the stability of PRL
decreases as it moves away from the fovea.24,31,37 PRL-fovea
distance has been reported to vary between 1.0° and 9.0°. In
this study, the PRL-fovea distance was found to range between
0.75° and 14.2°, and we also found that both fixation stability
and reading speed reduced with increased distance between the
PRL and fovea. This is due to decreased retinal resolution and
sensitivity in the PRL region.33
The majority of studies evaluating patients with AMD report
that PRL is most frequently localized in the nasal and superior
quadrants.20,31 For instance, Erbezci and Öztürk found PRL
most frequently in the nasal quadrant (29.2%) and secondly in
the superior quadrant.31 Very similarly, we found that PRL was
most commonly present in the nasal quadrant (31%) and the
superior quadrant (26%) in our group of patients. Additionally,
studies show that patients with central scotoma most commonly
prefer the left visual field,10,20,31,34,38 followed by the inferior
visual field.20,31,34 Our results were also in agreement with the
majority of published literature.
There are several studies reporting an association between
reading speed and the localization of PRL.34,39 For instance,
there are studies suggesting that PRL localization in the superior
quadrant increases reading speed.20,38 However, others suggest
a lack of meaningful relationship.35,38,40 Conforming to the
latter group of studies, we also found relationship between
reading speed and PRL location. In agreement with our results,
Fletcher et al. also did not find a relationship between reading
speed and the localization of PRL. They concluded that, apart
from the patient’s cognitive capabilities, the fixation and saccadic movements of the eyes were the primary parameters that
were important for reading in patients with central scotoma.38
Restoring reading ability in people with low vision due to
central scotoma is one of the main goals of low-vision rehabilitation. Thus, in these cases, it is important to identify correctable or modifiable factors that influence reading speed the
most. The present study has found fixation stability to be
a modifiable factor that affects reading speed the most.
Studies using microperimetric biofeedback signals have been
shown to increase fixation stability and reading speed.41,42 It
was reported by Tarita-Nistor et al. that the most appropriate
PRL should be created in each patient, since their findings
demonstrated a speed increase of 38%, as well as increases in
reading acuity and CPS.41
The strength of this study is the analysis of a great number
of variables that may have been associated with or could have

influenced reading speed, including scotoma size, contrast test
and duration of reading interruption.
The number of participants included in the study and the
lack of contrast sensitivity measurement via a test standardized
for individuals with low-vision are limitations of the study. If
there indeed is a PRL location that influences reading speed, we
believe that larger studies which include additional analyses for
reading direction (left-to-right, top-to-bottom) may be valuable to elucidate the role of PRL localization on reading speed.

Conclusion
The reading speed of low-vision individuals with AMD is often
insufficient for fluent reading, and the recuperation of reading
speed is an important aspect of low-vision rehabilitation. Since
reading performance appears to be significantly affected by fixation stability in patients with AMD, near vision rehabilitation
therapies may benefit from the incorporation of methods such
as microperimetric biofeedback training to increase fixation
stability.

Highlights
● Reading speed is strongly associated with fixation stability.
● Reading speed is associated with a number of factors at varying
degrees.
● The presence for absolute foveal scotoma is more determinant in the
reading speed then the size of the scotoma.
● There is no relationship between reading speed and the retinal locus
preferred for fixation.
