Department of Ophthalmology, Dr. D. Y. Patil Medical College and Research Centre, Pune, Maharashtra, India

Abstract
Background: Age‑related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of irreversible central vision loss in older adults. Low vision
interventions (LVIs) aim to enhance residual visual function and promote independence. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical and functional
outcomes of structured LVIs on visual performance, daily functioning, and quality of life in individuals with AMD. Methods: A prospective
interventional study was conducted over 1 year at Dr. D. Y. Patil Medical College, Hospital, and Research Centre in Pune. Eighty‑four participants
with confirmed AMD and low vision were enrolled and assessed at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months postintervention. The rehabilitation
program included optical, nonoptical, and electronic assistive devices, along with specialized visual training. Primary outcomes included best
corrected visual acuity (BCVA), contrast sensitivity, and reading speed. Secondary outcomes were assessed using the National Eye Institute
Visual Function Questionnaire‑25 (NEI VFQ‑25) and the modified Barthel index, evaluating self‑reported visual function and independence
in daily activities. Results: Of the 84 participants, 78 completed the full follow‑up. Statistically significant improvements were observed
in BCVA (mean gain: 0.17 logMAR), reading speed (mean increase: 18.8 words/min), and contrast sensitivity (mean gain: 0.22 units). The
NEI VFQ‑25 composite score improved by an average of 17.3 points, with marked enhancement in near‑vision tasks and social engagement.
Functional ability, as measured by the Modified Barthel Index, also showed meaningful improvement. No device‑related complications were
reported, and more than 85% of participants adhered to their prescribed rehabilitation plans. Conclusions: Structured low vision rehabilitation
leads to significant improvements in visual performance, functional independence, and perceived quality of life among patients with AMD.
These findings support the integration of LVIs into standard ophthalmic care protocols for elderly individuals with low vision.
Keywords: Age‑related macular degeneration, assistive devices, low vision, quality of life, visual function, visual rehabilitation

Introduction
Age‑related macular degeneration (AMD) is one of the leading
causes of vision loss in individuals aged 60 years and older.
It is a chronic retinal disorder that significantly contributes
to global visual impairment and disability.[1] The disease is
primarily characterized by progressive degeneration of the
macula, resulting in impaired central vision. This loss affects
essential daily activities such as reading, driving, and facial
recognition.
The global burden of AMD is rising steadily, largely due to
increasing life expectancy. Current estimates suggest that the
prevalence of AMD could exceed 288 million individuals by
the year 2040.[1,2] In India, this burden is further compounded
by demographic shifts and increased exposure to risk factors
such as smoking, poor nutrition, and prolonged ultraviolet
light exposure.[3]
AMD age‑related macular degeneration is broadly classified
into two forms: Dry (nonexudative) and wet (exudative).
The dry form, which accounts for the majority of cases,
typically progresses slowly and is associated with drusen
accumulation and geographic atrophy. In contrast, the wet
form is characterized by choroidal neovascularization and is
associated with more rapid and severe vision loss.[4] While the
advent of antivascular endothelial growth factor (anti‑VEGF)
therapy has significantly improved outcomes in wet
AMD, effective treatments for dry AMD remain limited.[5]
Consequently, there is a growing emphasis on low vision
rehabilitation strategies to help patients preserve functional
independence and make the most of their remaining
vision. These interventions aim not only to enhance visual
performance but also to improve the quality of life in affected
individuals.
Low vision interventions (LVIs) are designed to optimize the
use of residual vision through personalized strategies such as
magnification devices, contrast‑enhancing tools, orientation
and mobility training, and environmental modifications.[6]
Multiple studies have demonstrated that structured low vision
rehabilitation significantly improves visual functioning,
enhances psychological well‑being, and reduces dependency
in patients with AMD.[7,8]
However, the real‑world implementation of LVIs, particularly
in developing countries like India, is limited by several barriers.
These include the lack of specialized rehabilitation services,
inadequate referral systems, low patient awareness, and
socioeconomic constraints.[9] Despite growing global evidence

supporting the efficacy of LVIs, there remains a paucity of
data from Indian tertiary care centers evaluating their impact
on AMD populations.
A comprehensive understanding of patient‑specific outcomes
is crucial to inform service integration and guide policy
development for vision rehabilitation. Therefore, the present
study was undertaken at Dr. D. Y. Patil Medical College,
Hospital and Research Center, Pimpri, Pune, over 1 year, with
the objective of evaluating the clinical and functional outcomes
of LVIs in individuals diagnosed with AMD. The primary
aim was to assess improvements in visual task performance,
autonomy in daily living, and overall patient satisfaction
following tailored rehabilitation protocols administered in a
structured clinical environment.

Methods
Study design and setting

This prospective interventional study was conducted
from January to December 2024 by the Department of
Ophthalmology at Dr. D. Y. Patil Medical College, Hospital and
Research Center, Pimpri, Pune. Ethical clearance was obtained
from the Institutional Ethics Committee prior to the initiation
of the study. All participants provided written informed consent
in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

A total of 84 participants were recruited from the outpatient
ophthalmology clinic. All patients were clinically diagnosed
with AMD and categorized as having low vision. Inclusion
criteria were: Age ≥60 years, a documented history of chronic
ocular disease for at least 3 months, and best corrected visual
acuity (BCVA) in the better‑seeing eye ranging from 6/18
to <3/60 (as per World Health Organization definition of low
vision). Exclusion criteria included severe glaucoma, diabetic
retinopathy, widespread cognitive impairment, and intraocular
surgical procedure inside the preceding 3 months.

Baseline assessment

This included a thorough ocular examination, fundus exaination
and the use of indirect ophthalmoscopy and spectral‑area
optical coherence tomography, the measurement of Best
corrected visual acuity (BCVA) the usage of ETDRS charts, the
assessment of contrast sensitivity the usage of the Pelli‑Robson
chart, and Amsler grid trying out.[10] The 15‑object Geriatric

Low vision intervention protocol

Participants received a personalized low vision rehabilitation
program tailored to their specific visual goals and functional
limitations. The intervention comprised four key components.

Optical aids

Various optical devices were prescribed based on individual
needs, including dome magnifiers, high‑plus reading
spectacles, handheld telescopes, and stand magnifiers.

Nonoptical aids

Supportive nonoptical tools were incorporated to improve
task performance. These included high‑intensity task lighting,
reading stands, tactile markers, and large‑print reading materials.

Electronic devices

Advanced electronic aids were introduced for patients with
moderate to severe visual impairment. These included portable
video magnifiers and closed‑circuit television systems with
customizable magnification and contrast settings to enhance
visual accessibility.

Training modules

Each participant underwent a structured training program
consisting of four supervised sessions spread over 8 weeks.
The sessions were led by certified low vision rehabilitation
professionals and focused on: These include the proper
handling and use of prescribed low-vision devices, the
implementation of eccentric-viewing strategies, and the
development of adaptive techniques for performing activities
of daily living (ALsD).

Outcome measures

The main effects of the have a look at have been upgrades
in analyzing pace, contrast sensitivity, and best‑corrected
visual acuity (BCVA), as measured through Minnesota Low
Vision Reading Test (MNREAD) charts 3 and 6 months
after the intervention.[12] Functional independence and visual
characteristics as stated by the patient, have been the primary
and secondary goals. The National Eye Institute Visual
Function Questionnaire-25 (NEI VFQ-25) was used to evaluate
self-reported visual function.[13] The Modified Barthel Index
was used to assess independence in activities of daily living
(ADLs).[14]

Results
Participant characteristics

Out of the 84 individuals enrolled in the study, 78 participants
(92.9%) successfully completed the 6‑month follow‑up.
Attrition was due to relocation (n = 3), concurrent medical
illness (n = 2), or voluntary withdrawal (n = 1). The cohort
had a mean age of 68.4 ± 5.9 years (range: 61–81 years), with
a slight male predominance (53.8%). The majority (67.9%)
was retired, and 58.9% had attained at least secondary‑level
education. Clinically, 62.8% (n = 49) presented with dry AMD,
while the remaining 37.2% (n = 29) had stable neovascular
AMD managed with anti‑VEGF therapy. Common systemic

Visual acuity and reading performance

Implementation of low vision rehabilitation protocols yielded
notable improvements in visual outcomes. At baseline, the
mean best‑corrected visual acuity (BCVA) in the better‑seeing
eye was recorded as 0.82 ± 0.21 logMAR. Postintervention,
a statistically significant improvement was observed, with
BCVA improving to 0.72 ± 0.20 logMAR at 3 months and
further to 0.65 ± 0.19 logMAR at 6 months (P < 0.001). These
findings suggest enhanced visual clarity facilitated by tailored
assistive devices and structured training sessions. While
the improvements were more pronounced in cases with dry
AMD, statistically meaningful gains were also documented in
participants with stabilized wet AMD.
Reading performance also demonstrated considerable
enhancement. The mean reading speed improved from a
baseline of 45.3 ± 9.7 words per minute (wpm) to 57.8 ± 10.2
wpm at 3 months and 64.1 ± 11.5 wpm at 6 months (P < 0.001).
These outcomes, derived from MNREAD chart assessments,
reflect improved reading efficiency following consistent use
of magnifiers and electronic visual aids. Corresponding data
are detailed in Table 2.

Contrast sensitivity and central field function

Throughout the trial, there was a noticeable improvement in
contrast sensitivity as determined by the Pelli‑Robson chart.
From 1.02 ± 0.18 at baseline to 1.14 ± 0.17 at 3 months and then
to 1.24 ± 0.16 at 6 months, the mean score rose (P = 0.002).

Subjective visual function and quality of life

Discussion

The National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire‑25
(NEI VFQ‑25) was used to document notable improvements
in vision‑related quality of life. At the 3‑month mark, the
composite score increased from 53.6 ± 8.4 to 63.2 ± 8.9, and
at the 6‑month mark, it reached 70.9 ± 9.3 (P < 0.001). The
most significant improvements were found in near vision
activities (mean increase: +19.5 points), social engagement
(+13.2 points), and dependency reduction, according to
subscale analysis. The wider effects of low vision rehabilitation
on visual ability and psychological health are reflected in these
gains [Table 3].

Independence in activities of daily living

Using the Modified Barthel Index, the mean independence
score improved significantly from 68.5 ± 7.2 at baseline to
75.6 ± 7.0 at 3 months and 81.7 ± 6.8 at 6 months (P < 0.001).
The largest improvements were observed in tasks requiring
fine vision, such as grooming, dressing, and indoor mobility.
At the end of the study, 64.1% of participants achieved scores
indicating mild or no dependency, compared to 38.5% at
baseline [Table 4].

Device utilization and adherence

At 6 months, 85.9% of patients reported regular use
(≥4 days/week) of at least one low vision device. Among them,
72.4% used a combination of two or more aids (e.g., high‑plus
spectacles for near tasks and video magnifiers for extended
reading). Electronic devices were most favored by participants
under 70 years of age, while older patients preferred optical
aids. Compliance was positively associated with higher
education level and baseline VFQ‑25 scores. No adverse

The present study highlights the clinical relevance and
measurable benefits of structured LVIs in patients with AMD,
particularly in a resource‑constrained, real‑world Indian
healthcare setting. The interventions led to statistically and
clinically significant improvements in best corrected visual
acuity (BCVA), reading speed, contrast sensitivity, and quality
of life. These outcomes support the efficacy of personalized
rehabilitation strategies and are consistent with findings
reported in prior international studies.[7,8]
Beyond objective visual improvements, this study contributes
to the growing body of evidence supporting the psychosocial
and functional benefits of LVIs. The significant gains seen
in both composite and subscale scores of the NEI VFQ‑25
reflect not only improved visual performance but also
reduced dependency and enhanced social engagement. These
findings are consistent with those of Lamoureux et al., who
demonstrated the positive impact of LVIs on psychosocial
well‑being and patient satisfaction.[7]
Marked enhancements in BCVA and reading speed underscore
the value of device-based visual training. These observations
are in line with the findings of Stelmack et al., who reported
significant improvements in visual function and performance
in daily tasks among veterans undergoing similar rehabilitation
protocols.[6]
The role of reading aids in improving functional vision and
reading ability in individuals with low vision has also been
well established in systematic reviews.[15] Similar benefits
in reading rehabilitation using low vision devices have been
reported among veterans.[16]
Our results also affirm that combining both optical and
electronic assistive technologies offers a synergistic advantage,
especially when tailored to each patient’s cognitive and
functional capacities.[16]
Reading speed, an important indicator of functional vision, was
significantly improved in our study and is known to be reliably
assessed using standardized tools such as the MNREAD test
in patients with age-related macular degeneration.[17]

Moreover, improvements in ADLs, as measured by the
Modified Barthel Index, highlight the practical value of these
interventions in enhancing patient autonomy and reducing
caregiver burden–an aspect often under‑recognized in
conventional AMD management strategies.
Despite the encouraging results, certain limitations must be
acknowledged. The absence of a control group limits the ability
to draw definitive causal inferences, underscoring the need for
future randomized controlled trials to validate these findings.
In addition, the reliance on self‑reported data for device usage
introduces the potential for reporting bias. Nevertheless,
within these limitations, the study provides valuable insight
into the feasibility, effectiveness, and clinical applicability of
structured low vision rehabilitation programs in tertiary care
settings across India.

Conclusiion
Structured low vision rehabilitation leads to significant
improvements in visual performance, functional independence,
and perceived quality of life among patients with AMD. These
findings support the integration of low vision interventions
into standard ophthalmic care protocols for elderly individuals
with low vision.
