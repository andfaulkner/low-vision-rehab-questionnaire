Prevalence of Visual Impairment and Utilization
of Rehabilitation Services in the Visually
Impaired Elderly Population of Quebec
JACQUES GRESSET, OD, PhD and MONA BAUMGARTEN, PhD
University of Montreal, School of Optometry, Montreal, Quebec, Canada (JG), Department of Epidemiology and Preventive Medicine,
Division of Gerontology, University of Maryland, Baltimore, Maryland (MB)

ABSTRACT: Data on the prevalence of reported visual impairment and on the utilization of rehabilitation services were
collected on a sample consisting of 1777 community-residing people aged at least 65 years. A visual disability was
considered to be present if the answer to at least one of the following two questions was positive: Do you have trouble
reading ordinary newsprint with glasses (if normally worn)? Do you have trouble clearly seeing the face of someone 12
feet away with glasses (if normally worn)? Prevalence of a reported near disability was 7.6%, prevalence of a reported
distance disability was 4.4%, and 3.5% of subjects reported both types of disability. In a subsample of the surveyed
population, the positive predictive value was 21% and the negative predictive value was 100%, using moderate or
worse visual impairment as the gold standard. Among those answering yes to both questions, 11.4% received services
from a rehabilitation center and 10.0% from a nonprofit agency. The utilization rates (adjusted to apply only to those
whose visual impairment was confirmed by visual examination) reached 20% for rehabilitation centers and 17.5% for
nonprofit agencies. Low utilization of rehabilitation services raises questions concerning the role of general eye care
practitioners, community-based health centers, and rehabilitation centers in the rehabilitative process of the visually
impaired elderly. (Optom Vis Sci 2002;79:416–423)
Key Words: prevalence, visual impairment, rehabilitation services, elderly population

I

nformation on the prevalence of blindness and visual impairment is critical for quantifying the need for care and rehabilitation services for the visually disabled. Population-based studies of visual impairment and ocular disorders are the most valid
method for determining prevalence. Unfortunately, oculo-visual
assessments of large general populations are very costly and time
consuming. As an alternative to examination studies, national
agencies such as the National Center for Health Statistics in the
United States and Statistics Canada regularly use questionnairebased surveys of large samples to gather statistics on health and
disability status.1–3 Recent data on reported visual impairment
published by the National Center for Health Statistics and Statistics Canada, together with those from examination studies on clinically assessed visual impairment, indicate that most individuals
identified as visually disabled are elderly.1–5 The elderly subgroup
of the visually impaired population has been identified as being
underserved.6
Visually impaired elderly may receive rehabilitation services
from different and sometimes multiple sources. In Canada, these

sources include rehabilitation centers, private and hospital-based
low-vision clinics, and nonprofit agencies.7 In the province of
Quebec, rehabilitation centers provide a full complement of services funded by the provincial Ministry of Health and Social Services. These centers were established to serve visually impaired
individuals and are geographically located to meet the needs of the
population. Rehabilitation services are provided free of charge for
all individuals who meet the International Classification of Diseases (9th revision) definition of visual handicap,8 whereas rehabilitative aids may be loaned or purchased at low cost.9 In Quebec,
nonprofit agencies, such as the Canadian Institute for the Blind,
are mainly involved in advocacy activities, job placement, and
cultural and leisure activities and, in contrast to most of the other
provinces of Canada, not at all in low-vision and rehabilitation
services. The private or hospital-based low-vision clinics offer clinical assessment but not the full scope of rehabilitative services.
Prescription of low-vision devices is offered, but patients must pay
for the required visual aids. The International Classification of
Diseases (9th revision) criteria are also used by the nonprofit agen-

cies, but other low-vision clinics may have less stringent access

services for the visually impaired has risen during the last decade in
Canada, the proportion of users is still very low compared with the
prevalence of individuals reporting visual disability estimated from
Statistics Canada surveys.10, 11 Furthermore, the proportion of visually impaired elderly who use rehabilitation services is not well
documented.6, 12, 13 To determine what proportion of visually impaired elderly use rehabilitation services, a two-stage research
project was implemented. The purpose of the first stage was to
collect data on the prevalence of reported visual disability and on
the utilization of rehabilitation services in a large sample of community-residing people aged at least 65 years. The purpose of the
second stage was to estimate the sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values of two survey questions using the results of a visual
examination as the gold standard.

METHODS
The first stage of the study was part of a large survey on health
and aging in Canada, the Canadian Study of Health and Aging
(CSHA), which took place from 1991 to 1992. Study methods for
the CSHA have been described elsewhere.14 Briefly, sampling areas
were identified in the vicinity of each of the 18 research centers
participating in the CSHA. A random sample of individuals aged
at least 65 years was drawn for each selected area, with predetermined sampling fractions for age and sex categories. Individuals
aged 75 to 84 years and aged 85 years or older were oversampled.
There were four research centers in Quebec. The community
sample in Quebec was made up of 1800 persons aged at least 65
years. Data on visual impairment were collected during a home
interview carried out as part of the CSHA study. As defined in
previous Statistics Canada surveys such as the Health and Activity
Limitations Survey (HALS),2 a visual disability was considered to
be present if the answer to at least one of the following two questions was positive:
• Do you have trouble reading ordinary newsprint with glasses or
contact lenses if normally worn?
• Do you have trouble seeing clearly the face of someone 12 feet
away with glasses or contact lenses if normally worn?
If the answer was positive to at least one of these two questions,
subjects were asked whether they received services for visual impairment from either a rehabilitation center, a low-vision clinic
other than in a rehabilitation center (e.g., in a hospital or in the
private office of an eye care practitioner), or a nonprofit agency that
serves the visually impaired (e.g., Canadian National Institute for
the Blind). Prevalence and 95% confidence intervals were computed for each sex and age category and for the whole study population, taking into account the sampling design.15 Whenever the
conditions for computation of the usual confidence interval for a
proportion were not respected, a binomial 95% confidence interval for small samples was used.16 Weighted prevalences were calculated to correct for the discrepancy between the proportion of
subjects included in the study sample in the different sex and age
categories and the corresponding proportion of people in the population of Quebec.

In the second stage of the study, we assessed the validity of the
questionnaire in a subsample of our study group. Among subjects
in the Montreal area not eligible for other components of the
CSHA, all positive subjects, along with a random sample of negative subjects, were selected to undergo a visual examination at
home. Subjects were considered positive if they answered “yes” to
at least one of the two screening questions. The examination included measurement of visual acuity (VA), visual fields, retinoscopy, subjective refraction, aplanation tonometry, and funduscopy. Distance VA was measured at 4 m using the Early Treatment
Diabetic Retinopathy Survey charts17 in a back-lighted box.18
Near VA was measured at 0.4 m with a modified near Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Survey chart. Visual fields were assessed with a 0.333-m radius portable arc-perimeter and a 3-mm
white index. VA was measured twice: first with the usual correction, if any, worn by the subject (presenting VA)5 and again with
the best correction as determined by refraction (best-corrected
VA). The first author (JG), an optometrist who was not aware of
the subjects’ questionnaire responses at the time of the examination, carried out the visual assessment.
Subjects were classified according to the VA and visual field in
their best eye into the International Classification of Diseases (9th
revision) categories of visual impairment.8 Individuals with a best
VA between 6/12 and 6/18 were considered to have a mild visual
impairment,5 individuals with a best VA between 6/21 and 6/54
were considered to have a moderate visual impairment, and subjects having a VA ⱕ 6/60 or a visual field ⬍ 20° were considered to
have a severe visual impairment.8 The classification was done
twice: the definition using the presenting VA is referred to as the
“functional visual impairment,”19 and the other, with the best corrected VA, is referred to as the “visual impairment.” Using the
resulting classification as the diagnostic standard and the results of
the questionnaire as the test to be validated, the subjects were
reclassified as true positives (a), false positives (b), false negatives
(c), and true negatives (d). Crude sensitivity, Sn ⫽ a/(a ⫹ c), and
crude specificity, Sp ⫽ d/(b ⫹ d), of the questionnaire were computed, and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were determined from the binomial 95% CI tables for small samples.20
Because the sampling fraction for subjects with a positive questionnaire result was different from the sampling fraction for subjects
with a negative questionnaire result, the sensitivity and specificity
estimated in this study cannot be applied to an unselected population. The positive predictive value, PPV ⫽ a/(a ⫹ b), and the
negative predictive value, NPV ⫽ d/(c ⫹ d), were also computed.
The adjusted prevalence (Pr) of visual impairment was computed
according to the formula proposed by Choi21: Pr ⫽ [(m1)PPV] ⫹
[(m0)(1 ⫺ NPV)]/(m1 ⫹ m0), where m1 is the number with positive tests and m0 the number with negative tests in the unselected
population. Results were computed using four different diagnostic
criteria and two different test criteria.

Diagnostic Criteria
• Mild functional impairment or worse (presenting near or distance VA ⱕ 6/12) versus no functional impairment (presenting
near or distance VA ⬎ 6/12).
• Moderate functional impairment or worse (presenting near or
distance VA ⱕ 6/21) versus no functional impairment or mild

RESULTS
In the first stage, 1777 individuals (98.7% of the eligible subjects), including 1106 women and 671 men, completed the questionnaire on reported visual disability. The prevalence of disability,
according to the HALS Statistics Canada survey questions,2 is
shown in Table 1. For both near- and distance-vision complaints,
the prevalence rose with age. The prevalence was higher in women
relative to men in the younger age groups and higher in men than
in women for the older age groups for both near and distance visual
impairment, although this difference was not statistically significant based on an examination of the respective confidence intervals. The prevalence in the whole population of at least 65 years of
age, when weighted to remove the effect of the differing sampling

fractions, was 7.6% for near-vision impairment and 4.4% for impairment in distance vision. The prevalence of reported near-vision
impairment was significantly higher than the prevalence of reported impairment for distance vision. When considering the
prevalence of reported visual impairment according to the number
of positive responses (Table 2), similar trends were observed in
prevalence according to age and sex categories. After adjustment to
remove the effect of the differing sampling fractions, the prevalence in the whole population of at least 65 years of age was 8.5%
according to the Statistics Canada criteria and 3.5% for both nearand distance-vision impairment. These two proportions represent,
respectively, about 60,100 and 24,800 individuals in the Quebec
population. Table 3 shows the use of services by elderly persons
aged at least 65 years who reported visual impairment. The services
of private and hospital-based low-vision clinics were the most frequently used.
Among the 144 subjects eligible for the second stage of the
study, 31 were lost to follow-up and 42 refused to participate. We
were unable to schedule an eye examination before the end of the
study for six of the remaining 71 subjects. Among the 65 subjects
who received the visual examination, 19 had at least one positive
response to the questionnaire, and 46 had two negative responses.
The final participation rate was 45.1%, 40.4% among those with a
positive questionnaire and 47.4% among those with a negative
questionnaire. Participating and nonparticipating subjects did not
differ with respect to age or visual disability status. However, the
proportion of nonparticipants was larger among women than men
(62% versus 41%, respectively). After refraction, only two subjects
changed from mild visual impairment to no visual impairment.
Hence, the results presented in the following tables are restricted to
only functional impairment. The prevalence of mild functional
near visual impairment or worse was 30.8%, whereas the prevalence of moderate functional near visual impairment or worse was

There was a higher prevalence of problems in reading ordinary
newsprint than of problems in seeing the face of someone 12 feet
away. This finding is consistent with other studies3 and suggests a
hierarchy in task difficulty levels: to be able to read ordinary newsprint, an individual has to present a VA of at least 6/15. On the
other hand, both reading and face recognition are contrast dependent.31 However, because faces subtend a rather larger visual angle
than ordinary newsprint, face recognition might be considered a
more robust task.31 Consequently, individuals reporting both visual disabilities are probably more seriously affected than those
reporting only one. This assertion is also corroborated by the fact
that individuals with two positive responses use rehabilitation or
nonprofit agencies services more frequently than those with only
one positive response (Table 3). Overall, the proportion of subjects
who used rehabilitation services in this study was low. Yet, we
found that among individuals who use at least one service, many
use more than one. The utilization of rehabilitation centers and
nonprofit agencies that offer visual aids at no cost or low cost was
particularly low. Although the proportion of individuals reporting
the use of low-vision clinics in hospitals or private offices was
higher, this may simply reflect the respondent’s confusion of low vision services with other ophthalmologic subspecialties because
the utilization rate was identical among those with one and those
with two positives responses.
When visual impairment was considered to be present if one
response was positive (as proposed by Statistics Canada), we obtained a high NPV, indicating that a large proportion of individuals with a negative test are truly unimpaired. On the other hand,
the PPV was low: only 21% of those with a positive test had

moderate functional impairment or worse. In this sample, there
was little difference in the prevalence of functional visual impairment between individuals with disability at distance and those with
both near- and far-distance disability. The use of either the distance
or the distance and near visual disability criteria resulted in an
increase in the PPV of the questionnaire without markedly decreasing the NPV. However, in the case of moderate visual impairment, the estimated PPV was still quite low (57%).
If we assume that the estimates of positive and negative predictive value apply equally to those who refused the visual examination as to those who received it, we may use them to estimate the
prevalence of true visual impairment in the population of Quebec
aged at least 65 years in 1992. The adjusted prevalence of mild
functional impairment and worse was not very different from the
prevalence of reported visual impairment based on a criterion of at
least one positive response on the questionnaire (9.5% versus
8.5%, respectively). Similarly, the adjusted prevalence of moderate
visual impairment and worse did not differ much from the value
obtained using a criterion of two positive responses (2.6% versus
3.5%).
The study had several limitations. First, the participation rate in
the validation component was low. This may have been due to the
long interval (approximately 6 months) before recontacting the
subjects. Nonresponse bias (e.g., differential participation according to gender), therefore, may be an issue in this study. Other
selection biases might be responsible for the unusual absence of
differences in the levels of presenting acuity and best-corrected
acuity among the examined subjects. Here, the low level of participation in the second stage of the study may have introduced a bias
toward the enrollment of individuals with appropriate ophthalmic
correction. Also, the relatively small number of subjects resulted in
very wide confidence intervals for some of the estimates.
The question regarding the utilization of rehabilitation services
was only asked of individuals with at least one visual disability
complaint. It is highly unlikely that individuals who answered
negatively to both questions on the visual screening questionnaire
would use visual rehabilitation services. From the corrected utilization rate of rehabilitation services, the number of individuals
using rehabilitation centers or nonprofit agencies in Quebec can be
estimated to be around 5000 persons for 1992. This finding is
consistent with registration numbers in the rehabilitation centers
and the nonprofit agencies throughout Quebec at the time of this
study.10, 32
In this study, we estimated the predictive values and misclassification rates of the HALS survey questions to be able to derive the
prevalence of visually impairment and blindness. Our results may
seem to be in contradiction with the results of other investigators.29, 30 For Elliott et al.29 the prevalence of low vision and blindness could not be inferred from the results of the HALS survey
because they did not find a direct relationship between the HALS
results and the number of registrants at Ontario low-vision clinics
and the Canadian Institute for the Blind. We agree with these
authors29, 30 that questionnaire-based results overestimate the true
prevalence of visual impairment. Nevertheless, our results suggest
that with knowledge about the properties of the survey questions,
the results of the questionnaire survey might be used to estimate
the prevalence of visual impairment.

registered with the Royal National Institute for the Blind.33 Low
participation rates may be attributed to a variety of factors. First,
there may be a lack of awareness in the population concerning the
difference between pathologic visual decline and visual decline
related to aging.34 Seniors (as well as health care professionals) may
have low expectations regarding the vision of elders,35, 36 referrals
may involve long waiting times,36 both elders and professionals
may have little knowledge about available services,37, 38 and elderly
persons may not adhere to the recommendations of their
ophthalmologist.39
Rehabilitation centers were originally established to preserve the
full independence of visually impaired individuals by helping them
to cope with visual loss and by reducing their disability and handicap situations. In the elderly population, rehabilitation services
may preserve independence and prevent the need for institutionalization. This may imply a better quality of life for these individuals and a reduced cost for society as a whole.40 An unknown
percentage of individuals who report visual disability who undergo
surgical or medical treatment of their visual problem can be expected to no longer require rehabilitation services.30 However, the
low utilization of rehabilitation services observed in this study
raises the need to address the respective role of general eye care
practitioners, community-based health centers, and rehabilitation
centers in the rehabilitative process of visually impaired elderly
individuals.
