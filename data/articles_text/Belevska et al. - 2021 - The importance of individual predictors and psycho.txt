The importance of the individual predictors and the psychosocial
working conditions in assessing the Work Ability Index of people with
low vision

Abstract
Purpose: This investigation was aimed at clarifying the importance of the
individual predictors and psychosocial working conditions in estimating the
Work Ability Index (WAI) of people with impaired vision.
Materials and methods: This study utilized a quantitative cross-sectional design
involving 450 respondents who had low vision with visual acuity in the range of
0.05–0.3 and 150 respondents with visual acuity in the range of 0.5–0.3 as a
control group. The investigation was conducted using the standardized
instruments Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQ) and WAI.
Results: The average WAI score of the respondents with low vision was
30.1 ± 4.1 and that of the control group was 34.7 ± 3.4. Results indicated a
positive COPSOQ/WAI correlation in the following domains: work importance,
career prospects, rewards/feedback, role and the definition, quality of
management, social support, job satisfaction, and health condition. Negative
correlations were indicated in the following domains: quantitative, cognitive, and
emotional demands; exhaustion; and stress.
Conclusions: The WAI of people with low vision is both positively and
negatively impacted via different intensities associated with complex
correlations, including gender; age; profession; stress propensity; quantitative,
qualitative, and emotional demands of work; specificity of work, professional
education; and the presence of stressors.

Keywords: individual predictors; psychosocial conditions; Work Ability Index;
people with low vision

1. INTRODUCTION
Vision impairment represents a clinical stage in the development of many eye diseases
in which the terminal stage can evolve into a severe degree of visual impairment. This
represents not only an ophthalmologic but also a sociological problem for the people
affected [1]. Preserved eyesight is one of the main preconditions for the general
population to function normally, which is the reason behind very visual impairment
significantly affecting the quality of life, workability, and work performance of those
affected regardless of their socio demographic characteristics and occupations [2].
Therefore, impaired vision is a significant contributor to morbidity and is strongly
correlated with psychosocial and other conditions of the work environment. In addition,
the visual degradation process significantly affects the workability, choice of profession,
employment opportunities, work productivity, and assessment of the achieved results of
people with low vision [3–5].
Workability is the ability of an individual to perform any type of work that can
be valued, the ability of an organism to maintain an internal physiological balance
during and after the completion of the activity, and the ability to quickly and thoroughly
establish all the forms of physiological homeostasis that deviated from the normal
values during work. In what is different from the case of those with preserved vision,
whose workability is determined by the functioning of various organ systems and
depends on the health, physical, and sensory ability of the organism, determining the
workability of a person with low vision is a complex problem. Particularly, in addition
to medical problems, many times, these impairments result in legal provisions,
including driving restrictions. The Work Ability Index (WAI) is used universally to
assess the workability of an individual [6,7].

4


[page 6]
Healthy vision is fundamentally involved in various work processes and is
particularly apparent in professions that require a high degree of visual preservation [8–
10]. Few reports attempt to identify the predictors of the work environment that are
most important and find out the extent to which each influences the preservation of
workability (i.e., in the valorization of the WAI of people with low vision). This further
emphasizes the need to define factors that affect workability, where by today's emphasis
is placed not only on the health status of the people and the singularity of the job and the
workload but also on the complex interrelated psychosocial conditions of the work
environment that play the role of predictor and are crucially important in the assessment
of the WAI.
Assessing workability determines the compatibility of the biological potentials
and functions of an individual's organism with the requirements of a job and the
characteristics of the work conditions. In everyday practice, workability can be
expressed through the WAI [11–13]. Factors such as gender, age, profession, health
status, individual and contextual personality traits, and psychosocial conditions at work
affect the WAI scores [14–18].
Taking the large gaps in the literature regarding this topic as given, this
investigation was aimed at clarifying the complex correlations, effects, and
consequences of the influence of the mentioned factors on the workability of people
with low vision from various professions. Moreover, we sought to determine the
psychosocial conditions and other factors involved in the process of work that have the
significance of a predictor, as well as the manner in which they affect the WAI of
people with low vision. While researching the psychosocial factors, we analyzed the
following parameters that affect the WAI: job requirements, work organization,
singularity of the job and the workload, level of expertise and education, quality of

5


[page 7]
management, general health status, individual and contextual personality traits, and
management team. We have grouped these parameters into four dimensions: Job
requirements; work organization and career prospect; relationships of employees with
their superiors in the work process; and the effects/consequences of the psychosocial
predictors in the work process.
The literature was searched thoroughly, and we use reference material and preknowledge, coordinated and supplemented by the results and findings obtained from
several relevant studies [19,20].
Results from this investigation contribute to the illumination of the complex
associations and individual, contextual, and psychosocial relationships to the
workability of people with low vision who are involved in the work process, expressed
through the WAI.

2. SUBJECTS AND METHODS
2.1. Study Design
This investigation utilized a cross-sectional study design with a quantitative analytical
approach.

2.2. Subjects
Six hundred respondents were involved in the present study: 450 were people with low
vision, such as those with glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, and ophthalmic diseases with
refractory anomalies and with preserved visual acuity of 0.05–0.03, and 150 people with
preserved visual acuity of 0.5–0.3 were in the control group, according to the ICD-10
classification. The choice of respondents was based on inclusion and exclusion criteria

6


[page 8]
related to age, the degree of visual impairment, and type of ophthalmic disease.
Inclusion into the low vision group required low preservation of visual acuity, in the
range of 0.05–0.3, and the control group required preservation of visual acuity of 0.5–
0.3. The participant’s age was required to be above 25 years old, with the condition that
the low vision was caused by ophthalmological diseases such as glaucoma, diabetic
retinopathy, and refractory anomalies.

2.3. Methods
Among the respondents involved in the study, visual acuity was determined by clinical
ophthalmologic examination using appropriate ophthalmic equipment, which
determined visual acuity at a distance and in proximity and the degree of visual
preservation according to the ICD-10 classification.
The assessment of the impact of psychosocial conditions of the work process on
the WAI of the respondents was conducted using the National Center for the Work
Environment's Copenhagen Psychosocial Questionnaire (COPSOQ) and the WAI from
the Finnish Institute of Occupational Health (FIOH). Both questionnaires have been
designed and accepted in many European countries as standardized questionnaires in
scientific research. All data were collected by trained examiners, and the respondent's
involvement was voluntary. The anonymity and confidentiality of the answers were
guaranteed, and the internal credibility and consistency of the data were tested with
Cronbach's alpha coefficients.
COPSOQ has been designed and accepted as a standardized measuring
instrument around the world and is used to study and follow the connection and impact
of psychosocial factors of the work environment on workability. In the present
7


[page 9]
investigation, we used a COPSOQ variant consisting of 23 domains (questions), 17 of
which are dual, divided into five dimensions, and for each answer to set domains
(questions), an adequate score of 1–5 points was calculated. The analysis of the scores
obtained through the implementation of this test enabled us to derive basic
understanding and arrive at conclusions regarding the connection and the impact of the
psychological factors of the work environment on workability and risk assessment at
work. Further more considering adequate measures and interventions for their
elimination, this technique improves the work conditions and health preservation of
employees and the prevention of morbid conditions [21,22].
The WAI questionnaire is a standardized questionnaire created by the FIOH and
is used to assess the WAI in everyday life practice and scientific research. This test can
identify the risk factors that affect workability; however, it can also be used as a
selection method for the assessment of the results of intervention measures applied and
as a program for detecting the influence of negative factors that affect the reduction of
workability, with the aim of improving individual workability and the health of
employees of various professions.
The WAI questionnaire consists of seven dimensions—related to self-assessed
current workability; workability in relation to job requirements; number of current
illnesses; assessment of inability to work due to illnesses; sick leave during the 12month period; self-assessment of workability for 2 years; and psychic abilities composed of one, two, or three domains (questions), and for each of the answers, 0–5
points are given. The total WAI cumulative score can range from 7 to 49 points. The
assessment of the WAI was done by the calculated score of the points obtained, and if
the total score of the answer was in the range of 7–27 points, the WAI score was

8


[page 10]
considered to be poor, 28–36 points signify a moderate WAI score, 37–43 points
indicated a good WAI score, and 44–49 points denoted an excellent WAI score [23,24].

2.4. Statistical Analysis
All data were processed using a descriptive and comparative statistics method, using
statistical program SPSS 17.0. The attributive (qualitative) series were analyzed by
determining the coefficients of ratio, proportion, and rate and were presented in absolute
and relative numbers. Numerical (quantitative) series were analyzed by finding the
measures of central tendency (average value; median, minimum, and maximum value;
and interactive range) and dispersion measures (standard deviation and standard error).
The Pearson χ2 test for homogeneity, Yates corrected test, and Fisher exact test
were used to determine the difference between attributive dichotomous features.
Pearson (r) correlation coefficient was used to determine the association between
numerical home group statistical series, and the Spearman correlation coefficient was
used to determine the association between numerical non homogeneous statistical
series.
In order to test the significance of the difference between the parameters
analyzed, depending on the type and distribution of data, Student´s t test for two
independent samples, ANOVA for multiple independent samples, and the
nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test for independent samples were used.
A univariate regression analysis was used to assess the impact of psychosocial
factors on the WAI, the coefficients of linear regression (B), as well as their 95%
confidence intervals (CI), were shown, and the evaluation of the statistical significance
of the coefficient value was completed using an unpaired t test. (Coefficients represent

[page 11]
changes in the WAI score that are caused by increasing the score of independent
variables for one measuring unit.)
Ap < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

4. DISCUSSION
Recent assessments of the workability of different categories of people depending on
the occupation indicate that the Work Ability Index can be used as an objective means
to evaluate workability and identify risk factors of reduced workability. Moreover, it is
suitable for assessing the effects of the applied intervention program and suggesting
measures for the elimination of unfavorable and risky work environment factors that
adversely affect workability. The WAI is also suitable as a valid identifier of applied
preventive programs for the promotion of occupational health [12,18,23,25].
Vision impairment is an ophthalmologic condition that has multi-causal
etiology, has complex pathogenesis, and can evolve into severe forms of damage and
definite loss of vision, where upon it becomes a handicap that affects not only the
quality of life but also the ability to work [1].
People with vision impairment, in addition to other adverse effects, are exposed
to continuous stress, unfavorable psychosocial conditions in all organizational stages of
the work process, frequent mobbing, and discrimination during work, all of which
reflect on their workability and productivity [16,26].
In 34 low vision and 9 control group respondents (7.16%), mobbing was
conducted in the form of intimidation or another type of discrimination during work.
Khoo and associates [27] stated that during working hours, people with disabilities were
very often under constant stress not only because of the particularities of their work but
also because of the tendency to be very often discriminated against and marginalized by
colleagues and the surroundings in general. Sanders [28] and Alavinia [29], in similar
15


[page 17]
investigations, emphasized the subjective, emotional, and contextual characteristics of
the personality of the disabled as an additional negative factor in their self-assessment
of health and productive potentials. Both investigations are in accordance with our
findings. Several previous investigations conducted with people from different
professions indicated that workability, in parallel with the function of eyesight, was
affected by other factors. However, they do not fully clarify the complex relationships
of specific individual characteristics and the intensity of perceptions of different
relationships and actions, particularly psychosocial factors in the work process[30].
The WAI scores of the low vision group, when compared with the scores of the
control group respondents, were significantly different from the low vision/control
groups in favor of the better workability of control group respondents than those with
low vision.
Comparisons of the WAI results with similar parallel scientific studies suggest
that all respondents with low vision involved in our research obtained a significantly
lower average WAI score than that obtained by respondents with similar socio
demographic characteristics but with preserved vision. Lavasani[31], in a study carried
out on 275 employees with various disabilities in Malaysia, claimed that 29.5% had
poor workability, 35.3% had moderate workability, 28.7% had good workability, and
18% had excellent workability.
We observed that respondents aged 20–50 years old have better workability
when compared with subjects over the age of 50 years, with a statistically significant
difference, including both low vision and control group respondents. In previous studies
on different aspects of age and workability, several authors refer to the existence of a
negative correlation between workability and age [32–35].

16


[page 18]
There was a statistically significant difference between the low vision and
control group male and female respondents in favor of the better workability of male
respondents when compared with female ones. The Work Ability Index of male and
female participants of the low vision group and the control group, with age, showed
reduced scores, which, for male and female respondents over the age of 50 years, was
statistically significantly lower when compared with the WAI scores of both genders
younger than 50 years old. Van den Berg [36] in a study of multifactorial etiology of the
influence of the WAI scores on professional employees from different occupations
suggested that male respondents have a higher WAI score for two index points (4%)
when compared with the female ones. With regard to age, the WAI scores for
employees over 40 years of age were considered to decrease by four index points
(7.3%) when compared with younger employees. In his study, it is also evident that
each of the psychosocial factors regarding gender have different unique variables of
influence on workability.
Both Rotenberg [15] and Claudie Fernandes [37] reported that with both men
and women, there were differences in the WAI scores in relation to gender in that the
WAI scores in female employees were lower in relation to men and that the vulnerable
period in which there was a significant reduction in WAI scores of women was uniquely
expressed in the age groups of 25–34, 40–44, and 60–64 years old.
Using a Tukey post hoc analysis, we assessed the average WAI scores in relation
to the level of education and professional orientation of all the 450 respondents with low
vision and found a statistically significant difference in relation to the laborers/office
workers (r = 0.003) in favor of the better workability of office workers; laborers/farmers
(r = 0.005) in favor of better workability of laborers; laborers/others (r = 0.004) in favor
of the better workability of respondents from other professions; office workers/farmers

17


[page 19]
(r = 0.001) in favor of better workability of office workers; farmers/others (r = 0.003) in
favor of the better workability of respondents from other professions. Lavasani[31]
aimed at measuring the workability of employees with a disability and reported that
WAI for employees with primary education was 28.92 ± 8.36; with secondary
education, 30.99 ± 7.77; and with higher education, 41.11 ± 4.47. In similar research
focused on the workability of various categories of medical and nursing staff in
hospitals, Ehsanollah found that the WAI score was 38.25 ± 4.4 and that there were
WAI variations regarding the level of education, qualifications, and sub-professional
specifications [38].
In a survey conducted with employees in commercial activities, Van den Berg
[36] noted that the average WAI score was41.1 ± 5.1 and was greater than the average
WAI of employees in mentally demanding jobs (reported average of39 ±4.2 as
conducted by Tuomi [25]). Sjögren–Ronka [39] also emphasized the negative impact of
physical demands and stress factors on the workability of office workers employed in
various positions.
The influence of the psychosocial conditions of work environments on
workability in our study was assessed from 19 different aspects (domains), with
univariate regression analysis. It was observed that with low vision and control group
respondents, an increase in COPSOQ score for one was associated with an increase in
the WAI score with statistically significant difference (p < 0.001) in the domains of
work importance, career prospects, rewards/feedback, role and definition, quality of
management, social support, job satisfaction, and health status, which indicates that
improved conditions related to these domains favorably influence an increase in
workability and that it is necessary to try to maintain or improve their existing gradient.

18


[page 20]
In the domains of quantitative demands, cognitive demands, emotional demands,
and exhaustion and stress, an increase in COPSOQ score of one was associated with a
decline in the WAI score with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). This
result implies that people with low vision, particularly those with severe visual
impairment, should take preventive measures and avail of programs that will enable its
elimination. The measures taken will enable the improvement of workability and health
protection of people with low vision.
There was no significant impact of psychosocial factors on the WAI score in the
domains of workplace duties, predictability, employee–superior relationships, fairness,
reputation, and work/family conflicts, although, depending on the domain, there was a
negative/positive impact of the psychosocial conditions of the work environment on the
WAI.
In the interim, there are very few reference reports that address the problem of
workability of people with impaired vision. Moreover, detecting crucial psychosocial
determinants that play the role of predictor of workability and identifying the gradient at
which they have a positive or negative influence was challenging for us to try and
contribute to the illumination of this complex and exact problem. Our research results
were compared with the results of previous scientific studies conducted in different
countries of the world and relating to the influence of psychosocial conditions on the
WAI of other categories of healthy and disabled people from different professions. Van
den Berg [36] assessed the impact of psychosocial factors on employee health and
workability and found that workability was strongly associated with stress, the
organization of work processes, physical demands of work, the individual mental
perception of stress factors, and the possibility of their balancing by employees.

19


[page 21]
Edwards et al. [40], in a study on the effect of psychosocial factors on the
workability of nurses, emphasized the importance of factors such as physical overload,
employee/medical staff relationships, and bureaucratic management in performing work
tasks. Borritz[41] also showed the effect of physical exhaustion and overload on
individual potentials and managerial relationships in the work process.

5. CONCLUSIONS
Results from this investigation suggest the complicated influence of the psychosocial
aspects of work environments on the WAI of low vision and control group respondents.
Vision impairment in itself does not have a decisive importance in determining or
assessing the Work Ability Index. However, there exists a significant correlation with
the effects of psychosocial conditions of the work environment, such as the quantitative,
qualitative, and emotional demands of work; specificity of the type of profession;
vocational training and education; exposure and intensity of stressors at work; general
health condition and basic illnesses; and individual personal traits; among other
circumstances. Crucially, these are not merely related to the level of vision impairment,
suggesting that other factors are perhaps at play.
Timely consideration and detection of the psychosocial factors of the work
environment, which have a negative impact on the WAI, will enable adequate
preventive measures to be taken for their elimination and will contribute to not only the
