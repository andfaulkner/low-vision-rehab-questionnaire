Purpose: Evaluate the construct validity and describe sensitivity,
specificity and predictive value of two short charts of visual
acuity (VA) and examine whether these can identify and detect
signs of visual impairment among older people. Method:
The study included 43 persons, >65 years, with age related
eye disease, living in their own homes. An ophthalmologist
assessed the individuals’ VA at an eye clinic with the 5 m KM
chart. A research assistant assessed individuals’ VA by the 1 m
KM chart and the Visual Acuity Screening Test in their home
environment. Results: All persons with a VA level of <0.5 were
correctly identified by both instruments. The instruments
have good positive and negative predictive values for the 1 m
KM chart (73% and 100%) and for the Visual Acuity Screening
Test (69% and 100%). The construct validity between the
instruments was good, but the assessment at the eye clinic
assessed the participants as having higher VA level. Conclusions:
Both instruments have good construct validity, considering
they were carried out in poorer lighting conditions and a good
predictive value for screening out VA levels <0.5. The 1 m KM
chart showed the best agreement with the 5 m KM chart.

Implications for Rehabilitation
• Short charts of visual acuity (VA) can be used outside
the eye clinic to identify persons with VA level 0.5, in
need of rehabilitation, as they are at risk of becoming
dependent in daily activities.
• Short charts of VA are portable instruments and useful
in other environments than the eye clinic, but training
is needed before use.
• Short charts of VA are useful instruments that can be
included in Comprehensive Geriatric Assessments
(CGA) to identify persons with visual impairment.

fastest growing [3]. It is not unusual for an elderly person, in
combination with morbidity and functional decline, to also

However, it has been suggested that visual impairment should
be assessed for elderly people at risk of falls and hip fracture
[19] as well as in a clinical assessment of older people receiving
rehabilitation [20]. It has also been stressed that it should be
examined routinely in research to shed light on the pathway
of disability [20]. To detect the presence of visual impairment,
visual acuity (VA) measurement is the one most used, even
though it only measures one part of visual functioning
[21,22]. Population surveys have shown that a decreased VA
level is strongly associated with limitation in performing daily
activities [23] and it is thus imperative to find an instrument
that can be used outside the eye clinic to identify elderly frail
people who have visual impairment in terms of decreased VA.
According to WHO’s definition [24,25], people are “visually
impaired” when they have a visual acuity level of less than 0.3
or a visual field below 10°. This VA level is often used in healthcare for judging the need of rehabilitation. More recent studies
[8,23,26] show that elderly people already have a higher risk of
morbidity or mortality at a visual acuity level of 0.5. They are
also at risk of becoming more dependent in activities of daily
living (ADL) and of using more assistive devices than elderly
people with normal vision. Therefore, assessing those who are
at risk for frailty in the aging population requires a cut-off level
of 0.5 in the screening of frail elderly people. This means that
there are both in rehabilitation and research in geriatric settings needs for an instrument that can identify visual impairment as a marker of frailty among elderly people in order to
capture those with early signs of visual impairment.
To our knowledge, no study has evaluated an instrument
for frail elderly people with the purpose of screening those
who show signs of visual impairment, as this is a group at risk
of functional decline [2]. Such an instrument could be used
as a screening tool in clinical practice and research to get an
approximate value of VA in addition to other measurements
of frailty. It is also important to get an approximate value of
those who have moderate or severe visual impairment to be
able to estimate visual impairment when there is no immediate access to an optometrist/ophthalmologist or in the elderly
person’s proximity. The purpose of such an instrument is to
get an approximate value that can indicate visual impairment in terms of visual acuity. The measure could be used as
a guide for further planning for geriatric assessments in care
and rehabilitation. It could also be used to show the need for
referring to further eye examinations and low vision rehabilitation. The aim of this study was to evaluate the construct
validity and describe the sensitivity, specificity and predictive
value of two short charts of visual acuity in order to find an
instrument that can identify those with a VA level of ≤0.5 and
that can approximately distinguish between early, moderate
and severe signs of visual impairment.

Participants
The participants were recruited at an open eye clinic at a
specialist hospital in the southwestern part of Sweden. The
inclusion criteria were 65 years and older and some kind of
diagnosed, age related eye disease. The participants had to be
living in their own homes and had to understand and make
themselves understood in Swedish. A total of 48 persons were
asked and accepted participation in the study, but five persons
dropped out, n = 43. Seventy-seven percent of the participants had more than one eye disease, 74% were women, and
the majority, 70%, had no social services. Three quarters of
the participants described their self-reported general global
health [28] as good or very good (Table I). The age of the
persons who dropped out ranged between 73 and 91 years,
median 84 years, and two of the five dropouts were men. The
characteristics of the dropouts did not differ from those of the
43 participants. The main causes of dropouts were sickness,
too busy or travel.
Instruments of visual acuity
The KM chart is a letter chart based on Monoyer standard
that is composed of seven letters from the Roman alphabet
[29] and it has been established that these letters have similar
legibility [30]. The letter size used is four units wide and five
units high and the inter-letter space is equal to one letter. The
KM chart is arranged in rows with an arithmetical progression of VA recordings from 0.1 to 1.0. The eyes are tested
separately and binocularly, and VA is assessed from <0.1 to
1.0 in ten scale steps. The VA level was recorded as the last
row for which seven of ten letters were read correctly [29],
which corresponds to clinical practice. Two different KM
charts were used in this study: 5 m KM chart (gold standard)
and a 1 m KM chart. The KM chart can be manufactured for
different measurement distances and the chart adjusted for 1
m is 21 × 29.5 centimetres.
The Visual Acuity Screening Test is made up of a single
optotype, a stylized letter E, in various orientations (Tumbling
E optotypes) [31]. The individual’s task is to indicate where
the open side of the symbol is located – top, bottom, left or
right – from the individual’s point of view. A set is successfully
completed when the open side of every symbol in the test has
been correctly identified. The test ends when two mistakes
are made in two consecutive sets, and the individual’s score is
the value assigned to the last set in which he/she managed to
identify every symbol correctly. The eyes are tested separately

Procedure
Persons at a routine visit were asked by an ophthalmologist
to participate in the study. The participants were selected
consecutively according to the inclusion criteria. The ophthalmologist tested the participant’s VA with a KM chart
adjusted for 5 metre distance with best refraction and the
participant’s distance glasses were thereafter corrected if
needed.
After the visit to the ophthalmologist, a research assistant contacted the participants by telephone and additional
information was given about the study. Informed consent
was obtained after both verbal and written information was
given. The persons (n = 43) were assessed in their own homes
approximately 2 weeks after the ophthalmologist visit. The
home visits were made during daytime and lasted between
1 and 1.5 h. The research assistant first interviewed the participants according to a structured protocol that consisted of
questions about marital status, living arrangements, social
services and self-reported global health [28]. No person was
excluded on the grounds of a perceived change in vision since
the ophthalmologist measurement.

The research assistant then assessed illuminance and luminance using a photometer (Hagner model S2, Hagner AB,
Solna, Sweden). This was done to ensure finding the brightest
area for measuring VA so that the assessments could be made
under optimal lighting conditions given the circumstances.
The participant’s VA was assessed with the 1 m KM chart and
the Visual Acuity Screening Test, and these assessments lasted
between five and ten minutes. Participants that had distance
eyeglasses were requested to use them. The research assistant
had received training in assessing VA with the 1 m KM chart
and the Visual Acuity Screening Test by the ophthalmologist.
The entire data collection was done over a 3-month period.

Data analysis
The level of inter-instrument agreement (construct validity)
between the assessments made with the 1 m KM chart, the
Visual Acuity Screening Test and the 5 m KM chart (gold
standard) was evaluated focusing on the level of order consistency between the assessments made by the two raters, the
ophthalmologist and the research assistant. A high level of
order consistency is a prerequisite for the inter-changeability
of scale. The level of order consistency is defined by the coefficient of monotonic agreement, MA. MA is the difference
between the proportions of ordered and disordered pairs of
observations and can be calculated by MA = 1-2D. D is a measure of disorder. The MA can range between −1 and 1. The
maximum value, MA = 1, is obtained when there is a total
order consistency of the observations [27,34]. This means
that values close to 1 have the highest agreement, and there
is complete agreement between pairs of observations when
MA = 1. Percentage agreement (PA) was used to express the
proportion of the inter-instrument agreement between the
paired assessments. RP (Relative Position) and RC (Relative
Concentration) were measured to complement the measurement of inter-instrument agreement between the scales. The
possible value of RP and RC range from −1 to 1, and zero
values mean a lack of agreement/systematic disagreement
[27,34].
The assessments with the two KM charts and the Visual
Acuity Screening Test were made on a ten-point scale which
was reduced to a four-point scale. The definition of visual acuity levels in this four-point scale was made as follows: VA levels moderate visual impairment (<0.3−0.1) and severe visual
impairment (<0.1) were used according to WHO’s definition
of visual acuity for blindness and low vision [24,25]; WHO’s
VA level 1.0–0.3, mild and no visual impairment, was divided
into two VA levels, one level (1.0–0.6) had the same label
(mild and no visual impairment 1.0–0.6) while the other VA
level 0.5–0.3 was labelled early signs of visual impairment. This
was done in order to identify levels of acuity that risk compromising the performance of daily activities among older people
[12,23,26]. These four cut-off VA levels were used in the calculations of inter-instrument agreement between the scales.
The VA of the best eye with the best correction was the value
chosen as the VA parameter in the ophthalmologist assessment, and the VA of the best eye using the participant’s own
distance glasses was the value chosen as the VA parameter at
the home visit.

The sensitivity, specificity and predictive values were calculated to describe the probability of the instrument [35]
to screen out those with a VA of ≤0.5. Sensitivity means the
proportion of assessments that correctly identified VA ≤ 0.5
by the 1 m KM chart or the Visual Acuity Screening Test.
Specificity means the proportion of assessments that correctly
identified VA 0.6–1.0 by the 1 m KM chart or Visual Acuity
Screening Test. The positive and negative predictive values
give an assessment of the clinical usefulness of the test [35].
The positive predictive value is the proportion of persons correctly assessed as having visual impairment by the 1 m KM
chart or the Visual Acuity Screening Test. The negative predictive value is the proportion of persons correctly assessed
as not having visual impairment by the 1 m KM chart or the
Visual Acuity Screening Test.

Results
The level of order consistency (MA) was 0.964 for the four point scale of the 5 m KM chart and the 1 m KM chart
(Figure 1a) and 0.948 for the 5 m KM chart and the Visual
Acuity Screening Test (Figure 2a). There was an inter-instrument agreement of 44% (PA) for the 5 m KM chart and the
1 m KM chart (Figure 1a) and of 37% (PA) for the 5 m KM
chart and the Visual Acuity Screening Test (Figure 2a). The
results of these inter-scale comparisons showed a high level
of order consistency (high MA) and a low PA, which indicates
a systematic disagreement between the two four-point sets of
data (Figure 1a and 2a). The ophthalmologist systematically
assessed the individuals as having higher VA levels than the
research assistant. This is confirmed by the RP value of −0.32
for the 5 m KM chart and the 1 m KM chart and −0.36 for
the 5 m KM chart and the Visual Acuity screening test. The
systematic disagreement in position between the raters is
illustrated by the convex shape of the ROC curves for the 5 m
KM chart and the 1 m KM chart (Figure 1b) and for the 5 m
KM chart and the Visual Acuity Screening Test (Figure 2b).
The RC value was 0.04 for the 5 m KM chart and the 1 m KM
chart. The RC value was 0.14 for the 5 m KM chart and the
Visual Acuity Screening Test and showed a slight systematic
disagreement in concentration between the two scales.

The sensitivity, which is the proportion of assessments
made with the 1 m KM chart or the Visual Acuity screening
test that correctly identified visual impairment (VA ≤ 0.5),
was 100%. The specificity, that is, the proportion of assessments that correctly identified normal vision (VA > 0.6–1.0)
was 38% for the 1 m KM chart and 25% for the Visual Acuity
Screening Test. The positive predictive value, i.e. the proportion of persons with visual impairment (VA ≤ 0.5) who were
identified as having visual impairment, was 73% for the 1 m
KM chart and 69% for the Visual Acuity Screening Test. The
negative predictive value, which is the proportion of persons
with normal vision (VA > 0.6–1.0) who were identified as having normal vision, was 100% for both instruments (Table II).

Discussion
Both short charts of visual acuity, the 1 m KM chart and the
Visual Acuity Screening Test, used for assessment of visual
function in the home environment could identify the individuals with visual impairment. The results showed that we
can expect the two short charts of visual acuity to distinguish
100% of those with a visual impairment (VA ≤ 0.5). The instruments have good positive and negative predictive values for
the 1 m KM chart (73% and 100%) and for the Visual Acuity
Screening Test (69% and 100%) which is very good [35]. This
is important since research [9,12,13,23,26] shows that older
people with visual impairment already tend to have problems
in daily activities when they have early signs of visual impairment (VA ≤ 0.5). Identifying these is thus important so that
rehabilitation can start early. Research shows that the earlier
the intervention starts, and the less serious the activity limitations are, the better are the conditions for the ability to retain
activity [3,17,18,23,36].
The level of ordering (MA) was high, which means that
there was no change in ordering of the participant’s level of
visual acuity between the short charts of visual acuity and
gold standard. This is an important prerequisite for the construct validity (inter-instrument agreement) of the scales
[27,34]. However, the results show a low PA between the
5 m KM chart and the 1 m KM chart or the Visual Acuity
Screening Test, which indicates a systematic disagreement

systematic disagreement is explained by the fact that the ophthalmologist assessed the persons to have higher VA levels
than in the assessment by the research assistant, which was
confirmed by the RP values [34]. It can be argued that this
difference is due to the discrepancy in test distance between
the instruments and that the charts are different. Another
reason can be that the ophthalmologist and research assistant
have different knowledge and experience of assessing VA levels. A more potent explanation is that the ophthalmologist’s
assessments were made with more sophisticated assessment
instruments and under optimal lighting conditions, while
the assessments made by the research assistant were made in
home environments. Even though the brightest area for measuring VA was chosen, lighting conditions are most likely
poorer in home environments than in the ophthalmologist’s
office. Yet another explanation for the systematic disagreement can be that the ophthalmologist made the assessments
with the best refraction and, even if the participants’ were
prescribed to buy new distance glasses if needed, we do not
know if this was done. To summarize, under these circumstances, there should be a difference between the assessments
but, since the intention is to approximately distinguish those
with visual impairment, this disagreement does not seriously hamper the reliability of the instruments but should be
noticed when used.
One problem with the assessments was that persons
who have no visual impairment could be assessed as having impaired vision. The short charts identified fewer

participants having normal vision compared to the ophthalmological assessments. This means that a visual screening test in an everyday environment might overestimate
visual impairment slightly. Considering the importance of
identifying persons at risk of visual frailty, an underestimation could be potentially more hazardous. Measures for
sensitivity and specificity are not sufficient to describe the
probability of an instrument; estimates of predictive values
to correctly identify those with visual impairments are also
needed [35]. The instruments have good positive and negative predictive values for the 1 m KM chart (73% and 100%)
and for the Visual Acuity Screening Test (69% and 100%).
The instruments are therefore useful tools when there is a
need of screening for visual impairment when there is no
access to an ophthalmologist or optometrist. However, it is
important to consider that these short charts of visual acuity cannot replace a complete eye examination or complete
ophthalmological examination.
Although visual impairment has been shown to be a
marker of frailty in the older population, it is seldom included
as an assessment for older people in geriatric care or geriatric
rehabilitation [20]. These short charts can be used to identify older persons that have visual impairment as one part of
the CGA and is one indicator that should be measured when
assessing frailty [20]. They should only be seen as guides for
further planning and form the basis for CGA in care and
rehabilitation or be used to show the need for referral to eye
examination.
The short charts of visual acuity differ in some areas but
are mostly similar. The design of the instruments differs, the
1 m KM chart is constructed by Roman alphabet letters and
the Visual Acuity Screening Test is constructed by tumbling E
optotype. An advantage for the Visual Acuity Screening Test,
with tumbling E optotype, is that it can be used to test persons
with illiteracy and populations not familiar with the Roman
alphabet [32]. The similarities are that the amount of time
necessary for the assessment was experienced as the same, the
instruments seem equally easy to understand and the instructions before the assessments were alike. Both short charts

have a small format, which makes them easy to transport. In
addition, both are standardized for assessments at a distance
of 1 m and can thus be useable in environments other than
the eye clinic for instance home environments. Assessments
in home environments can be of great advantage for those
frail elderly persons with mobility problems.
A limitation of both short charts of visual acuity as markers of frailty in the aging population is that they measure only
one parameter in visual impairment, visual acuity. However,
visual field defect, another parameter in visual impairment, is
not included in this screening. Eye diseases in this age group
that are characterized by visual field defects such as glaucoma
where decline in visual acuity comes at a later stage of the disease will not be found in an early stage by using these short
charts of visual acuity [37]. The argument behind the need of
a short chart of visual acuity as markers of frailty was to identify persons that are at risk of developing dependency in daily
activities, and persons with glaucoma do not report problems
in daily life until at a later stage [37]. Visual acuity has shown
to be strongly correlated to problems in daily activities compared to other visual impairment parameters [37]. Thus, recognizing the limitation mentioned above, these short charts
of visual acuity should be valuable tools as markers of frailty.
In conclusion, these short charts of visual acuity have good
construct validity (inter-instrument agreement) considering
that it was held in the persons’ homes and a good predictive
value for identifying those who have a VA level of ≤0.5. The
inter-instrument agreement between the 5 m KM chart and
the 1 m KM chart or the Visual Acuity Screening Test showed
that the 1 m KM chart has better values in the MA, PA, RP
and RC. This means that the 1 m KM chart has the best agreement with the 5 m KM chart, which was used in this study as
the gold standard.
