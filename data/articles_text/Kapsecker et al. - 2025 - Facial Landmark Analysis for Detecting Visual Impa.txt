Facial Landmark Analysis for Detecting Visual
Impairment in Mobile LogMAR Test
Maximilian Kapsecker , Elena Mille , Florian Schweizer , Jens Klinker , Joe Yu ,
Alexander Leube , and Stephan M. Jonas

Abstract—Visual impairment is a widespread global
health issue that affects millions of people across all ages
and backgrounds. Timely intervention is essential for the
effective management of eye diseases. Smartphones offer
the possibility of continuously recording facial gestures
during interaction with the device, whereby changes such
as squinting of the eyes could indicate progressive vision
loss. In this context, a mobile health application was developed to conduct a digital logMAR test while simultaneously capturing real-time facial features. A total of 37
participants took part in a controlled mobile eye test study.
The facial landmarks recorded during the test were analyzed to identify patterns that can distinguish between sequences of letters that were read correctly, partially, or not
at all. Specifically, explorative data analysis and receiver
operating characteristic curves were employed to determine facial landmarks with high discriminative power in
relation to reading ability. The predominant facial regions
that showed the most significant change under reduced
performance during the vision test were the nose, mouth,
and cheeks. Notably, the characteristic maximum squinting
of the cheeks stood out with an area under the curve of
0.82. The analysis showed the potential of tracking specific
facial features for continuous and unobtrusive vision assessment. It motivates to integrate facial feature analysis
into an everyday application such as a web browser and
to conduct a study in a non-standardized environment on a
larger scale.

I. INTRODUCTION
ISION plays a pivotal role in our daily lives, surpassing
other senses in importance for decision-making [1], [2]. It
is essential for recognizing objects, navigating environments,
avoiding collisions, and understanding detailed features like
facial expressions and body language [3], [4]. Impairments in
vision, especially in high-stakes situations where functional
sight is critical, can pose significant problems. For instance, there
is evidence that older people with visual impairments are more
likely to fall and suffer fractures [5] and that driving a car with
a visual impairment is dangerous [6]. According to the World
Health Organization, at least 2.2 billion people worldwide had
impaired near or distant vision in 2019, with more than 1 billion
cases preventable or yet to be treated [7]. Vision impairment
represents a significant global health challenge, affecting quality
of life, social interactions, and economic productivity [7], [8],
[9], [10], [11], [12], [13].
In the digital age, the widespread use of computers and
screens has increased the demand for visual skills, possibly
also hastening visual deterioration [14]. The increasing number
of eye diseases such as myopia, which has reached pandemic
proportions in East Asia [15], [16] and poses an economic challenge [13], underlines the need for proactive eye care. Evidence based guidelines also emphasize the importance of regular eye
examinations [17]. While the infrastructure for vision assessments is well-established in high-income countries [18], delays in addressing vision impairment remain prevalent [19]. In
many developing regions and rural areas, there is also a significant gap in healthcare infrastructure, particularly in access to
ophthalmologists and a clinical environment [18], [20], [21].
Smartphones, now ubiquitous globally, present a unique
opportunity to partially bridge the healthcare gap [22], [23].
Equipped with sophisticated sensors and computational capabilities, smartphones are increasingly being used in healthcare
applications [24]. Applications like Peek Vision1 have demonstrated potential to enhance healthcare access by offering services like telemedicine, diagnostic tools, and health monitoring [25]. Additionally, smartphones offer accessibility features,
such as increased font sizes and more readable fonts, which

Research has also explored the use of mobile devices for vision
assessment. Mobile applications have been developed to conduct
visual acuity tests, color vision tests, and contrast sensitivity
tests [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]. These applications leverage the device’s display to present standardized test
charts and collect responses from users, facilitating remote and
self-administered vision assessment. Labiris et al. have shown
through multiple implementations, such as smart tv-based and
web-based systems, that digital visual acuity assessments can be
used in clinical contexts and as self-assessments at home [35],
[36], [37], [38]. Although their implementations have a focus
on the greek language, they still highlight the feasibility and
correctness of digital visual acuity assessment. Another potential
advantage of digital assessments is ease-of-use, especially with
non-trained staff [39]. However, Tofigh et al. have highlighted
potential issues with digital visual acuity assessment implementations, such as calibration of brightness, screen resolution, and
color contrast [40].
There is a general consensus in prior research regarding the
strong feasibility of conducting standard eye tests in a mobile
environment. In particular, Suo et al. (2022) provide a systematic
review and meta-analysis of mobile versus clinical visual acuity
tests, reporting sensitivities of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.79–0.93), 0.86
(95% CI, 0.84–0.87), and 0.85 (95% CI, 0.55–0.98) and specificities of 0.78 (95% CI, 0.70–0.85), 0.91 (95% CI, 0.90–0.91),
and 0.98 (95% CI, 0.95–0.99) for the age groups 3–5 years,
6–22 years, and over 55 years, respectively [41]. Building on
the findings that demonstrate the feasibility of mobile visual
acuity testing, this study investigates potential digital biomarkers
that indicate reduced vision. The aim is to provide evidence for
the future development of mobile systems for passive vision
monitoring (see Fig. 1).
While existing applications enable mobile vision assessments,
to the best of the authors’ knowledge, there has been limited
exploration into the integration of smartphone sensors to detect
subtle behavioral changes, such as facial expressions, that may
indicate visual impairment. Chen et al. (2023) developed a
mobile eye examination tool for young children for the early
detection of 16 ophthalmic disorders by analyzing gaze direction and facial features in response to visual stimuli [42].
The performance evaluation of the deep-learning based method
on an external dataset revealed an AUC of 0.86 and showed
robustness while adapting to different environments. As an

innovation to current mobile vision assessments, the rationale
for recording these indicators stems from the hypothesis that
tracking behavioral data could reveal signs of visual impairment
and serve as digital biomarkers. Facial expressions, for example,
can be key non-verbal indicators of discomfort. Research, such
as Ekman and Friesen’s Facial Action Coding System, has shown
that facial expressions like squinting or furrowing of the brow
may be associated with emotional experiences [43], which might
include difficulty seeing clearly. Galati et al. reported an increased frequency of certain facial movements when comparing
children with congenital low vision and sighted children [44]. By
recognizing these non-verbal cues, it might be possible to infer
eyesight issues and initiate further assessments. Additionally,
individuals experiencing vision problems may exhibit subtle
changes in facial behavior, such as blinking patterns or tilting of
the head, which can signal visual discomfort or distress.
Carrying out eye tests with modern mobile devices offers the
possibility of simultaneously recording facial landmarks and
identifying correlations with visual impairments. These facial
landmarks could then assist in detecting visual impairments
without explicit eye examinations, by unobtrusively monitoring subtle changes in facial features. Given the frequent use
of smartphones in everyday life, this approach is particularly
promising as a complement to clinical assessment, which may
overlook visual impairments that rather manifest in real-life situations [45]. Regular and unobtrusive tracking of relevant facial
features, e.g., while reading on a smartphone, offers a novel
method for the potential recognition of vision-related changes,
fostering timely interventions and addressing the monitoring of
progressive vision loss.
The objective of this study is to design and validate a mobile based vision assessment application that can accurately detect
and quantify vision impairment using standard eye examination
techniques while simultaneously recording facial landmarks.
Through facial landmark analysis, the application provides
behavioral insights into how vision impairment affects facial
expressions. Therefore, this study addresses the overarching
question: Can the analysis of facial landmarks during a standard
vision test performed with mobile devices draw conclusions
about readability – possibly related to visual impairment? The
long-term aim is to integrate facial feature tracking into everyday
technologies, like web browsers and e-readers, for the detection
of vision-related behavioral changes, providing timely alerts to
users.
To adequately answer this question, a well-designed study
needs to be conducted. Studies in an uncontrolled setting can be
influenced by numerous confounding factors that can obscure
the true impact of visual impairments on facial landmarks. In an
uncontrolled environment in which participants use the application alone, different lighting conditions, the angle and distance
at which the device is positioned and the subjective assessment
of their own eyesight could play a role. In contrast, a standardized environment allows for the controlled assessment of
these factors, ensuring that the contributions of facial landmarks
are more isolated, pronounced and significant. This controlled
approach is an important first step in accurately assessing the
potential of facial feature analysis in the detection of visual

II. MATERIAL AND METHODS
To address the research objectives and answer the proposed
questions, the methodology followed three key phases, each
described in detail within this section: (1) adapting a standard
visual acuity test into a mobile application, incorporating supplementary components such as the recording of facial landmarks
and questionnaires; (2) conducting an eye-test study using this
mobile application to gather relevant data; and (3) analyzing
the collected data, focusing on the relationship between readability and facial gestures, primarily through descriptive and
explorative statistical methods.
A. Mobile Application
The application, named EyeSight, was specifically developed
for the iPhone 13 Pro using Swift, the primary programming
language for iOS. EyeSight was designed to perform standard visual acuity tests while simultaneously capturing facial
landmarks to analyze visual behavior. Questionnaires before
and after the visual acuity test were used to collect metadata
about the participant. The software is available under https:
//github.com/NumericalMax/viim.
Visual Acuity Test: Visual acuity, the ability of the human
eye to recognize fine details, is defined as the resolving power
of the eye. It is quantified by the visual angle of the detail,
which is the angle formed by the smallest distinguishable two
points separated in space of the object seen by the eye (cf.
Fig. 2(a)) [47]. For standardized visual acuity tests, a symbol
called a Landolt ring is used, which is a C-like optotype where
the gap – referred to here as the detail of the letter – is 1/5 of the
total height (cf. Fig. 2(b)). In a more clinical set-up of a visual
acuity test, letters that fulfill such shape definitions can also be
used. For a consistent measurement and for the development of a

vision test that is both accurate and adaptable to different display
devices such as the iPhone 13 Pro, technical considerations such
as screen size and viewing distance lead to limitations and were
taken into account in the present study.

N, O, P, R, T, V, Y, and Z. The open-source font Optician Sans is

Data collection: During the conduction of the logMAR test,
the optical sensors, i.e. the device’s front-facing camera and
the true depth sensor, were used to collect data on a sample
basis. Specifically, the face-tracking functionality of Apple’s
ARKit2 framework, which interfaces directly with these optical
sensors, served as the data delivery layer. Each sample represents
a single snapshot of the face that contains metadata such as
the timestamp and current logMAR level, the tracked face mesh
(represented by 1220 three-dimensional vertices), and blendshapes. Latter resembles a preprocessed set of 52 interpreted
facial expressions.34 Blendshapes are represented with a title
key, e.g. Brow Outer Up Left, and a single floating point number
(0–1) to quantify expression strength. These values are sampled
at the device’s maximum frequency of 60 Hertz.
Squint detection: One of the application’s key functional
features is the detection of eye squinting. Short-sighted people
in particular tend to squint their eyes in order to adjust their
vision to see objects in the distance [49]. To reliably detect
this, a key functional requirement was the integration of a
calibration step to measure individual squinting intensity. During
calibration, participants follow a sequence of eye poses, starting
from neutral, then progressing to slightly squinting, strongly
squinting, and back to slightly squinting (cf. Fig. 3(b)). The application simultaneously records and averages the values of the
Eye Squint Right and Eye Squint Left facial landmarks provided by
ARKit, setting thresholds for each pose. To ensure objectivity,
distraction poses such as looking left or right and smiling are
included in the calibration process. Calibration relies on a snapshot of each participant’s current perception and execution of
squinting strength, making exact reproducibility of thresholds
unlikely if the experiment is repeated.
Squint detection is triggered when the participant’s thresholds
are exceeded. This functionality becomes particularly relevant
during the second round of the eye examination cycle, where
squinting causes the screen to turn red, preventing the participant
from compensating for their vision by squinting – potentially
revealing alternative compensatory facial gestures.

B. Study
The participants were recruited via posters and personal contacts, mainly at the Technical University of Munich (TUM). The
study was approved by the TUM ethics committee (2023–509S-SB). All participants were first informed about the context
and procedure of the study, and written informed consent was
obtained from all participants beforehand.
For the study the participant was seated at a distance of 3
meters in front of the mobile device (iPhone 13 Pro), which
was mounted horizontally – measured with iOS inbuilt spirit
level application – on a tripod at the participant’s eye level (cf.
Fig. 3(e)). The device was adjusted so that it was at the same
height as the participant’s head and the participant had the feeling
that they were looking straight at the screen. For those with
visual impairments, the distance from the device was reduced
to either two or one meter, depending on the severity of the
impairment, to allow the display of higher logMAR levels (cf.
Fig. 3(a)). To ensure consistent lighting conditions for every test,
ceiling lights were switched on. Some participants who wore
glasses underwent the procedure twice, once with glasses and
once without. The study conductor was located beside the tripod
and guided the participant through the test.
After facial calibration and going through the first part of the
questionnaire, the actual visual acuity test was carried out twice
and with both eyes, i.e., binocularly. Each time the participant
was presented with rows of letters that became smaller until
the participant could no longer read them. In the second round,
any attempts to squint the eyes were automatically detected by
the application and prevented by turning the screen red. The
participant did not receive any feedback as to whether the letters
were recognized correctly. The study conductor secretly labeled
the rows of letters by tapping on different areas of the screen,
depending on whether all, some, or none of the letters in a row
had been recognized (cf. Fig. 3(c)). The second part of the
questionnaire concluded the study run. Throughout the study
process, the recording of facial landmarks and the calculation of
squint thresholds were performed automatically by the system.
All other data collection and procedures were manually entered
by the study conductor, including completing the questionnaire,
labeling whether the letter row was read correctly, initiating the
calibration phase when the participant was ready, and navigating
to the next screen.
Finally, the study conductor exported the recorded data to the
phone’s file system and labeled it with a numerical identifier. The
raw files were then moved to a computer for further processing.

enriched with further metadata such as the participant identifier

comparability across participants in subsequent analyses.
Samples indicating mouth opening, identified by the JawOpen
blendshape (typically due to speech), were excluded to prevent
bias. Talking can influence other facial features and potentially
mask the true changes relevant to letter-reading ability during
vision tests. While speaking directly relates to reading ability and
can bias results, other potential artifacts such as gaze direction
and head movement were not systematically excluded. Gaze
direction might itself indicate reduced readability, and head
movements were minimal since participants generally remained
focused on the mobile screen, making it challenging to establish
thresholds for excluding such deviations.
The blendshapes were recorded at ARKit’s highest resolution
of 60 Hz, allowing for detailed capture of facial gestures, even
those of short duration. This high-resolution recording provided
greater flexibility in data analysis, resulting in multiple snapshots
of blendshape values per participant and per letter row. The
raw blendshape values were recorded as a sequence and then
aggregated by participant and letter row using the mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum. In the final dataset,
the Label column annotated the reading ability for each letter
row as all, some, or none of the letters, along with metadata
from questionnaires and information on whether squinting was
permitted. To minimize biases from initial unfamiliar testing
conditions and learning effects, the first presented row of letters
shown to each participant was excluded from the dataset.

Explorative Data Analysis and Statistical analysis: To facilitate a robust exploratory analysis, the blendshapes associated
with facial regions – jaw, eye, mouth, cheek, forehead, and nose
– were averaged into a single representative value. Blendshapes
associated with the tongue were omitted from the analysis based
on the assumption that all samples involved closed mouths.

Timeseries plots of the facial regions were created in order
to visually examine the change with reduced readability of
the letters. Furthermore, boxplots and analysis of variance
(ANOVA) were employed to discern any significant differences
in facial regions based on the participants’ ability to recognize
all, some, or none of the letters in a row. The prerequisites
for a valid one-way ANOVA – (1) independence of samples,
(2) normality of distribution, and (3) homoscedasticity – were
met in the dataset. The null hypothesis, asserting no difference in
regional scores among the groups, was rejected at a significance
level of 5%.
Assessing Sensor Accuracy: Previous work revealed a
strongly decreasing reliability of the true depth sensor in dependence of increasing participant-device distance [50]. To verify
the accuracy of facial landmark detection for a distance of three
meters, four authors performed three predefined gestures with
a repetition count of five. The gestures were raising the brows,
smiling, and squinting the eyes. Subsequently, the blendshapes,
in which a change was anticipated were visually examined
regarding signal deflection and the number of repetition counts.
Facial Landmarks as a Predictor of Readability: This study
aimed to identify facial landmarks associated with reduced
readability, potentially indicative of impaired vision. An interpretable thresholding method was applied to each of the
aggregated blendshape values to evaluate their effectiveness in
distinguishing between different readability levels. To address
the class imbalance – where the some (n = 202) and none
(n = 25) categories were underrepresented compared to the all
category (n = 638) – the some and none labels were combined
into a single not all class, thereby achieving a more balanced
sample distribution.
Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves [51], [52]
were employed to assess the ability of blendshape aggregates to
differentiate between the all and not all readability classes. The
ROC curve analysis facilitated performance evaluation through
the area under the curve (AUC) metric, which quantifies the
discriminative power of the blendshape aggregates. AUC values
range from 0 to 1, with values further from 0.5 indicating
stronger discriminative ability. For example, an AUC of 1.0
for the mean Cheek Squint Left blendshape aggregate indicates
perfect separation of readability levels.
ROC curves and AUC calculations were performed in two
ways:
1) Individualized: Each blendshape aggregate (4 × 52)
was evaluated separately for the 45 individual eye examinations. This approach is highly individualized, as
each value array (e.g., Cheek Squint Left Mean as shown in
Fig. 4) was assessed for its ability to separate classes. The
resulting AUC values – calculated across 4 aggregations,
52 blendshapes, and 45 eye examinations – were ranked
based on performance and visualized using boxplots to
summarize the discriminative capabilities of the various
blendshapes.
2) Generalized: Each blendshape aggregate (4 × 52) was
also evaluated using the combined data from all 45 eye
examination tests in one. AUCs and their corresponding
95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the
DeLong test [53] and presented in a table. To evaluate the

4431

potential impact of the mild to moderate class imbalance,
we repeated the analysis by subsampling the majority
class (all) to match the sample size of the minority class
(not all).
A threshold-based classifier was constructed to predict readability based on the blendshape features. To enhance accuracy
and reduce feature dimensionality, only blendshape aggregates
with AUC values greater than 0.75 were selected for classifier
development. Due to the relatively small number of participants,
using a traditional hold-out test set comprising approximately
20% of the data was insufficient to achieve statistically significant results. Therefore, performance was assessed using
a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) approach. In the
LOOCV method, the classifier was iteratively trained on all
participants’ eye examination cycles except one, which was used
for evaluation. Specifically, the classifier learned thresholds for
the selected blendshape aggregates based on the training data
from all but one participant’s eye examination cycles. These
thresholds were then applied to the left-out data, binarizing the
aggregated blendshapes as either 0 or 1. To predict the readability
of a specific letter row, the binary blendshape values were
averaged to obtain a probability score. If the averaged probability
exceeded 0.5, the letter row was classified as positive, indicating
that it was not readable by the participant. For each left-out
participant’s eye examination cycle, classifier performance was
assessed using balanced accuracy, which is the arithmetic mean
of sensitivity and specificity. The resulting balanced accuracies,
sensitivities, and specificities were averaged and reported for
two scenarios: allowing squinting and not allowing squinting.
III. RESULTS
A. Study
From September 2023 to December 2023, 37 individual
participants performed a total of 46 eye tests. Due to metadata
corruption, one test was excluded, leaving 45 valid vision
tests. The mean diopters (D) of participants was −1.6 D, with a
standard deviation of 2.8 D. Of these, 27 tests were conducted
with participants who own a visual aid – either glasses or
contact lenses – with 16 of the participants using them during
the test. Most participants reported that they had no difficulty
reading a smartphone screen, which is related to the fact that
many either had no visual impairment or were short-sighted.
12 participants stated difficulties when reading cinema titles or
recognizing street signs.
The eye tests were primarily conducted at a distance of 3
meters (39 trials), with fewer trials at 2 meters (2 trials), 1 m
(2 trials), and 0.5 meters (2 trials). Post-test feedback indicated
that 22 participants believed they might need a new visual aid,
and 17 found the test challenging.

IV. DISCUSSION
A. Feasibility
The results of this study demonstrate the feasibility of adapting standard eye tests for mobile environments. The letter sizes
were calibrated to adhere to medical standards, while the experimental setting simulated an optometrist’s environment. The
detection of squinting was effective for most participants and
the overall signal accuracy was sufficient to allow analysis from
a distance of at least three meters (cf. Fig. 6). Although the
test could not detect fine-grained differences, the participants’
logMAR values converted to visual acuity (cf. Table I) correlated
well with their self-reported diopters. This aligns with previous
findings on the performance of mobile visual acuity tests [41]
and highlights the significant potential of mobile applications
for assessing myopia and other visual impairments.
In addition, the pilot study provided promising results in
detecting behavioral changes related to letter perception and
reading accuracy. Visual inspection of facial regions, particularly those encompassing individual facial features, indicated
significant changes when participants struggled with letter sizes,
typically starting from a logMAR of 0.0. The average logMAR of
0.0 generally stands for a good visual acuity of the participants,
which is not surprising due to the predominance of young
participants [54]. The consistently low orange line in 5(a) and
5(b) is due to squinting being prohibited, resulting in less curve
deflection compared to when participants were free to use their
facial expressions.
The average AUC scores further confirmed the presence of
discriminative factors related to letter recognition and readability. Cheek squinting emerged as the predominant blendshape for
distinguishing between classes. This finding aligns with early
work by Bradburne (1909), who associated squinting with refractive errors, fusion defects, or other vision impairments [49].
By narrowing the eye’s opening and reducing unfocused light
rays, squinting can effectively increase visual focus and acuity,
providing a plausible physiological basis for why this particular
facial expression is so strongly linked to changes in perceived
readability. Likewise, the facial regions of the cheeks, mouth,
and nose were frequently represented among the top discriminators – likely because they mimic eye squinting behavior.
The exploratory data analysis also revealed a significant trend
within the facial regions of the mouth, cheeks, and nose (cf.
Fig. 5(b)). The results suggest that the hypothesis of distinct
facial patterns between effortless reading and reading difficulties cannot be readily dismissed. Moreover, the AUC of the
maximum Cheek Squint feature was 0.82 (95% CI, 0.78–0.87),
which is approximately in line with the reported AUC of 0.86

4435

(95% CI, 0.78–0.94) by Chen et al. (2023) [42]. Although Chen
et al. (2023) predicted visual impairments in young children
primarily based on gaze direction and facial gestures, their
and the underlying findings affirm the significant role of facial
expressions in identifying readability challenges.
B. Implications
The results demonstrated largely uniform patterns during
reduced readability, suggesting similar behavioral patterns occur
during reduced vision. Specifically, squinting of the cheeks and
associated facial movements might serve as a digital biomarker
for visual health. Facial metrics were recorded from a distance
of three meters, implying that tracking facial characteristics
even closer to the phone – such as when interacting with it
or holding it near the face while looking into the distance –
might accurately detect indicators of vision loss, particularly
when tracking changes over a long period. The study data
showed high heterogeneity among participants, for example in
the intensity of facial expressions, implying that changes in
individual facial expressions need to be considered on a per person basis rather than pursuing a one-threshold-fits-all strategy. The practical implications are that extending the approach
into non-standardized settings through a long-term study is both
feasible and justified. This extension would mitigate the current
limitations of the study, some of which stem from the necessity
of establishing a proof-of-concept before progressing to more
sophisticated and time-demanding procedures. Detecting signs
and progression of vision impairment using mobile devices as a
long term perspective would have a significant impact on global
health, considering the high prevalence and rapid increase of eye
diseases.
C. Limitations
The experiments were conducted in a standardized environment to minimize external influences on the vision testing
process. Nonetheless, there remains a risk that the observed
behavioral changes could be attributed to non-vision-related
factors, such as adaptation to the testing environment, which
might alter facial expressions. Additionally, the head was not
fixed during testing, allowing for minor movements in distance
and angle relative to the smartphone, which could impact the
accuracy of the results. While samples with detected open
mouths were excluded, interactions between speech dynamics
and other facial landmarks, like the cheeks, might still introduce
bias into the analysis. The generalizability of facial expressions
in visually impaired people in non-standardized environments
is also still limited.
A key limitation of this study is the relatively small and
homogeneous sample of 37 participants, which restricts both the
statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. With a
limited number of participants, subtle differences or variations in
facial reactions may not have been detectable, increasing the risk
of type II errors. The absence of participants with more diverse
forms of visual impairment beyond mild to mid-severe myopia
and hyperopia, as well as the lack of individuals from a broader
range of ages and ethnicities, further constrains the applicability

of these results. For instance, older adults or those with age related ocular conditions may exhibit distinct behavioral patterns
not captured in this cohort. Similarly, a more ethnically diverse
population could present a wider array of facial expressions
and cultural variations in nonverbal communication. Although
steps were taken to standardize data and consider individual
thresholds, the extent to which these findings can be generalized to different populations and clinical contexts remains an
open question. Future studies should incorporate larger, more
diverse cohorts to enhance the robustness and significance of the
results.
Regarding the test distance, conducting the examination at
more than three meters – according to DIN Norm a minimum
distance of four meter is required [48] – presents challenges due
to the limited screen size of smartphones, which restricts the
display of large letters. Conversely, when testing too close to the
smartphone screen the far point of people with low myopia might
lie beyond the test distance. Their visual impairment will not be
noticable, no matter how small the letters get. Consequently, the
current implementation is most effective within a range of two
to three meters.
Compared to existing remote vision testing tools, the application currently falls short in terms of usability. While it includes an
onboarding process and guides the user through the test, there is
room for improvement, particularly in providing visual guidance
and audio instructions. These improvements would allow the
test to be carried out independently and without the help of
a second person. In addition, the results of facial recognition
are not processed or displayed directly on the device, but the
data must be transferred to a computer for further analysis. This
requirement restricts the use of the application in its current form
to the research field and does not make it accessible for wider
personal use.
D. Future Work
The application’s scope can be extended by incorporating
additional data, such as voice patterns and posture, enabling
analyses akin to those performed with facial features. A potential
hypothesis is that diminished readability correlates with changes
in voice confidence. Integrating a remote control feature would
allow optometrists to administer tests via telehealth platforms.
Additionally, collaborative learning of blendshape thresholds
using secure aggregation protocols could enhance the identification of crucial vision-related factors, all while preserving user
privacy. Cross-platform recording and analysis of facial features
could be a valuable step into the future, allowing the application
to be used across a wide range of mobile devices as well as
desktop and laptop computers.
To validate and expand upon the findings of this study in
a practical context, the authors plan to integrate facial feature tracking into common, non-standardized everyday environments, such as web browsers and e-book readers. The proposed
roadmap involves first consolidating the current facial landmark
tracking and external data analysis components – currently
performed post-hoc on a central computer – into a stand alone
mobile library that can be natively deployed on popular operating systems. In addition, the authors will integrate other sensor

data (e.g., device orientation, camera-to-user distance, and ambient light intensity) to automatically adjust for environmental
variations that may affect vision behavior. Once the library is
fully integrated, for example into an open-source web browser
platform, frequent on-device data sampling can occur during the
user’s interaction.
The authors plan to implement a user feedback mechanism via
notifications. Periodically, users may be asked to provide subjective assessments of their perceived vision quality, as well as the
results of any recent professional eye examinations. These user
inputs can serve as soft labels that, combined with the continuous
facial expression data, will help correlate observed changes
in facial behavior with variations in visual acuity over time.
Additionally, research into user experience and communication
strategies must be conducted to ensure users are sensitively
informed about changes in their data (e.g., significant increases
in squinting strength).
A clinical study is planned, in collaboration with ophthalmologists, to further evaluate the feasibility and accuracy of these
methods in both inpatient and remote settings. This initiative will
involve regular professional eye examinations and ongoing user
feedback, strengthening the clinical evidence and applicability
of the proposed solution. With a larger, more diverse participant
cohort, it will be possible to advance with more sophisticated
AI models such as tree-based algorithms. These advances could
lay the foundation for a widely available digital solution for the
unobtrusive detection of signs of visual impairment.
V. CONCLUSION
This study explored the use of mobile devices to conduct a
standard eye examination, capturing facial features to identify
patterns indicative of wrong letter reading, which may suggest
vision impairment. The adaptation of the logMAR test to a mobile
environment showed no technological constraints. While the test
effectively differentiated between severe and mild impairment,
it struggled to identify more subtle variations in vision. Analysis
of facial features uncovered patterns related to the size of letters
perceived, thus affecting reading ability. Preliminary findings
suggest that gestures such as cheek squinting could predict vision impairment, though further validation in larger, longer-term
studies in non-standardized settings is required. Future research
should consider the inclusion of additional sensor data, cross platform compatibility, and integrating facial feature tracking
into daily-use applications. This approach aims to continuously
monitor facial behaviors linked to vision issues, complemented
by regular eye exams for significant evaluations. Thereby, ondevice monitoring and analysis of behavioral data for detecting
ongoing vision deterioration provide a privacy-preserving and
continuous assessment tool that is available independent of time
and location.
