1. Introduction
It is estimated that 3.8 % of the World's population experience depression, with
adults over 60 years of age having a prevalence of 5.7 %.1 In Canada (2022), 14.4 % of
Canadians aged 12 or older reported their mental health as fair or poor.2 This comes
at significant cost to the Canadian healthcare system, with an estimated $21.3 billion
in direct costs in 2011 related to mental illness.3 Visual impairment has been
reported to have a significant association with psychological distress measured using
the General Health Questionnnaire-12 and depression using Beck Depression
Inventory in population-based settings.4,5 It is therefore important to identify
predictors of depression in visually impaired patients to direct services sooner and
mitigate the consequences of mental health disease.
This study investigates the potential of vision tests as surrogates for depression
screening in visually impaired adults, focusing on the relationship between self reported visual function and depression. Building on previous research with mixed
results, we explore reading acuity, critical print size, and fixation stability - tests not
previously examined. We hypothesize that these could be effective depression
screeners, given the importance of reading in the daily lives of low vision patients.

2.1.1. Questionnaires
The questionnaires were completed during the patients’ low vision assessment, at the
same time as the vision tests
Demographic data collected includes age, gender, racial/cultural group, education
level, income, and primary language.
The PHQ-9,6 a validated depression screening tool,7,8 for the visually impaired
population,9 focuses on the last 2-weeks, and consists of a nine-symptom checklist.10
A depression severity score is generated and characterized as either none-minimal
(score 0–4), mild (score 5–9) moderate (score 10–14), moderately severe (score 15–
19) or severe (score 20–27).7,10 A score of ≥10 signifies a likely diagnosis of major
depression (88 % for both sensitivity and specificity).7
The VFQ-25, a validated self-report questionnaire11 assesses patients visual
functioning and vision related quality of life, through Likert scale measures. The
questionnaire consists of 13 domains; 11 vision-related domains, a single-item
general health score, and an overall composite score.12 The highest possible score per
domain is 100 (better functioning).12 In keeping with other studies,13 the driving
subscale was eliminated from the analysis, as a high proportion of study participants
were not driving.

2.1.2. Vision tests
Visual acuity, reading acuity, contrast sensitivity and fixation stability were collected
retrospectively (chart review).
Best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was measured using the Early Treatment Diabetic
Retinopathy Study Chart with illuminator cabinet (from Precision Vision;
https://www.precision-vision.com/ ) binocularly at one meter. Visual acuity was
scored by letter, with a termination rule of ≥3 errors in a single line and was
converted to the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (LogMAR) for
statistical analysis.
The Minnesota Low Vision Reading Chart (MNREAD) iPad application (Precision
Vision),14 a continuous-text acuity test, measured reading acuity (smallest print
identified correctly, with minimum errors) and critical print size (smallest print read
correctly at the fastest speed).15 Standard operating procedure was used.15 Results
were converted to LogMAR for analysis.
Contrast sensitivity was assessed using the Pelli-Robson contrast sensitivity chart
(Precision Vision).16 Patients were tested binocularly at one meter. The log contrast
score was recorded.
The Macular Integrity Assessment (MAIA) microperimeter (CentreVue®, Italy) was
used to measure Fixation Stability as measured by the Bivariate contour ellipse area
(BCEA). The BCEA 95 % measure for the better seeing eye was used; the BCEA 95 % has
proven to be an accurate parameter for reporting fixation stability.17 Fixation losses
>30 % indicate an unreliable test result18 and were classified as unable to perform test
during analysis.

Multivariable linear regressions were performed for each combination of the five

vision tests (distance visual acuity, reading acuity, critical print size, contrast
sensitivity, and microperimetry) as predictors with the PHQ-9 score, the VFQ-25
overall composite score, general health score, and the 10 domain specific VFQ-25
scores (excluding the driving domain) as outcomes. Each of these 65 linear regression
models included age, gender, education level and income group as covariates. Each
vision test was modelled by two predictor variables where one predictor was an
indicator variable for the patient being unable to do the test and the other predictor
Actions you could take:
was a continuous predictor of the test score among people who did the test. The
vision test scores were standardized to have a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1,
so that the beta coefficient for each vision test score would estimate the expected
change in outcome per one standard deviation increase in the vision test score after
controlling for age, gender, education and income among people who did the test.
The association between each vision test and outcome was assessed by the joint test
that the coefficients for the unable indicator variable and the test score variable were
both equal to zero. The raw p-value for each of the joint tests is reported along with
the false discovery rate, which accounts for multiplicity due to testing the statistical
significance of 65 joint tests. We considered an association statistically significant
when it had a false discovery rate below 5 %. We used a similar approach to examine
the association between the VFQ-25 scores and the PHQ-9, except here there was no
indicator variable for unable and we scaled the VFQ-25 so that the parameters
estimate the expected change in PHQ-9 per 25-point increase in VFQ-25.
We applied multiple imputation to data that were missing for reasons other than
being unable to do the test. We generated 100 imputed datasets using the Markov
Chain Monte Carlo method. The imputation model included all variables that were
involved in the regression modelling even though none of the covariates were
missing. We then ran the linear regression separately using each imputed dataset and
obtained overall estimates which combined the multiple imputed regression results
using Rubin's rules.19 Further details are provided in Appendix A (available online).
As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the previously described multivariable
regression analysis without imputation and replacing the PHQ-9 total score and
overall composite VFQ-25 scores with Rasch person measures estimated using the
method of successive dichotomization.20 The Rasch person scores are unitless
measures on the logit scale that account for variable “difficulty” or “endorseability” of
each question and the potential that a fixed unit of increase across the items or across
responses within an item may not be on an interval scale linearly related to the
underlying constructs being measured. Further details are provided in appendix B
(available online).
The Rasch person measures were estimated using the msd package in R.21 All other
analysis were performed using SAS® software (version 9.4, copyright 2016 SAS
Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).22

Consistent with previous studies, we found no significant association between

While our study analyzed the association between fixation stability and depression,
we did not explore visual fields. Previous studies found no significant association
between depression and visual field loss/defects, regardless of the visual field test
used,29, 30, 31 although there may be an association with rate of visual field change
and depression. Diniz-Filho et al. (2016), studying Glaucoma patients, reported that
faster visual field loss resulted in larger changes in the Geriatric Depression Scale.32
Our findings that self-reported visual function rather than specific vision tests is
significantly associated with risk of depression is consistent with results from
previous studies.31,33 Zhang et al. (2013) found self-reported visual function remained
significantly associated with depression after controlling for possible confounding
variables while visual acuity did not.26 Wang et al. (2012), investigating depression
and Glaucoma, concluded objective measures of visual acuity and visual field defects
were not associated with depression, but several of the VFQ-25 questions were.31 A
study of patients with Retinitis Pigmentosa found that while depression was
correlated with lower VFQ-25 scores, it was not correlated with objective vision tests
of weighted visual acuity or functional vision score (combined visual acuities and
visual field measures).33 Silverstein et al. (2021) investigated adults in senior living
facilities and found that neither contrast sensitivity or visual acuity were significantly
related to symptoms of depression, whereas self-reported visual function (VFQ-25)
was significantly correlated to depression score of the Patient Health Questionnaire-2
(PHQ-2).24

While the vision tests were not associated with the PHQ-9, they were associated with
the VFQ-25 mental health domain. In turn, the VFQ-25 mental health domain was
significantly associated with the PHQ-9. While both questionnaires address mental
health, the VFQ-25 mental health domain specifically asks questions related to
vision-specific mental health, whereas the PHQ-9 addresses broader, global changes
in mental health. This difference makes the PHQ-9 less specific for detecting changes
in visual function, which may reduce its sensitivity in this context, thereby
highlighting the complexity of measuring depression and mental health in this
population.
The retrospective study design limited our ability to collect relevant patient health
information and control for potential confounding variables such as pre-existing
depression, comorbidities, concomitant use of anti-depressants, or previous
experience of vision rehabilitation. For simplicity and ease of interpretation, our
regression approach models adjusted linear associations between the vision tests,
VFQ-25 scores and the PHQ-9. This may not be the most efficient measure of
association if associations are non-linear or if the scales themselves are not on a
linear interval scale. We have used the VFQ-25 overall composite and domain scores
as defined by its authors, although we acknowledge that the psychometric properties
of these scales have been criticized.37 Nevertheless, our sensitivity analysis using
Rasch person measures yielded the same conclusions as our main analysis. However,
the Rasch analysis also revealed that a large portion of patients were at or near the
lower end of the PHQ-9 scale which could cause us to miss potential associations
between vision tests and mental health among people with a low risk of clinical
depression according to the PHQ-9. Thus, while our data did not provide any
evidence that the vision tests were associated with risk of clinical depression, it may
be incorrect to conclude that there is no association between vision test results and
mental health more generally. Future, work may wish to consider using a scale more
sensitive to mild depression or mental health in general.
A strength of the study is the inclusion of both clinical vision test measures and self report visual functioning. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to
explore reading acuity, critical print size and fixation stability in association to
depression.
