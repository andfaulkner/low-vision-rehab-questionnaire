Abstract
In order to implement evidence-based practice, a randomized
study was set up to evaluate the ADL- based Health Education
Programme ‘Discovering new ways’ for elderly persons with
age-related macular degeneration.
Purpose: To investigate the impact of this program on
perceived security in the performance of daily activities 28
months after the intervention.
Method: Two-hundred and twenty-nine persons randomized
to either the Health Education programme or an Individual
Intervention Programme participated in the study. At the 28month follow-up there was a dropout of 98 persons and the
results are based on 62 persons participating in the Health
Education Programme and 69 persons in the Individual
Intervention Programme.
Results: There were statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerences in
perceived security between the groups in 15 out of 28 daily
activities. Furthermore, the Health Education Group showed a
signiﬁcant tendency towards an improved level of security
while the Individual Intervention Group tended to deteriorate.
Conclusions: The ﬁndings provide strong support for the long term eﬀect of the programme and for the implementation of
evidence-based practice. The study corroborates the eﬀectiveness of the Health Education Programme in enhancing security
and hindering a progressive decline in perceived security in
daily activities.

Introduction
A profession has a responsibility to ensure the best
practice that has proved to be eﬀective and to use
evidence in practice.1, 2 In order to implement

evidence-based practice, a randomized study was set
up to evaluate the ADL- based Health Education
Programme ‘Discovering new ways’.3 The main goal of
the programme, which targets elderly persons with
age-related macular degeneration (AMD), is to sustain
and restore the participants performance of their daily
activities and to enable the early detection of those
who perceive insecurity when performing daily activities. The programme was evaluated at a 4-month
follow-up in terms of perceived security in daily activities4 and was found to enhance security or hinder a
progressive decline in perceived security in daily activities. Further research is needed to investigate whether
the changes will continue in the long term. The aim of
the present study is thus to examine the long-term stability of perceived security in daily activities.
AMD is a severe, progressive, chronic eye disease of
the central part of the retina (macula), which rarely
leads to total blindness5 and is the most common cause
of irreversible visual impairment among elderly persons
past the age of 60. Studies show that about 30% of those
who are 75 years or older are aﬀected by AMD.6, 7
Elderly persons with AMD3 claim to have problems in
several activities as a result of their visual impairment.
A population study8 of 85-year-olds with AMD found
this group to be more dependent on personal help in
both personal and instrumental daily activities
compared with persons of the same age with normal
vision. Since no cure exists for the majority of people
with AMD, this chronic impairment thus represents an
important public health problem.

Education Programme ‘Discovering new ways’ occurred
in three stages, pre-intervention stage, intervention and

Methods
STUDY DESIGN

The study was carried out at a low vision clinic at a
university-aﬃliated hospital in Sweden as a randomized
longitudinal study. The study was designed so that the
follow-ups took place 1, 4, 16 and 28 months after the
intervention was completed. The Ethics Committee of
the faculty of medicine at the university approved the
study. All persons gave their informed consent to taking
part in the study.

Intervention Programme n = 69, Health Education

STUDY PROCEDURE

The participants were randomly assigned, according
to a random number table, either to the Health Education Programme or to an Individual Intervention
Programme that was standard at the low vision clinic.
The occupational therapists that collected the data were
not blinded to the composition of the groups but were
not involved in the program.
Assessments at baseline and at the 28-month follow up were both made when the participants in the
Health Education Programme and in the Individual
Intervention Programme attended the low vision
clinic. The study procedure did not diﬀer between
the programmes. The independently registered occupational therapists interviewed the participants according
to a structured protocol, consisting of questions about
marital status, living arrangements, social service,
granted public transportation and perceived health.
An assessment was also made of perceived security
in performing daily activities, further described
below.10 An optometrist made the optical assessment
at the visit. Visual acuity was tested with a letter chart
(Monoyer-Granström, Kifa), graded 0.1 – 1.0 at a
distance of 5 m, with the person’s own glasses and
with best refraction.

STUDY POPULATION

The target group comprized all persons referred by an
ophthalmologist to rehabilitation and who were attending the low vision clinic for the ﬁrst time. The inclusion
criteria were as follows: 65 years or older, living at
home, aged-related macular degeneration as the primary

The primary outcome measure was perceived security
in performing daily activities.3, 10 The instrument has
been tested for reliability and responsiveness and was
found to have a high level of within-subject stability
and responsiveness. The instrument has also face and
content validity.10

The instrument is a questionnaire, which consists of
seven areas with a total of 28 items. The areas are as
follows: meals, self-care and care of clothing, communication, cleaning, mobility, shopping and ﬁnancial
management. The perceived security in performing each
speciﬁed task is rated by means of a four-point ordinal
scale: very insecure, insecure, quite secure, secure. The
instrument is self-rated, and the participants were
instructed to ﬁll in the questionnaire on their own after
instructions from the occupational therapists. For those
persons who had trouble reading, the rater read each
question and each alternative response. The participants
were also given the response alternatives in enlarged
print. The participants were instructed to assess their
actual performance and how they managed at that very
moment. They had to perform the activity independently, and in those activities where they asked for help
in a limited part of the performance were regarded as
independent. They were instructed to leave out any
activities that they did not perform.

The non-participants (n = 83) diﬀered statistically
signiﬁcantly from the participants (n = 131), with a
higher proportion of persons who had been granted

the other characteristics; gender, age, living alone, social

no statistical signiﬁcant diﬀerence between the dropouts

INTERVENTION WITH THE HEALTH EDUCATION PROGRAMME:
DISCOVERING NEW WAYS

The programme was carried out with groups of four
to six persons with age-related macular degeneration.
Twenty groups were formed consecutively during the
study period. The intervention period was 8 weeks long,
and the groups met for 2 h once a week. The programme
and its theoretical basis have been described elsewhere.3, 11 The groups were led by experienced occupational therapists trained in leading the groups. The
occupational therapist provided information and skills
training and guided and encouraged the group participants in the learning process. Other health professionals
such as an ophthalmologist, optometrist, low vision
therapist and lighting expert were invited to give information.
The information and the skills training given by the
occupational therapist proceeded from diﬀerent strategies in daily activities described by elderly persons with
age-related macular degeneration.3 The strategies were
used as a problem-solving model, and the participants
were enabled to use the model as a way of thinking when
performing daily activities.
A booklet12 containing the information given by
health professionals as well as the information about
daily activities was produced and used in the Health
Education Programme. The participants were asked to

PROGRAMME

The Individual Intervention Programme for the study
was the standard intervention for the target group at the
low vision clinic. The participants were provided with
optical aids with the aim to improve reading and near
and distance viewing. Hand and stand magniﬁers as well
as glasses for reading were prescribed and introduced at
the clinic. Assistive devices and glasses were brought
home directly for practical application. Information
about lighting mainly for reading was given, and the
participants also received information about the disease
if requested. The Individual Intervention typically
included 1 – 2 1-h sessions at the clinic followed up by
telephone contact within 2 to 4 weeks. The intervention
was carried out by occupational therapists with special
training in low vision.

STATISTICAL METHODS

The median, mean and range were calculated for
description of the data. The 95% conﬁdence interval
(CI) for diﬀerence between participants, non-participants and dropouts were calculated for proportions
using exact formulas for the binomial distribution and
means13 plus for diﬀerences between the Health Education Group and the Individual Intervention Group.
The impact of the Health Education Programme on
changes in perceived security in performance of daily
activities was evaluated using a non-parametric statistical approach developed for evaluation of ordered categorical data.14, 15 By this method, is it possible to
measure systematic patterns of change for the group
separately from the individual change unexplained by
the systematic group change. In this study a measure
of systematic group change, Relative Position (RP),
and a measure of individual variation of change, Relative Rank Variance (RV), have been used.
Each item was evaluated in respect to the following
issues:

The intervention eﬀect on each item of performance was evaluated by means of a contingency
table,14 which shows the frequency distribution
of the two assessments of perceived security preand post-intervention. The four categories of

perceived security were very insecure (1), insecure (2), quite secure (3) and secure (4). The main
diagonal of unchanged level of perceived security
is oriented from the lower-left to the upper-right
corner in this approach, (ﬁgure 1). Figure 1a
shows that 22 (19 + 3) persons out of 51 rated

The presence of systematic change in perceived
security in performance is evident from the
categorical distributions, also called the marginal
distributions, measured on the two occasions.
The marginal distribution in ﬁgure 1a is 4, 11, 15
and 21 at baseline and 0, 2, 6 and 43 at the 28month evaluation. The diﬀerence between those
marginal distributions indicate a systematic
change over time, which can be visualized by
plotting the cumulative proportions of those
marginal distributions against each other, starting with the point (0.0). This plot forms a kind of
Relative Operating Characteristic (ROC)
curve.14 A ROC curve below the diagonal
indicates an improvement, (ﬁgure 1a) and a
ROC curve above the diagonal indicates deterioration (ﬁgure 1c). By the ROC curve it is
possible to identify and to locate the most
important changes in perceived security in
performance of daily activities over time. Possible values of RP range between 7 1 and 1, and
zero value mean lack of systematic change for
the group. The higher value, the higher change.14
Dispersed observations in the contingency table,
a sign of individual variations in change between
the two occasions unexplained by the measures
of group change, are measured by Relative
Rank Variance. The measure takes into account
the distances between the assessments. Figure 1b
shows an example of a contingency table where
the assessments are spread over the table. For
example, in the activity ‘writing a memo to
yourself’ the 7 persons assessing themselves as
very insecure (1) at baseline and as secure (4) at
evaluation (RV = 0.28, CI 0.09 to 0.47) are the
main contributors of the individual dispersion
since their rank values diﬀer greatly. The higher
the value of RV (0 4 RV 4 1), the more
heterogeneous are the individual changes,14
RV 4 0.1 is considered cut-oﬀ value for evidence of heterogeneity. The 95% conﬁdence
intervals were calculated for the RV and RP
measures using the data-based simulation bootstrap method.16

DAILY ACTIVITIES BETWEEN THE GROUPS

The study showed statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerences in
systematic change (RP) in perceived security between the
Health Education Group and the Individual Intervention Group in several activities. The non-overlapping
conﬁdence intervals for RP between the two groups indicate statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerences in perceived security in 15 activities. The values of RP varied from 7 0.09
to 0.47 with a median of 0.25 in the Health Education
Group. In the Individual Intervention Group the range
of RP values varied from 7 0.32 to 0.15 with a median
of 7 0.14. The Health Education Group changed
towards an improved level of security compared to the
Individual Intervention Group who changed towards a
deteriorated level of security, as evident from the 95%
CI of the RP values in table 2. For example, a systematic
change towards a lower level of security in ‘pouring out
coﬀee/tea for yourself’ was found in the individual intervention group (RP = 7 0.04, 95% CI 7 0.23 to 0.15),
whereas a systematic change towards a higher level of
perceived security was found in the health education
group for the same activity (RP = 0.35, CI 0.21 to 0.5).
The conﬁdence intervals for the groups do not overlap
each other, which is strong evidence for statistically
signiﬁcant diﬀerences between the groups.
Both groups showed individual changes (RV) in
perceived security but no statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerence was found between the groups. The values of RV
among the participants in the Health Education Group
varied from 0.04 to 0.32, as shown in table 2, with a
median of 0.16. The RV values in the Individual Intervention Group ranged between 0.05 and 0.46 with a

The randomized study was set up to secure evidence based practice in occupational therapy. According to
statistical theory, randomization is a method of eliminating bias, thereby establishing that the diﬀerences
between the groups are caused by the intervention. As
blinding technique was not applied with regard to the
intervention, group bias can occur through subconscious eﬀects. However, this might not be a serious
problem since the evaluators were not involved in the
intervention and most of all because the measurement
of the outcome was based on self-reports.13 The ﬁndings
in this study provide substantial support for evidence based practice.
The non-participants and the drop-outs did not diﬀer
from the participants at baseline, apart from the nonparticipants who to a higher degree had been granted
public transportation service. This could indicate that
the non-participants were more disabled, but the other
characteristics contradict this. The participants in this
study seems therefore to be representative of the elderly
population with age related macular degeneration that
comes to the low vision clinic for their ﬁrst time.
There were statistically signiﬁcant diﬀerences in
systematic group change (RP) between the Health
Education Group and the Individual Intervention
Group as regards 15 daily activities. Besides these diﬀerences there were signiﬁcant changes of RP in improvement of 20 daily activities in the Health Education
Group and deterioration in 12 daily activities in the
Individual Intervention Group. This means that in ﬁve
of the activities in the Health Education Group and ﬁve
in the Individual Intervention Group there were additional signiﬁcant group changes (RP) within the groups,
apart from signiﬁcant diﬀerences between the groups
(those marked in italic but not bold in table 2). The
Health Education Group changed towards an improvement in these activities and the Individual Intervention

The ﬁndings in this study are in agreement with the 4month evaluation4 demonstrating long-term stability of
the improvement of perceived security in daily occupation in those following the Health Education
Programme. The powerful components of the model in
the Health Education Programme ‘Discovering new
ways’ such as theoretical basis and tools for learning
have been discussed more in depth in previous articles.4, 11 However, we want to highlight the importance
of client-centred practice.2 Focus groups methodology
is a method that enables the target group to participate
in the planning and development of the programme and
is therefore more likely to meet their needs. The advantages of the focus group methodology are that the
profession can become familiar with its market and
acquire knowledge of the target population and the
factors that might inﬂuence daily activities. Focus group
methodology gave an opportunity for collaboration and
partnership1, 19 with the target group to develop the
programme. A client-centred approach involving the
client in all phases of the intervention is supported in
the literature19 and might be one of the explanations
why the programme has been found to be so successful
so far.
The programme was developed from a broad
approach combining qualitative and quantitative
methods with their diﬀerent advantages and
strengths.20, 21 The methods were used in sequence so
that what was learned from the studies in the preintervention stage was added to what was learned
from the studies in the intervention stage, and so
on. The strengths of diﬀerent methods can be successfully combined to address the complexity of the
research question and when the goals of the project
include both pure research and applied uses in practice.21 Further, evidence-based practice uses research
evidence to make decisions about intervention that
are eﬀective for the speciﬁc target group.2 The metho-

The long-term evaluation provides evidence that the
Health Education Programme supports the elderly
person by enabling change. Studies show17, 18 that elderly
persons with visual impairment have multiple health
problems and that all those health problems must be
addressed when developing a programme. The Health
Education Programme described in this article is one
example of a programme tailored to meet the needs of
elderly with AMD based on the target group’s perceived
security in daily activities. When developing a
programme for elderly persons, it is vital to focus not
only on one impairment but to see the person as an integrated whole, considering the multiple health problems
of the population and how they aﬀect daily activities.
The study has shown that an intervention such as the
one described above is eﬀective, allowing people to
continue to live with a chronic progressive disease independently. Further research will be conducted in order
to investigate whether the programme has any impact
on perceived health and morbidity. A health economic
evaluation will also be made of the programme.
In conclusion, the ﬁndings provide strong support for
the long-term eﬀect of the programme for persons with
AMD. There is strong evidence that the Health Education Programme enhances security and hinders a
progressive decline in perceived security in daily activities, thereby providing the material necessary for
evidence-based practice.
