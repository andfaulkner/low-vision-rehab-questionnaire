Design of the Low Vision Quality-of-Life
Questionnaire (LVQOL) and Measuring the
Outcome of Low-vision Rehabilitation
JAMES S. WOLFFSOHN, BSCOPTOM, PHD, AND ANTHEA L. COCHRANE, BSCOPTOM

● PURPOSE:

To design and validate a vision-specific
quality-of-life assessment tool to be used in a clinical
setting to evaluate low-vision rehabilitation strategy and
management.
● METHODS: Previous vision-related questionnaires were
assessed by low-vision rehabilitation professionals and
patients for relevance and coverage. The 74 items selected were pretested to ensure correct interpretation.
One hundred and fifty patients with low vision completed
the chosen questions on four occasions to allow the
selection of the most appropriate items. The vision specific quality of life of patients with low vision was
compared with that of 70 age-matched and gender matched patients with normal vision and before and after
low-vision rehabilitation in 278 patients.
● RESULTS: Items that were unreliable, internally inconsistent, redundant, or not relevant were excluded,
resulting in the 25-item Low Vision Quality-of-Life
Questionnaire (LVQOL). Completion of the LVQOL
results in a summed score between 0 (a low quality of
life) and 125 (a high quality of life). The LVQOL has a
high internal consistency (␣ ⴝ 0.88) and good reliability
(0.72). The average LVQOL score for a population with
low vision (60.9 ⴞ 25.1) was significantly lower than the
average score of those with normal vision (100.3 ⴞ
20.8). Rehabilitation improved the LVQOL score of
those with low vision by an average of 6.8 ⴞ 15.6 (17%).
● CONCLUSIONS: The LVQOL was shown to be an
internally consistent, reliable, and fast method for measuring the vision-specific quality of life of the visually
impaired in a clinical setting. It is able to quantify the
quality of life of those with low vision and is useful in

HERE IS A NEED FOR A MEASURE OF THE OUTCOME OF

low-vision rehabilitation to be able to argue the case
of low-vision assessment within managed care plans,
to improve services offered, and to secure and enhance
funding for low-vision services.1 There are significant
individual, public health, and community consequences of
reduced vision, such as increased cost of education, reduced personal income, and loss of productivity for those
caring for or assisting the visually impaired. Relative to the
costs of visual impairment, it has been suggested that the
provision of low-vision rehabilitation services may be quite
low. The prevalence of low vision is likely to increase
significantly over the next decade because the percentage
of elderly people, who are most often afflicted with low
vision, is increasing.1
There are many measures taken in a routine low-vision
assessment on which successful outcome has been rated.
These measures include distance acuity,2 near acuity,3,4
reading speed and duration,5,6 and comprehension and
fluency.5 The term “success” is often used in visual rehabilitation, based on whether a patient finds an aid beneficial (as defined by the patient) and uses it to solve one or
more visual problems,7,8 or failed to attend follow-up
appointments.9 Some studies have examined the ability of
patients with low vision to perform simple tasks, such as
identifying paper currency and clock reading, before and
after low-vision rehabilitation, but the results vary with
the examiner performing the assessment, and implementation is time consuming and expensive.10
As a more detailed answer to the question of how to
measure outcome, subjective quality-of-life questionnaires
have been developed. They aim to identify and quantify
the impact of visual impairment on aspects of daily living,
such as self-care, mobility, and dependency.11 It is recognized that patients are not interested in improvements in
a biomedical indicator, but rather in how treatment affects
their quality of life.12,13

Various simple questionnaires have been used to assess
quality of life in low vision.6,14 –16 However, most have
been developed using a population with reduced vision
from potentially treatable conditions11,17,18 or for people
with a single ocular condition causing low vision,12,15,19 –21
so few are valid for the measurement of the outcome of
low-vision rehabilitation in a general population with low
vision. Visually related quality-of-life questionnaires, such
as the VF-14 developed for cataract assessment,22,23 have
been modified to be appropriate for particular populations,
but the validity of these modifications have not been
examined. Measurement of quality of life with many of the
questionnaires used in research studies is very time consuming, and therefore their clinical application would
place a significant burden on patients.24,25
Hoppe and associates26 examined the quality of life of
patients with low vision before and after a low-vision
assessment, finding no significant improvement had been
achieved. However, the questionnaire used was not specific
to patients with nontreatable visual loss and tapped traits
not relevant to rehabilitation of the visually impaired.
Scott and associates27 found that quality of life in 156
patients was unchanged by low-vision rehabilitation when
measured by a general health-related questionnaire (SF36), but improved with vision-specific questionnaires.
However, they noted that neither of the vision-specific
questionnaires used (VF-14 and NEI-VFQ-51) had been
designed specifically for a population with low vision.
The current study aimed to develop a questionnaire,
using patients with a wide variety of conditions causing
nontreatable vision loss, which addresses issues relating to
794

quality-of-life reduction in those with low vision and is
able to quantify the benefit of low-vision rehabilitation.
The tool developed will be of great benefit to low-vision
specialists if it enables fast and accurate audit of the
outcome of low-vision rehabilitation and objectively examines of the benefits of different models of rehabilitation
and care of the visually impaired.

METHODS
THE LITERATURE WAS REVIEWED TO COLLECT ALL THE

subjective questionnaires previously designed for assessing
quality of life (Table 1). The resulting questions were
merged, so no items were repeated. The items were then
assessed by a multidisciplinary team of low-vision rehabilitation staff (including ophthalmologists, optometrists,
orthoptists, occupational therapists, welfare officers, audiologists, and those with low vision) for relevance: questions not relevant to the majority of patients with low
vision were excluded; coverage: whether all areas of life
affected by low vision, such as activities of daily living,
social and emotional factors, were examined; face validity:
whether questions appear on the surface to be measuring
what they actually are.
Closed-end questions, with a specific set of responses,
were used, because they can be consistently scored. The
study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review
Board, and informed consent was obtained from subjects
participating in the experiment.
The resulting questionnaire had 74 questions that were

pretested to ensure correct interpretation by the users. The
questionnaire was printed in writing of sufficient size
(equivalent to N20) for most visually impaired people to
read. Many different response options have been used by
previous vision-related quality-of-life questionnaires (Table 1). An ordinate rather than a line scale was used for the
questionnaire to allow implementation over the phone as
well as in person and by post. A six-point scale was
instituted to allow a reasonable level of discrimination,
while not causing confusion among the mostly elderly
population examined. Patients with low vision responded
to items grading them between 5 (having no difficulty with
the item because of their vision) and 1 (having a great
difficulty with the item because of their vision) or as 0
(item could no longer be performed because of their
vision). They also had the option to rate the question as
“not relevant” if it was not relevant to them in their daily
lives (given an average score so that those individuals who
scored more items irrelevant did not have a lower summed
questionnaire score and therefore an apparently worse
quality of life). Missed questions (contained in less than
0.5% of questionnaires) were also given an average score.
The 74-item questionnaire was trialed on 150 patients
with low vision randomly selected from the database of the
Vision Australia Foundation. They had a wide range of
common conditions causing low vision in a developed
world country. Distance visual acuities ranged from 6/7.5
to 1/60 with visual field defects affecting the central 20
degrees of visual field in 53%. The questionnaire was
completed on four occasions, each separated by 4 weeks (⫾
4 days) by the same implementation method each time (by
post, phone, or in-person interviews). The aim of the
initial trial of the questionnaire was to identify those items
that were most reliable, internally consistent, not redundant, relevant to most subjects, and that the full range of
response options were used. The 25 items that met these
criteria formed the Low Vision Quality-of-Life Questionnaire (LVQOL) (Figure 1).
To assess the LVQOL ability to differentiate the visually
impaired from those with normal vision, the LVQOL was
completed by an age-matched and gender-matched control
group (of 70 subjects) attending a large public optometric
clinic in Melbourne for a routine eye examination.
The LVQOL was sent to 515 consecutive first-time
patients of a low-vision clinic (which provides free rehabilitation services) before their appointment for rehabilitation. A second LVQOL was sent to patients after
rehabilitation, which consisted of:
1. A 15-minute to 30-minute interview with a case
manager to identify visual needs. Details on the
patient’s age, gender, living status, and primary condition causing visual impairment (as diagnosed by
the referring ophthalmologist) were recorded.
2. A 60-minute low-vision assessment with an optometrist, which included a review of the patient’s ocular
history and visual complaints, establishment of visual

goals, retinoscopy and refraction at distance and
near, measurement of visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, and central visual field. Advice was given (for
example, on lighting and contrast), and magnification aids were prescribed if appropriate. For each
patient, the following clinical information was collected (consulting rooms had an illuminance of 500
lux): progression of visual difficulties as stated by the
patient; distance visual acuity (Bailey-Lovie distance
logMAR chart back-lit to a luminance of 160 cd/m2)
scored as the number of letters correctly identified
with effort; near visual acuity (Practical Near Acuity
Chart at 25 cm, read with the best distance correction and a ⫹4.00-diopter near addition)28; magnification aids; presence of a visual field loss or
disruption in the central 20 degrees field of view,
measured using an Amsler chart at 14 cm; peak
contrast sensitivity (Melbourne Edge Test, luminance 47 cd/m2 at 40 cm)29; assessment of the
patient’s psychological status based on a reduced
version of the Nottingham Adjustment Scale30,31;
services from members of a multidisciplinary team as
appropriate to their needs. The team includes orthoptists, occupational therapists, orientation and
mobility instructors, audiologists, and welfare officers
who offer assessment and assistance either at the
clinic or at the patient’s residence.31
Internal consistency was examined using the item-total
correlation and Cronbach’s ␣, reliability using the intraclass correlation coefficient, and principal components
using varimax rotation factor analysis.32 The summed score
of the LVQOL items was calculated for analysis, as has
been previously recommended for quality-of-life analysis.30
Analysis of variance was used to examine overall effects
between groups and Tukey’s pairwise multiple comparison
test to assess individual group differences. Correlation
models were examined using basic subsets.

RESULTS
● SELECTION OF APPROPRIATE ITEMS FOR THE LVQOL:

To select the most appropriate items for the LVQOL from
the initial pool of questions, the following exclusion
criteria were implemented:
Frequency of Endorsement and Discrimination. The proportion of subjects who gave each response alternative was
calculated. This allowed the exclusion of five questions
that were not relevant to a significant proportion of the
subjects (taken at 33%). They were related to using public
transport alone, playing sports and games, going to the
cinema or theater, and how much benefit was gained by
talking to a peer worker. Items with very low or high
endorsement were also eliminated, because little is learned

Homogeneity and Internal Consistency. A scale must be
homogenous, tapping different aspects of the same attribute and not different parts of different traits. Therefore,
items should be moderately correlated to one another, and
each should correlate with the total scale score. The most
sensible index of a particular sub-scale would appear to be
a simple summing of the scores.32 Such a linear model is
valid only if all items are measuring the same trait. If items
are highly correlated, latter questions add little information, suggesting redundancy with a possible loss of content
validity. If the correlations are too low, then the questions
are tapping different traits. The two measures calculated
were the item-total correlation (correlation of an individual item with its sub-scale without that item) and Cronbach’s ␣ (a measure of internal consistency that should be
between 0.70 and 0.90).33 Item-total correlation was less
than 0.20 for four questions regarding general health, the
impact of the low-vision assessment, and the feeling of
independence. These questions were therefore excluded
from the questionnaire, because they do not tap the same
trait as the rest of the scale. Cronbach’s ␣ resulted in the
removal of no further questions.

Factor Analysis. A vision-related quality-of-life questionnaire can either test different aspects of vision using
sub-scales, such as the Activities of Daily Vision Scale,34 or
produce a summed score of the patients’ quality of life,
such as the VF-14.22,23 The LVQOL is of the latter type,
with the 25 items producing a summed score between 0
and 125 (the higher the score, the higher the quality of
life). Analysis of the results by implementation method did
not alter the final LVQOL items. The LVQOL summed
score showed a high internal consistency (Cronbach’s ␣ ⫽
0.88) and a good reliability (0.72). Factor analysis using
varimax rotation identified up to four principal factors in
the LVQOL, each with a high internal consistency (Cronbach’s ␣ greater than 0.80). The LVQOL items were
grouped into these sub-sections (general vision, mobility,
and lighting issues; psychological adjustment; reading and
fine work; activities of daily living).
● ASSESSING THE VISION SPECIFIC QUALITY OF LIFE OF
SUBJECTS WITH NORMAL VISION: The

LVQOL was
designed to measure the quality of life of those with visual
impairment and must therefore differentiate those with
low vision from those with normal vision. The LVQOL
was completed by an age-matched (68.3 ⫾ 10.5 years) and
gender-matched (39% male) control group. The average
(⫾ SD) LVQOL score for those with normal vision was
significantly higher than those from the population with
low vision (100.3 ⫾ 20.8 versus 60.9 ⫾ 25.1; P ⬍ .001).
Over 67% of those with normal vision had a LVQOL score
of 95 or greater compared with less than 20% of those with
low vision (Figure 2).

variance in the prerehabilitation LVQOL measures and

acuity measures, contrast sensitivity, or a patient’s age. The

amount of information lost, and with only 25 questions,
the individual item responses can easily be recorded for
reference regarding specific variables if needed.
A single questionnaire is unlikely ever to cover the
whole range of quality-of-life issues in visual impairment
for every individual, because the extreme length of a
totally comprehensive questionnaire would preclude its use
in clinical and many research settings.18 However, the
LVQOL provides useful clinical information that, in addition to functional measures of vision, such as visual acuity,
contrast sensitivity, and visual field, describe the visual
status of an individual. Such information can be used to
assess the outcome of low-vision rehabilitation and will
help to identify those cases where further training, service
provision, or re-assessment may be needed to improve the
quality of life of an individual.
The LVQOL was shown to differentiate a population
with low vision from an age-matched and gender-matched
group attending an optometric clinic. Some of the age matched and gender-matched group will have had early
cataract, blepharitis, dry eye, and other diseases affecting
vision. Because they were all on state benefit, they were
also likely to be of a lower socio-economic status than the
clinic population with low vision. Therefore, it is not
surprising that some members of this group rated their
vision-related quality of life as reduced, but on the whole
their quality of life was much higher (approximately 40
units of the 125 range of the LVQOL) than the population
with low vision. Therefore, the 6.7-point average improvement in LVQOL score equates to a 17% improvement in
quality of life. This equates well with the 5.4-point

22.2-point improvement in overall score (on a 1300-point

As expected, the LVQOL score was related to functional measures of vision, such as distance and near visual
acuity, contrast sensitivity and field loss, but in combination these factors accounted for only 32% of the variance.
Age was not related to the LVQOL score, which again
confirms it is not measuring age-related visual or health related factors (such as presbyopia). Improvements in
quality of life with rehabilitation were not associated with
patient demographics (age and gender), visual function
(visual acuity and contrast sensitivity), or the disease
causing low vision. Therefore, it would seem that people
attending a low-vision clinic with visual loss from any
condition receive significant benefit to their quality of life
from low-vision rehabilitation. The age-matched and genVOL. 130, NO. 6

Those patients judged to have reached a psychological state of realistic acceptance had a higher quality of
life and a greater improvement with low-vision rehabilitation than those who were still adjusting to their
visual loss, as would be expected. This finding emphasized the need for a basic assessment of a patient’s
psychological state to provide important additional
information to functional visual measures. Those subjects that made use of the multidisciplinary rehabilitation team had a lower quality of life, but a greater
improvement with rehabilitation, than those who did
not make use of the team. This may have been because
of patients’ self-selection of whether they would accept
the additional (free) services offered to them but would
indicate that the multidisciplinary approach to low vision rehabilitation is an important way to maximize
quality of life, especially of those with greater functional
loss. Further research is needed to assess what elements
of rehabilitation, such as number of clinic visits, are
necessary for improving the quality of life of the visually
impaired.
By using quality-of-life measurement tools in a clinical
and research setting, it should be possible to determine
changes in the way in which low-vision rehabilitation can
be performed to achieve greater improvements in a patient’s quality of life. However, as long as the condition
causing the reduced vision remains untreatable, a 100%
improvement cannot be expected. Examining the link

not bias the results collected. Translating the LVQOL into

other than English. To improve response rates further,
those who attend the clinic without completing the
questionnaire could be requested to fill out the LVQOL

after rehabilitation could increase the response rate among
those who fail to complete the postrehabilitation questionnaire.
The LVQOL takes 5 to 10 minutes to complete and
would therefore appear to not put undue strain on a person
with low vision. The average LVQOL score (approximately 60 out of 125) demonstrates that a population of
patients with low vision perceived their functional status
and quality of life to be markedly impaired. Care was taken
to exclude culture-specific aspects of lifestyle, such as
technology, from the LVQOL to make it applicable in a
wide variety of cultural settings, so it could be used
worldwide without need for alteration of content.
In conclusion, the LVQOL has been shown to be an
internally consistent, reliable, and sensitive measure of the
quality of life in those with untreatable visual loss. It can
be used in a clinical setting to provide important additional information to conventional functional visual measures, without significant burden on patients. It is able to
quantify the quality of life of those with low vision and is
useful in determining the effects of low-vision rehabilitation.
