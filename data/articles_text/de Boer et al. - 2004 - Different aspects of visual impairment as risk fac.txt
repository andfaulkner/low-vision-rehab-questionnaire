Different Aspects of Visual Impairment as Risk Factors for Falls and
Fractures in Older Men and Women
Michiel R de Boer,1 Saskia MF Pluijm,2 Paul Lips,2,3 Annette C Moll,1 Hennie J Völker-Dieben,1
Dorly JH Deeg,2,4 and Ger HMB van Rens1

ABSTRACT: Visual impairment has been implicated as a risk factor for falling and fractures, but results of
previous studies have been inconsistent. The relationship between several aspects of vision and falling/
fractures were examined in a prospective cohort study in 1509 older men and women. The analyses showed
that impaired vision is an independent risk factor for both recurrent falling and fractures.
Introduction: Falls and fractures are a major health problem among the elderly. Visual impairment has been
implicated as a risk factor for both falls and fractures. However, results from studies are inconsistent. The
inconsistency between findings can primarily be attributed to differences in the designs of these studies. Most studies
have been cross-sectional or case-control studies, and many have not correctly adjusted for potential confounders.
Furthermore, until now, the potential mediating effects of functional limitation, physical performance, and physical
activity have not been examined.
Materials and Methods: A total of 1509 people was examined in 1995–1996. Contrast sensitivity was assessed with
the VCTS_6000-1 chart for near vision. In addition, self-reported visual impairment was assessed by questions on
recognizing faces from a distance of 4 m, reading the small print in the newspaper, and problems with glare.
Furthermore, many potential confounders and mediators were assessed. Falls and fractures were assessed prospectively during a 3-year follow-up period. The associations between the vision variables and falls and fractures were
examined using Cox proportional hazards analyses.
Results: After adjustment for potential confounders, contrast sensitivity was shown to be associated with recurrent
falling (hazard ratio [HR] ⫽ 1.5), and the question on recognizing faces was shown to be associated with fractures
(HR ⫽ 3.1). Furthermore, functional limitations and physical performance were shown to be mediators in the
relationship between vision variables and recurrent falling/fractures.
Conclusions: The results indicate that impaired vision is an independent risk factor for falling and fractures, but
different aspects of visual functioning may have different relationships to falling and fractures.

INTRODUCTION

ALL AND FALL-RELATED fractures are a major public
health problem among the elderly. Thirty percent of
people over the age of 65 who live independently fall at
least once a year, and 15% fall at least twice a year.(1–3) It
has been well established that falls and fractures are among
the principal causes of disability, admission to institutional
care, and mortality in the elderly.(4,5) Examining treatable
risk factors might help to develop preventive measures that
can be included in prevention strategies. Visual impairment
has been implicated as a treatable risk factor for both falls
and fractures.(6,7)

In a recent review on the association between impaired
vision and injuries, it was concluded that visually impaired
people have a 1.7 times higher risk of falling, a 1.9 times
higher risk of recurrent falling, and a 1.3–1.9 times higher
risk of fractures.(8) However, some studies that were included in this review did not find any association. Furthermore, the risk estimate in the studies that did find an
association ranged from 1.2 to 5.4 for falling and from 1.3
to 6.0 for fractures.
The inconsistency between findings can be attributed to
differences in designs, statistical analyses, and duration of
follow-up of these studies. Most were cross-sectional or
used a case-control design and did not adjust correctly for
potential confounders. To examine the strength of the association between vision and falls or fractures accurately,

needed. Furthermore, to our knowledge, prospective studies
that examine the relation of impaired vision with both
falling and fractures within the same study population have
not been described. This hampers the interpretation of different observed risks for falls and fractures.
Previous studies have shown that impaired visual acuity
is a risk factor for self-reported and performance-based
problems in activities such as getting in and out of bed and
ascending stairs.(9,10) It has also been shown that functional
limitations, physical performance, and level of physical
activity are risk factors for fractures and falls.(2,6,7,11) This
may indicate that the association between poor vision and
falls or fractures is partly mediated by functional limitations, physical performance, and level of physical activity.
However, studies specifically examining these potential mediating effects have not been reported.
In this paper, we present findings from an ongoing cohort
study among elderly in The Netherlands. Falls and fractures
were both assessed prospectively for a period of 3 years, and
the multifactorial design allowed for correction of various
potential confounders and examination of potential mediators. The purposes of this study were (1) to examine whether
impairments in near contrast sensitivity and other aspects of
vision are associated with falls and fractures within the
same cohort of people and (2) to examine the potential role
of physical performance, functional limitations, and physical activity as mediators in the associations between visual
impairment and falls and fractures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Study sample
The Longitudinal Aging Study Amsterdam (LASA) is an
ongoing cohort study on predictors and consequences of
changes in autonomy and well being in the aging population
in The Netherlands.(12) The sampling and data collection
procedures have been described in detail elsewhere.(13,14)
Briefly, a random sample of 3107 older men and women
(55– 85 years of age) living in The Netherlands, stratified by
age, gender, and urbanization, were enrolled in the baseline
examination in 1992 or 1993. From 1995 to 1996, 2302
(87.2%) of the 2639 eligible respondents completed a main
interview. Lack of follow-up between the first and second
cycle was mainly attributable to the death of the participant:
417 of 3107 (13.4%) respondents died. This study was
performed in a subsample of the LASA sample that consisted of respondents who participated in the medical interview of the second data collection cycle and who were born
in or before 1930 (ⱖ65 years of age as of January 1, 1996).
Of the 1720 eligible respondents, 1509 (87.7%) took part in
the medical interview. These persons were asked to participate in a 3-year prospective fall and fracture follow-up
study. Informed written consent was obtained from all respondents. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics
Committee of the Vrije Universiteit Medical Center
(VUMC) and conducted according to the principles of the
Helsinki declaration.

Measurements
Assessment of falls: For 3 years, participants were asked
to record fall events every week on a fall-and-fracture
calendar and to mail the calendar page to the institute after
3 months. They were contacted by telephone if the procedure of the calendar was too complicated for them, if no
calendar was returned even after a reminder, or if the
calendar was completed incorrectly. Proxies were contacted
if participants were not able to respond. These were persons
from the same household as the participant or someone from
the nursing staff if the participant was institutionalized.
A fall was defined as an unintentional change in position
resulting in coming to rest at a lower level or on the
ground.(15) A recurrent faller was defined as a subject who
fell at least two times within a period of 6 months during the
3-year fall follow-up.(16) The survival time was defined as
the time between the first and second falls if a pair of falls
occurred ⬍6 months apart, the date of death, or the date of
the last follow-up.(11)
Ascertainment of fractures: Participants were also asked
to record any fractures that occurred in the 3-year follow-up
period on the fall-and-fracture calendar every 3 months. In
addition, information on fractures was collected retrospectively in 1998 and 1999. The general practitioners (GPs) of
the respondents were asked to confirm the reported fractures. Furthermore, when a participant died, the GP was
asked whether a fracture had occurred since the participant’s last contact with the researchers. In case of doubt,
radiographs were obtained from the hospital and checked by
a clinician of our institute. Eighty-two percent of all reported fractures were verified by a GP or by radiographs. A
fracture was classified as definite (i.e., the self-reported
fracture was confirmed by the GP or radiographs) or as
possible (i.e., the self-reported fracture was not confirmed).
Fractures of the head and neck, foot and toes, hand and
fingers, and fractures caused by a motor vehicle accident
were excluded from the analysis. Both definite and possible
fractures were used in the analysis. Duration of follow-up
was calculated as the time from the examination (1995–
1996) to the date of the first fracture, the date of death, or
the date of the last follow-up.
Assessment of vision: There are several aspects of vision
that can be clinically assessed, such as visual acuity, visual
field, and contrast sensitivity. Visual acuity refers to the
ability to see details, like small letters. Contrast sensitivity
refers to the ability to differentiate between two objects of
different light intensity. For example, the ability to see a
large black garbage bin against the background of a gray
wall—the closer the light intensities of two objects are, the
higher the contrast sensitivity needed to be able to differentiate between the objects. Contrast sensitivity of a person
can be different depending on the spacing of the objects
(spatial frequency). For example, the large garbage bin
might be detectable for someone, whereas two smaller bins
(together the size of the large bin) standing a little apart
from each other against the same background might not be
detectable for that person. Information on the contrast sensitivity of a range of spatial frequencies can be integrated in
a score (the integrated contrast sensitivity [ICS]) that re-

In this study, contrast sensitivity was measured using the
VCTS_6000-1 chart for near vision (Vistech Consultants,
Dayton, OH, USA) at five different spatial frequencies (1.5,
3, 6, 12, and 18 cycles/° of visual angle; see Fig. 1A). The
chart was placed 30 –32 cm from the participant’s face. The
respondent was asked to state whether the lines in the
gratings were directed to the left, to the right, or straight
upward. We have assessed the ICS for each eye separately
by calculating the surface under the contrast sensitivity
curve when plotted on a linear scale (Fig. 1B). The ICS of
the best eye was used as the main determinant. This score
was dichotomized at the fifth percentile, that is, comparing
the 5% of persons with the lowest ICS scores with the rest
of the population. This cut-off was chosen because the
prevalence of visual impairment in The Netherlands (WHO
criteria) has been shown to be around 5% for the age group

in this study.(18) In previous studies, impaired contrast sensitivity of the low spatial frequencies has been implicated as
a risk factor for poor mobility and fractures.(6,19) Therefore,
a dichotomized score of the mean contrast sensitivity values
based on the two lowest spatial frequencies (1.5 and 3
cycles/degree) was also examined (5% worst scores versus
the rest).
Vision was also assessed by questioning the participants
on whether they could read the small print in a newspaper or
could recognize someone’s face at a distance of 4 m (wearing glasses or contact lenses if needed). Both questions were
scored on a four-point scale. These scores were dichotomized (no difficulty or little difficulty versus much difficulty
or cannot see). Finally, the participants were asked three
questions on glare: whether one could see better in cloudy
weather than in sunny weather, whether they were blinded
by the light on a clear day, and whether they were blinded
by the light when going from inside to outside during

daytime. The scores on these questions (range, 1– 4) were
summed to a total score that ranged from 3 (no problems) to
12 (many problems).
Effect modifiers: Age and gender were considered potential effect modifiers in the relationship between vision and
falling and vision and fractures.(20,21) Baseline information
on age and gender were derived from the municipal registries.
Potential confounders: In addition to age and gender,
diabetes mellitus, osteoarthritis, cognitive impairment, and
depression symptoms were considered as potential confounders in the relationship between poor vision and falls or
fractures. They have all been shown to be associated with
falls, fractures, osteoporosis,(16,22–25) and visual impairment.(26,27)
Level of education was considered as an additional potential
confounder in the relationship between impaired vision and
falls.(11) Current smoking (yes or no), use of corticosteroids,
weight, and previous fractures (after the age of 50) were
considered as additional potential confounders.(6,28 –30)
Diabetes and osteoarthritis were assessed by self-report
with a validated questionnaire.(31) Cognitive impairment
was assessed using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).(32) Depression symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies–Depression Scale
(CES-D).(33) Level of education was assessed by asking the
respondent for the highest educational level completed,
ranging from primary to university education. The responses were converted into years of education (range, 5–18
years). Use of corticosteroids was assessed by asking the
respondents if they had ever used corticosteroids for a
period of ⱖ3 months. Weight was assessed using a standardized scale. Finally, participants were asked about previous fractures (after the age of 50) in the medical interview.
Potential mediators: Functional limitations, physical activity, and physical performance were considered to be
possible mediators in the relationship between impaired
vision and falls or fractures. Functional limitations were
assessed with a questionnaire, previously validated in The
Netherlands,(34) about the degree of difficulty with three
activities of daily living: stair climbing, cutting one’s own
toenails, and using personal or public transportation. Scores
on the three questions (range, 0 – 4) were summed to a total
score ranging from 0 to12. Physical activity was assessed
with the validated LASA Physical Activity Questionnaire
(LAPAQ).(35) Six yes or no questions about walking outside, bicycling, doing sport activities, and performing light
and heavy household activities were summed to a physical
activity score (range, 0 – 6). Level of physical performance
was assessed with three physical performance tests(36) that
included time needed to walk 3 m back and forth along a
line, time needed to stand up and sit down five times with
arms folded, and time needed to perform a tandem stand.
For the first two tests, a score of 1– 4 points was assigned,
corresponding to the quartile of time needed. For the tandem
stand, 1 point was given to those who stood less than 10 s
and 2 points were given to those who stood at least 10 s.
Participants who were not able to perform a test obtained a
score of 0 points. The scores of the three tests were summed
to a physical performance score (range, 0 –10).

Statistical analyses: Independent-sample t-tests and ␹2
tests were used to test differences in means and prevalences
of the vision variables, potential mediators, and potential
confounders by recurrent falls and fractures. Variables that
were not distributed normally were analyzed with the
Mann-Whitney test. The association between vision variables and recurrent falling and fractures were examined
using Cox proportional hazards analyses. The mediating
effects of physical activity, physical performance, and functional limitations were examined by testing the four conditions proposed by Baron and Kenny.(37) These conditions
state that (1) the predictor is significantly associated with
the hypothesized mediator, (2) the predictor is significantly
associated with the dependent variable, (3) the impact of the
predictor on the dependent variable is less after controlling
for the mediator, and (4) the mediator is significantly associated with the dependent variable. The first condition was
tested with multiple linear regression analysis, whereas the
second, third, and fourth conditions were tested using Cox
proportional hazards analysis.
Potential confounders and effect modifiers were examined in all Cox proportional hazard, logistic regression, and
linear regression analyses. First, to test whether there was
effect modification, interaction terms were included in the
model. Subsequently, potential confounders were added to
the model, one at a time, beginning with the strongest
confounder. Confounders that led to a change in the ␤ of
⬎10% were kept in the model. Continuous independent
variables were divided into quintiles or quartiles and added
to the model as dummy variables to examine possible nonlinear effects. When nonlinear effects were observed, the
variables were kept in the model as dummy variables. A p ⬍
0.05 was considered to reflect a statistically significant
finding. Data were analyzed using the software package
SPSS 9.0 for Windows (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).

RESULTS
Fall and fracture follow-up
A total of 72 of the 1509 eligible participants did not
participate in the fall follow-up. Additionally, 19 people
were excluded from the analysis because they did not participate during two consecutive 3-month periods of time
during the 3-year fall follow-up. These 91 people (6%) were
considered nonresponders. Data on fractures were not available for 32 people (nonresponse). Sixteen additional people
were excluded from the analysis because they had fractures
of the head, neck, foot, toes, hand, and fingers or fractures
caused by an accident. Compared with the responders, nonresponders of the fall and fracture follow-up more often had
impaired ICS and low-frequency contrast sensitivity scores
and more frequently reported difficulty in recognizing faces
and reading a newspaper. Furthermore, they were older, had
more functional limitations, had lower scores on the physical performance tests, weighed less, were less physically
active, and reported diabetes, depression symptoms, and
cognitive impairments more frequently.

Baseline characteristics
Table 1 shows differences in baseline characteristics for
recurrent fallers and nonrecurrent fallers. Recurrent fallers
had significantly lower ICS and low-frequency contrast
sensitivity scores and more frequently reported difficulty
with recognizing faces and glare. Furthermore, they were
older, had more functional limitations, had lower physical
performance, weighed less, reported diabetes and previous
fractures more frequently, scored higher on the CES-D, and
scored lower on the MMSE.
Recognizing faces was the only vision variable for which
statistically significant differences were found between respondents with fractures and respondents without fractures
(Table 1). Furthermore, respondents with fractures were
older, had more functional limitations, had lower physical
performance, were less physically active, weighed less,
reported osteoarthritis and previous fractures more frequently, used corticosteroids more often, and scored lower
on the MMSE.

Associations between vision variables and recurrent
falling and fractures
Table 2 shows the association between the vision variables and risk of recurrent falling. Unadjusted Cox re-

gression analyses showed significant hazard ratios (HRs)
for all vision variables. The strongest association was
found between low-frequency contrast sensitivity and
recurrent falling (HR ⫽ 2.09; CI ⫽ 1.41–3.10). After
adjustment for all potential confounders, only ICS and
low-frequency contrast sensitivity remained significantly
associated with recurrent falls. Table 3 shows that there
is a strong association between recognizing faces and
fractures in unadjusted analyses as well as in adjusted
analyses (HR ⫽ 3.10; CI ⫽ 1.65–5.82). For the other
vision variables, no statistically significant elevated HRs
could be observed.

DISCUSSION
In this study, we showed that people with poor visual
contrast sensitivity had a 1.5-fold increase in the relative
risk of recurrent falling. Although Harwood(38) suggests that
the influence of potential confounders in studies on the
relationship between vision and falling is limited, our findings suggest that it is important to correct for confounding
factors. For example, the risk estimate of the ICS decreased
from 2 to 1.5 after adjustment for age and depression
symptoms.
To our knowledge, there are only three studies that examined contrast sensitivity as a risk factor for falls in a
prospective study that included adjustment for potential
confounders.(2,39,40) Only one found a significant association between contrast sensitivity and recurrent falls (no risk

Our findings indicate very little difference between the
relationships of ICS and low-frequency contrast sensitivity
with recurrent falling, which is in agreement with a study by
Ivers et al.(41)
In this study, no associations between any of the subjective measures of vision and recurrent falling were found. In
previous work on the same cohort, Tromp et al.(7) identified
a relationship between recognizing faces and falls and recurrent falls. However, in that study, the follow-up was 1
year compared with the 3-year follow-up of the analyses
presented in this study. Moreover, recognizing faces was
included as a predictor in a risk profile (i.e., only partly
adjusted for other potential risk factors), whereas in this
study, it was treated as an independent risk factor.
Several prospective studies estimated the risk of impaired
contrast sensitivity on incident fractures.(6,42– 44) In a study
with a mean follow-up of 4.7 years, Ivers et al. examined the
relationship between contrast sensitivity and fractures with
separate analyses for fractures of the hip,(42) wrist, shoulder,
and ankle.(43) In line with our findings, they did not find a
significant association between contrast sensitivity and any
type of fracture in adjusted analyses. In a study by Dargent Molina et al.,(44) with a mean follow-up of 1.9 years, no
association was found between contrast sensitivity and hip
fractures as well. Cummings et al.(6) did find an increased
risk of hip fractures for people with impaired low-frequency
contrast sensitivity. However, the latter two studies were
analyzed as risk models, and depression, cognition, and
diabetes were not assessed, leading to a possible overestimation of the risk estimates for the vision variables. Fur-

thermore, the cohort of study participants consisted of
women only.
We could not locate prospective cohort studies in which
self-reported visual impairment was examined as a risk
factor for fractures. However, in agreement with our findings, recognizing a friend across the room was significantly
associated with hip fractures in a case-control study.(45)
Although impaired vision was shown to be an independent risk factor for recurrent falling as well as for fractures,
there is a discrepancy in the type of visual risk factors for
falls and fractures. The ICS was a risk factor for recurrent
falling, whereas recognizing faces was a risk factor for
fractures. Although it is thought that recognizing faces is an
activity that is dependent on the level of contrast sensitivity
rather than on visual acuity,(19) in this study, the association
between recognizing faces and the ICS are low (␬ values:
0.30 for the responders in the fall analyses and 0.32 for
responders in the fracture analyses). Recognizing faces
might encompass more than visual functioning alone. It is a
daily life activity in which the environment and possibly
memory functions are also important. Furthermore, recognizing faces was assessed with a question and is therefore
influenced by the subjective impressions that respondents
have about their eyesight and overall health. Subjectively
reported health has been shown to be a risk factor for hip
fractures in women.(6) Another difference between recognizing faces and contrast sensitivity lies in the distance of
measurement. The question on recognizing faces was asked
for a distance of 4 m, whereas the contrast sensitivity test was
administered at 30–32 cm. However, it is unclear how these
differences might explain the discrepancy in our findings.

Functional limitations and physical performance were
shown to be mediators in the relationship between vision
and recurrent falling. Functional limitations were also
shown to be a mediator in the relationship between vision
and fractures. This indicates that impaired vision leads to
impaired physical performance and functional limitations,
in turn leading to recurrent falling and fractures. Our finding
that physical performance plays a mediating role in the
relationship between contrast sensitivity and falling is supported by reports in the literature. The tests we used to
assess physical performance (walking 3 m back and forth on
a line, standing up and sitting down five times with arms
folded, and performing a tandem stand) are markers for
postural stability and gait. Several studies have shown that
contrast sensitivity and other aspects of vision are associated with postural stability, particularly when standing on a
compliant surface,(46,47)and with aspects of gait.(48) Furthermore, gait and balance abnormalities have been shown to be
predictors of falls.(2,3,44,48)
The role of functional limitations as a mediator in the
relationship between impaired vision and falling and fractures is also supported by the literature. Functional limitations were assessed with questions on the degree of difficulty with climbing stairs, cutting one’s own toenails, and
using public transportation. It is well known that visually
impaired people have to use adaptive strategies to maintain
their ability to perform activities such as climbing stairs and
using public transportation.(49,50) However, even when they
have mastered these adaptive strategies, it will still take
them more effort and more time than normally sighted
people, and therefore, it is not surprising that they feel
limited in their ability to perform these activities. Furthermore, self-reported functional limitations have also been
shown to be predictors of falls and fractures.(7,45)
This study has several limitations. First, nonresponse was
associated with poor vision and most potential confounders;
frail respondents were less likely to participate in the medical interview and the fall and fracture follow-up. This has
probably led to an underestimation of the association between the vision variables and falling or fractures. Second,
in this study, contrast sensitivity was assessed using the
VCTS_6000-1 chart for near vision. The distant version of
this chart has been shown to be valid, but reproducibility
was only moderate.(51,52) Reproducibility and validity of the
near vision chart have not been assessed but are likely to be
similar to those of the distant version given the similarity
between charts and instruction. Imperfect reliability will
lead to nondifferential misclassification. This also may have
led to an underestimation of the association between contrast sensitivity and falling or fractures. The other aspects of
vision were assessed with one (recognizing faces and reading the newspaper) or more questions (glare) with unknown
validity and reliability. It is unclear if and how this might
have influenced the associations that we found. Third, measurement of distant contrast sensitivity might have given us
more insight into the discrepancies found between the ICS
and recognizing faces in their relationship with falling and
fractures. Future studies should address this problem. Finally, although the study consisted of a large sample size,
the number of fractures was limited. Therefore, inferences

about associations should be interpreted with caution. In
addition, because of the limited number of fractures, we
could not differentiate between types of fractures.
In summary, this study shows that there is a significant
and independent relation between impaired vision and both
recurrent falling and recurrent fractures in a prospective
study design. The multifactorial design of this study enabled
correction for all known important confounders. The results
showed that, in particular, ICS was associated with recurrent falls, whereas recognizing faces was associated with
risk of fracture. Moreover, functional limitations and physical performance seem to play a mediating role in these
relations. Future research should focus on the effectiveness
of prevention of visual impairment and disability through
surgical, optometric, and rehabilitative interventions. Self reported visual impairments should not be neglected in
these studies.
