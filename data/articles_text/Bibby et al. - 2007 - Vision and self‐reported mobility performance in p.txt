patients with low vision

Background: As vision plays a significant role in mobility performance, it is usual to refer
low vision patients, particularly those who complain of mobility difficulties, for orientation and mobility (O&M) training to help them maintain safe independent travel. Our
study aimed to determine whether there was a relationship between measures of vision
and self-reported mobility, and the applicability of a patient-based mobility assessment
in patients with heterogeneous causes of visual loss.
Method: We assessed the high and low contrast visual acuity, visual field and scanning
ability of 30 patients with low vision. A validated mobility questionnaire was used to assess
the participants’ perceived visual ability for independent mobility.
Results: Vision was significantly correlated with self-reported mobility performance,
however, visual field was a significant predictor accounting for 56.5 per cent of the
variance. The instrument was well constructed with valid content and high reliability
scores.
Conclusions: Self-reported mobility performance together with measures of vision could
be used as a guide to refer patients for O&M training. The patient-based assessment
instrument was valid to measure perceived visual ability for independent mobility in
patients with heterogeneous causes of visual loss.

Key words: low vision, orientation and mobility training, scanning ability, self-reported mobility, visual acuity, visual field

Vision plays an important role in navigation of the environment. Research has
shown that visual acuity, visual field and
contrast sensitivity correlate with mobility
performance, however, visual field and
contrast sensitivity are stronger predictors
than visual acuity.1–4
In a group of participants with heterogeneous causes of low vision, Marron and
Bailey1 showed that visual field and contrast sensitivity together accounted for 53
per cent of the variance in mobility performance. In a more recent study, Kuyk,

Elliott and Fuhr3 investigated mobility performance (time to complete a course and
the number of mobility incidents) of a
large sample of visually impaired adults
(n = 156) using measures of visual sensory
and perceptual functions. They found that
visual field, scanning reaction time and
contrast sensitivity together accounted for
45 per cent and 48 per cent of the variance
in total time and total errors, respectively.
Using patients with age-related macular
degeneration (AMD), Brown and associates5 demonstrated that visual acuity

Thus, if there are deficits in these visual
functions, one’s navigational skills would
be adversely affected and consequently,
one may not be able to travel
independently.
Fortunately, low vision patients can be
referred by their eye care practitioners
and/or rehabilitation officers for orientation and mobility (O&M) training to

dently.6 In O&M training, low vision
patients learn new mobility skills to ambulate and negotiate the environment safely
and independently.7 By learning new skills
to compensate for their visual loss, low
vision patients have increased travel
options, thus maintaining their quality of
life. Geruschat and Del’Aune8 showed
that mobility skills of low vision patients
improved significantly following O&M
training. Further, Kuyk and associates9
showed that self-reported mobility function of low vision patients improved significantly following a comprehensive
blind rehabilitation program that included O&M training.
There are no established guidelines in
New Zealand or Australia that eye care
practitioners and rehabilitation officers
could use to refer patients for O&M training. In Australia, low vision patients are
usually referred for general vision rehabilitation services, which may include O&M
training based on their visual field and
visual acuity.10,11 Self-reported mobility difficulty is often used as a general guide by
eye care practitioners and rehabilitation
officers to determine if O&M training is
warranted. This was confirmed by Beggs12
who found that mobility officers at a
residential rehabilitation centre referred
their clients for O&M training based on
how the clients felt about their mobility
skills. It is not surprising that low vision
people’s perception of their own mobility
skills will determine whether they are confident to travel independently. It may not
be unexpected that those patients with
poor vision are likely to report difficulties
with travelling independently.
Our study sought to determine if there
is a relationship between vision and
self-reported mobility performance. We
hypothesised that vision is significantly
related to self-reported mobility performance.
We used a validated patient-based
assessment instrument, which was used
in patients with retinitis pigmentosa13 or
glaucoma14 to assess self-reported mobility
performance. Our study also aimed to
determine the applicability of the questionnaire in patients with heterogeneous
causes of low vision.

METHODS

Participants
Thirty-two low vision participants were
recruited from the Low Vision Clinic, the
University of Auckland. Two participants
were excluded from the study because
they were no longer visually impaired due
to recent cataract surgery. The participants’ ages ranged from 26 to 91 years
and they had various ocular conditions
(Table 1). The mean age (standard deviation) of the participants was 65.1 (± 21.7)
years.

Measurements of vision
The participants’ vision was assessed binocularly with their habitual optical corrections using the following visual function
tests.
HIGH CONTRAST VISUAL ACUITY (HCVA)

Binocular HCVA was measured using the
high contrast Bailey-Lovie distant chart,
scored letter-by-letter and expressed as
the logarithm of the minimum angle of
resolution.15

LOW CONTRAST VISUAL ACUITY (LCVA)

LCVA was measured using the low contrast
Bailey-Lovie distant chart and scored
using the same letter-by-letter method as
the high contrast Bailey-Lovie chart.15
There were 10 participants (33 per
cent) who were unable to perceive any
letters from the low contrast chart
irrespective of the test distance. This
indicates that using the low contrast
Bailey-Lovie chart for contrast sensitivity
assessment may not be appropriate for
low vision patients who have severe visual
loss.
VISUAL FIELD (BVF)

The kinetic program of the Allergan
Humphrey Field Analyser (600 series) was
used to measure the participants’ visual
field with the following stimulus parameters used by Soong, Lovie-Kitchin and
Brown:16
–
1. size: IV 4 E (Goldmann notation)
2. diameter: 4.6 mm
3. luminance: 13.2 cd/m2
4. background: 10 cd/m2
5. speed: 4 deg/sec.
A C++ Builder computer program was
used to convert polar co-ordinates of the
isopters into points on the surface of a
sphere to compute the participants’ visual
field in percentage of a sphere.17 The
remaining binocular visual field as a percentage of a sphere (BVF) was calculated
by taking the difference between the area
of the total visual field and that of any
scotoma within the field area.
SCANNING ABILITY (SA)

The participants’ SA were assessed by
measuring their scanning reaction time
(SRT),18 using the Director 8 Macromedia
computer program based on the time
(seconds) taken to locate, in order, a
sequence of four numbers (1, 2, 3, 4) on
each of four black and white photographs
with randomly scattered distractor stimuli
(Figure 1).
In each photograph, the numbers were
located at different positions so that the
participants were required to scan the
computer monitor to locate them. The
presentations of the four photographs
were randomised among participants to

Each photograph was displayed on a

Each participant was required to indicate the location of the numbers on each

between trials, when the scanning ability

trials for each photograph, then the mean
of the four photographs to determine the

The participants’ scanning ability, SA

We assigned a nominal value of five

Assessment of self-reported
mobility
We used the patient-based assessment
instrument, the Independent Mobility
questionnaire to assess the participants’
perceived visual ability for independent
mobility.13,14 The questionnaire was administered verbally by an experimenter.

The instrument consisted of 35 items of

of questions requiring ‘Yes’ or ‘No’
answers about the participants’ mobility related behaviour, such as the history of
falls and whether they had had mobility

required to rate on a scale of 1 to 5 the
level of difficulty they encountered in
each of the 35 mobility situations, when
unaccompanied by a person or without
the use of a mobility device. They were
informed that 1 meant ‘no difficulty’ and
5 meant ‘extreme difficulty’ while 2, 3 and
4 were not explained. The participants
were also told to score a mobility situation
‘NA’ if they performed an activity with
assistance and to score ‘X’ if the difficulty
was due to causes other than visual loss.

Data analysis
The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) was used for data analysis.
Using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, we
found that the measures of vision were not
significantly different from a normal distribution (p > 0.05).

interval measures of perceived visual ability for independent mobility (person measure) and visual ability required for each
mobility situation (item measure) from
the ordinal ratings of difficulty of the
35 mobility situations.13,19 The analysis
transformed the raw ordinal data into
an equal–interval scale using logarithmic
transformations of the raw data and probabilistic equations.20 The measurement
unit of the interval scale was the logit,

If a person’s perceived visual ability for
independent mobility was more than the
visual ability required for a mobility situation, we expected that he/she had a
greater likelihood of reporting less difficulty with the mobility situation. On the
other hand, if a person had less perceived
visual ability, we expected that they would
report greater difficulty in that mobility
situation.
Using a Winsteps Rasch-model computer program,21 an unconditional maximum likelihood estimation routine was
performed to determine the parameters
of the Wright and Masters22 version of the
Rasch model for rating scale data. The
analysis provided reliability indices to indicate the spread of person ability and item
difficulty along the measurement continuum.20 The person reliability index indicated the replicability of person ordering
using a different instrument measuring
the same construct, perceived visual ability
for independent mobility. The item reliability index indicated the replicability of
item placements along the item difficulty
continuum if the instrument was applied
to a different sample of participants with
comparable ability.
In addition, the Rasch measurement
model produced chi-square fitting statistics to determine how well the data set met
the requirements of the model. The infit
value provided information about an item
performance for participants whose ability

was close to the item’s difficulty. The infit
statistic was calculated by weighting each
squared standardised residual value by its
variance for each item responded by every
participant and then summed. The total
value was then divided by the sum of the
variances. The outfit value, which was not
weighted, provided information on the
influence of outlying scores. The outfit statistic was obtained based on the sum of
squared standardised response residuals,
that is, for every participant, each standardised residual cell was squared for every
item responded to by the participant and
summed. The average was then derived by
dividing the number of items responded
to by the participant. Both outfit and infit
statistics were reported in mean squares
and were transformed into the standardised forms.
As the person measure estimate was
not significantly different from a normal
distribution (p > 0.05), we used Pearson
product moment correlation to determine
the relationships between the visual functions and person measure estimate.

Rasch analysis
The Rasch analysis produced a map of the
person and item measures, which displayed the level of perceived visual ability
for independent mobility (person measure) (left side) and visual ability required
for each mobility situation (item measure)
(right side) (Figure 3). The items of
mobility situations were indicated by the
item numbers from the questionnaire,
while each subject’s perceived visual ability for independent mobility was represented by an ‘x’. The logit scale, which had
equal values along the vertical axis, was
displayed in the centre of the map.
The item-person map showed the relationship between the item measure estimate and person measure estimate. The
participants’ perceived visual ability loca-

tions were plotted, so that any subject who
was located at the same position as a
mobility situation on the logit scale had a
50 per cent probability of succeeding in
performing that mobility situation. If a
mobility situation were located further up
the scale, the subject would have a higher
probability of succeeding in performing
the mobility situation.
The participants with least perceived
visual ability for independent mobility
were at the top of the scale, while those
with most perceived visual ability were at
the bottom of the scale. The mobility situations that were located further down the
scale became more difficult to perform.
For example, moving about at work
(item 4) was more difficult to perform
than moving about at home (item 3)
(Figure 3).

work’ and ‘walking in familiar areas’ while
‘walking in high glare areas’, ‘walking at

person measure estimates. The mean of
the person measure estimate of -0.85 was
moderately close to zero, indicating that
the sample’s perceived visual ability was
closely matched to the difficulty of the

measure, the person measure estimates

that the same ordering of person measure

In our sample, the means of the

the person measure and item measure
estimates were very close to the expected

the expected mean zero in both measures

close to the expected 1.

which showed that visual acuity, visual
field, contrast sensitivity and scanning
ability were important in mobility performance of low vision patients.2,4,23–25 This
indicates that patients with reduced visual
acuity, visual field, contrast sensitivity and/

Our findings agree with West and colleagues,26 who found significant associations between self-reported mobility
limitations and high and low contrast
visual acuity, contrast sensitivity and visual
field in a large population-based study.
Szlyk and co-workers27 also showed that
self-reported mobility was significantly correlated with visual field and high contrast
visual acuity in a group of patients with
retinitis pigmentosa (RP).
Similarly, in a group of participants with
RP, Turano and associates13 showed that
perceived visual ability for independent
mobility was covariant with contrast sensitivity and visual field but not with high
contrast visual acuity. In another study,
Turano, Massof and Quigley14 found that
visual acuity was significantly correlated
with perceived visual ability in a group of
participants with glaucoma.
The difference in findings may be
attributed to the different causes of low
vision in the studies and the stage or severity of the disease. This suggests that the
importance of different visual functions in
mobility performance may be dependent
on the type and progression of the ocular
disease.
We found that visual field was the significant predictor in perceived visual ability
for independent mobility in a multivariate
regression model, that is, visual acuity,
contrast sensitivity and scanning ability
did not explain any more of the variance
than visual field. Similarly, West and coworkers26 showed that visual field was
a significant predictor of self-reported

The correlation between contrast sensitivity and perceived visual ability for independent mobility might have been limited
by the fact that one-third of the participants were unable to perform the test.
Thus, in a future study, contrast sensitivity
could be assessed using the Pelli-Robson
chart to obtain measurements in participants with severe visual loss. Further, the
measurement of scanning ability might
have been confounded by a motor component (eye-hand co-ordination), as the
participants were required to point at a
number using a pointer as soon as they
located each number on the computer
monitor screen.
We had a small sample of participants
with large variability in measures of vision.
For future research, a larger sample size
could be used to confirm the findings of
this study. Further, it would be interesting
to determine the correlation between self reported mobility and objective measures
of mobility performance.
It is also essential to determine the
critical level of perceived visual ability for
independent mobility and measures of
vision, at which mobility performance
becomes sufficiently impaired to warrant
O&M training. This would provide accurate guidelines to eye care practitioners
and rehabilitation officers to assess better
the need for O&M training for people
with low vision. This would enable rehabilitation resources to be used effectively and
efficiently in view of the limited financial
funding available in Australia and New
Zealand for rehabilitation of people with
low vision.
