# Candidate items and domains (non-GAL/GQL instruments) that target field loss, contrast, glare/low luminance, mobility or driving

Purpose: working notes for the Glaucoma Today narrative review on brief self-report screening questions that identify adults (especially glaucoma patients with field and contrast loss and preserved acuity) who need low vision rehabilitation (LVR).

Rules used: every number is from the article text in `data/articles_text/` and is followed by [page N], where N is the `[page N]` marker in the text file (not the journal page). NR = the paper does not say. Nothing estimated, nothing borrowed from another paper. The extraction CSVs (`analysis/extraction/effects.csv`, `instruments.csv`) were used only as a cross-check, and no value here depends on them. All of it is advisory until the reviewer checks it against the source.

## 0. Plain statement on what these items can and cannot support

- None of the papers below tests an individual item, or a short set of items, as a stand-alone screening item against an LVR-need reference standard. No paper reports sensitivity, specificity, AUC or a cut-point for any item listed here. What exists is correlation or regression of an instrument-level or domain-level score (usually a Rasch person measure) against clinical vision measures.
- Bibby 2007 says this itself: the "critical level" of perceived mobility ability and of vision measures at which mobility becomes impaired enough to warrant O&M training still has to be determined [page 8].
- Agathos 2025 used GAL-9 and referral to O&M/PT, but referral was a clinician decision that was an outcome of the study, not a reference standard [page 17]. The authors say they "do not attempt to validate the GAL-9 in AMD or suggest that it be used for screening in patients with maculopathies" [page 18].
- Item-level evidence is almost absent. Most papers report Rasch item difficulty (how hard the item is), which is not a validity or accuracy statistic. Where an item name is attached to a field or contrast finding, it is only by the paper's own grouping, flagged below.
- GQL-15: no included text. "GQL-15" occurs in the folder only inside reference lists (Pérez-Mañá 2022 line 1384; Agathos 2025 line 1273, "Glaucoma Quality of Life-15 Questionnaire with Rasch Analysis"), and a bibliographic mention in Zhao 2024 (line 994, a list of instruments). There is no GQL-15 paper in the folder. GAL-9 enters only through Agathos 2025 (and a reference title in Jeon 2020, line 769). I did not look beyond the folder.

## 1. Bibby 2007 (Screening ID 120): Independent Mobility questionnaire (Turano), Rasch person measure

Instrument: 35 mobility situations rated 1 (no difficulty) to 5 (extreme difficulty), plus a part of yes/no mobility-behaviour questions that includes falls history and mobility training [page 4]. The paper cites earlier validation in retinitis pigmentosa and in glaucoma (Turano refs) [pages 2, 4]; those papers are not in the included text. The questionnaire was read out by an experimenter [page 3].

Sample: 30 adults with low vision from a university low vision clinic (32 recruited, 2 excluded after cataract surgery), age 26 to 91, mean 65.1 (SD 21.7) [page 2]. Mixed causes: AMD most common; by my count of Table 1, four of 30 have a glaucoma entry (IDs 4, 9, 11, 26, including congenital glaucoma and glaucoma with other disease) [page 2]. Acuity range in Table 1 is 6/5 to 6/6000 [page 2]; mean acuity NR. Not a glaucoma sample; not a preserved-acuity sample.

Vision measures: binocular high-contrast VA (Bailey-Lovie logMAR), low-contrast VA (Bailey-Lovie low-contrast chart), binocular visual field (BVF; kinetic Humphrey, remaining binocular field as % of a sphere), scanning ability [pages 2 to 3]. Ten participants (33%) could not read any letter on the low-contrast chart [page 3]; unmeasurable field assigned 0 [page 3]; unmeasurable acuity or scanning assigned a nominal value of five SD about the mean [page 3].

Headline correlations (Pearson, Rasch person measure vs vision measure; Table 2) [page 4]:

| Vision measure | r | p | n the paper gives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binocular visual field (% of a sphere) | -0.762 | 0.000 (as printed) | NR separately; study n = 30 |
| Low-contrast VA (logMAR) | 0.576 | 0.001 | NR. Table 2 says only "sample size was reduced in the analysis" [page 4]; 10 of the 30 could not read the chart [page 3] |
| High-contrast VA (logMAR) | 0.410 | 0.024 | NR separately; study n = 30 |
| Scanning ability (per second) | -0.662 | 0.000 | NR separately; study n = 30 |

- Sign: a higher person measure = less perceived ability (least-able participants sit at the top of the scale) [page 5]. So worse acuity (higher logMAR) goes with a higher measure (positive r), and a larger remaining field goes with a lower measure (negative r), consistent with "reduced HCVA, LCVA, BVF and/or SA are likely to report difficulty" [pages 4 to 5].
- The paper gives no CI. Stepwise regression: BVF was the only significant predictor, adjusted R2 = 0.565 (56.5% of variance), Person measure = 0.231 - 0.142 x BVF, p < 0.05 [page 5]. Acuity, contrast (low-contrast VA) and scanning "did not explain any more of the variance than visual field" [page 6].
- Contrast caveat: contrast was measured only as low-contrast letter acuity, and a third could not do it, so the contrast result is limited by the authors' own account [page 8]. The authors suggest Pelli-Robson in future [page 8].
- Item-level content (Rasch item measures, Table 3) [page 7]. The text names glare and night as the hardest. Walking in high glare areas (item 18) was the most difficult item, measure -1.58 logits (error 0.21) [pages 5, 7]. Also hard: walking at night (-1.01), walking in unfamiliar areas (-0.92), moving about in crowded situations (-0.84), detecting descending stairs (-0.81), avoiding tripping over uneven travel surface (-0.80), seeing cars at intersections (-0.67), walking in dimly-lit indoor areas (-0.65) [page 7] (item-measure values read from Table 3, whose columns are scrambled in the text file; verify alignment against the PDF). Lighting-transition items are in the list: "Adjusting to lighting changes at night: indoors to street lights" and "...during the day: indoor to outdoors", among others [page 7]. Easiest: moving in the home (3.23) [page 7].
- Peripheral-vision item content: "Avoid bumping into walls / shoulder-height / waist-height / head-height / knee-height / low lying objects", "Aware of person's presence", "Avoid bumping into people" [page 7]. These are about obstacle avoidance; the paper does not report any item-level link between these items and field loss.
- Falls: the part-two yes/no questions include falls history [page 4]; no falls statistic is reported.
- Not validated as a screening item. Whole 35-item instrument only; no accuracy statistic.

## 2. Sumi 2003 (ID 110): Japanese revised visual disability questionnaire in glaucoma

Sample: 147 Japanese glaucoma patients (69 POAG, 56 NTG, 22 primary angle-closure); mean age 61.5 (SD 11.9); better-eye BCVA mean 0.3 (SD 0.5) logMAR, worse eye 0.9 (SD 0.8); HFA 30-2 mean deviation (MD) -11.2 (SD 5.9) dB better eye, -17.4 (SD 6.5) dB worse eye [page 2]. This is the only glaucoma-specific included study among those read, and it is monocular HFA 30-2 (central 30 degrees), not binocular; the "highest sensitivity" per test point was used to approximate binocular sensitivity and the authors say binocular field "was not examined" [pages 2, 6].

Instrument: 30 questions in 7 domains (letters, sentences, walking, going out, dining, dressing, miscellaneous); each answered with three options scored 2/1/0; domain Disability Index (DI) = mean score of its questions, plus a total DI [page 2]. Internal consistency and test-retest reported in the appendix (Cronbach's alpha 0.85 to 0.88; kappa 0.9 to 1.0) [page 7].

Domain wording relevant to field, glare/low-light, mobility (Table 1) [page 3]:
- Walking domain: "Do you have difficulty walking because of your visual problems?" (9); "Can you take a walk by yourself?" (10); "Do you misjudge traffic signals?" (11); "Do you bump into people or objects while walking?" (12); "Do you stumble on the stairs?" (13); "Do you fail to notice changes in the ground?" (14); "Do you fail to recognize your friends until they talk to you?" (15); "Do you fail to see people or cars approaching you from the side?" (16).
- Going out domain: "Do you have difficulty going out because of your visual problems?" (17); "Do you need somebody to accompany you to go to new places?" (18); "Can you get a cab by yourself?" (19); "Do you have difficulty traveling by train?" (20); "Do you feel uneasy going out at night because of your visual problems?" (21).
- By content only (my reading, not the paper's analysis): item 16 is the peripheral-field item and item 21 the night item. The paper analyses domain DIs, not single items, so it provides no item-level correlation with any field region.
- Driving: not included. No patient complained of driving inconvenience, and in Japan a licence is not issued below best-corrected acuity 0.7 [page 6].

Association with visual field (Pearson, n = 147; negative = more field loss, more disability) [page 4]:
- Every field measure correlated significantly with the DIs (r = -0.70 to -0.30) [page 4]. The better-eye and worse-eye MD correlated with the DIs, r = -0.64 to -0.44 [page 4].
- Total DI: better-eye MD r = -0.64 and worse-eye MD r = -0.59 (Table 3) [page 4]. Going out DI: better MD -0.63, worse MD -0.60. Walking DI: better MD -0.61, worse MD -0.58 [page 4]. (Values re-aligned from a scrambled table; verify against the PDF.) No CI; Table 3 flags p < 0.0005 for most entries [page 4].
- Region: the best-correlated field measure for all item and total DIs was the highest-sensitivity average of cluster VFE, the lower hemifield within 5 degrees of fixation, r = -0.70 to -0.55 [page 4]. For total DI, VF10 (central 10 degrees, highest sensitivity) r = -0.67; VF30 -0.63; VF11-20 -0.60; VF21-30 -0.58 (lowest-sensitivity versions weaker, -0.43 to -0.34) [page 4]. So the central 10 degrees and especially the inferior paracentral field were more strongly related to the self-reported disability than the outer 20 to 30 degrees.
- Stepwise regression for total DI: only VFE was a significant field predictor (standardised coefficient -0.57, p < 0.0001; R = 0.73) [page 4]. With acuity added: better-eye VA (0.42, p < 0.0001), worse-eye VA (0.16, p = 0.0067), VFE (-0.24, p = 0.0057), R = 0.87, about 76% of variance [pages 1, 6] (Table 4). The VFE coefficient is significant in every domain DI, from -0.27 (p = 0.0002, dining) to -0.21 (p = 0.0013, miscellaneous); the Letters row prints 0.24 (p = 0.0007) without a minus sign, which conflicts with the text range of -0.27 to -0.21 and is probably a lost sign [page 6]. Walking domain: VFE -0.22 (p = 0.0417); Going out: -0.25 (p = 0.0035) [page 6].
- Acuity: better-eye VA vs total DI r = 0.74, worse-eye r = 0.56 [page 4]. Acuity in the better eye also contributed to every DI, so this is not a "preserved acuity" finding: acuity and field both mattered.
- Contrast sensitivity: not measured. Glare: no item.
- Not validated as a screening item. Whole questionnaire and domain DIs only; no accuracy statistic; no LVR-need reference standard. The authors note the lower central field may be more crucial in a population that reads and writes vertically and that findings may differ in a Western sample [page 7].

## 3. GAL-9: Agathos 2025 (ID 55), Glaucoma Activity Limitation-9 used in maculopathy

Important: this is not a glaucoma sample. 125 patients over 55 (26 men) with binocular maculopathy (116 AMD, 9 Stargardt) and intact peripheral field attending a low vision rehabilitation clinic; glaucoma, RP and other peripheral vision loss were excluded [page 4]. Mean age 80.6 (SD 8.7) [page 9]. Better-eye VA mean 0.75 (SD 0.34) logMAR, worse eye 1.21 (SD 0.38) (from Table 1, whose text layout is scrambled; verify) [page 10]. The authors chose GAL-9 because it was "developed for peripheral vision loss" and no central-field analogue exists [page 5]; the original GAL-9 validation is a cited reference only, not in the included text [page 19].

The nine GAL-9 items, each rated none / a little / some / quite a lot / severe (1 to 5), "How much difficulty does your vision give you, even with glasses, with the following activities?" (Table 2) [pages 6 to 7]: Q1 Walking after dark/dusk; Q2 Seeing at night; Q3 Walking on uneven ground; Q4 Adjusting to dim lights; Q5 Going from light to dark room and vice versa; Q6 Seeing objects come from the side; Q7 Walking on steps/stairs; Q8 Judging distance of foot to step/curb; Q9 Finding dropped objects. Total score = sum, maximum 45 [page 7]. Also asked: G1 "How many times have you fallen in the past 12 months?"; G2 "Do you have problems with balance or gait?"; G3 "Do you have mobility problems because of your vision?"; G4 other causes; G5 O&M training [page 6].

Contrast sensitivity and falls (all within this maculopathy sample) [pages 13 to 17]:
- Contrast sensitivity was measured binocularly with LEA symbols at 30 cm [page 4]. GAL-9 total vs binocular CS: Spearman rho = -0.62, 95% CI -0.72 to -0.49, p < 0.0001; vs better-eye VA rho = 0.39 (0.22 to 0.53); vs worse-eye VA rho = 0.37 (0.21 to 0.52) [page 15]. The number of pairs is NR (125 enrolled; the one-way ANOVA is reported with F(2,97), which suggests about 100 analysed, but the paper does not say) [page 13].
- Mean GAL-9 total 21.0 (SD 7.9) overall [page 13]. By CS severity: mild 15.8 (SD 5.5), moderate 20.5 (SD 6.9), severe 29.1 (SD 5.7), F(2,97) = 37.47, p < 0.0001; by VA severity: 18.0 (SD 8.0), 20.8 (SD 7.5), 25.4 (SD 6.5), F(2,97) = 8.05, p < 0.001 [page 13].
- Regression of GAL-9 total (AIC-preferred model, R2 = 0.45, p < 0.0001) [pages 8, 9, 15]: contrast sensitivity beta = -7.58 (95% CI -10.66 to -4.503, p < 0.0001); relative binocular scotoma size beta = 0.232 (0.0513 to 0.412, p = 0.0123); falls in past 12 months beta = 1.077 (0.405 to 1.749, p = 0.0019); female sex beta = 5.38 (2.685 to 8.078, p = 0.0001); age beta = -0.088, p = 0.1734 (not significant) [pages 8, 9]. Visual acuity was not retained in the preferred model [page 15].
- Falls: 41.6% reported at least one fall in the past 12 months [page 11]; fall counts were similar across severity groups, with the most recurrent fallers in the mild and moderate groups [pages 11, 17].
- Balance or gait problems: 52% overall; by CS group 38.5% (mild), 48.3% (moderate), 78.6% (severe); by VA group 50.0%, 52.5%, 53.6% [page 13]. Mobility problems attributed to vision: 36% overall [page 13]; by CS group 18.0%, 31.0%, 71.4%; by VA group 23.7%, 33.9%, 57.1% [page 13].
- Hardest items: dark environments (Q1, Q2), lighting transitions (Q4, Q5) and uneven surfaces (Q3); Q3 most difficult, Q6 "seeing objects from the side" least difficult in this central-loss sample [page 15].
- Referral to O&M/PT: 31.2% overall; 15.4%, 25.9%, 64.3% by CS group; 21.1%, 28.8%, 50.0% by VA group [page 17]. Referred patients had higher GAL-9 (28.5 SD 6.9 vs 17.6 SD 5.6, U = 373.5, p < 0.00001; group sizes NR) [page 17]. This is the nearest thing in the folder to an item set linked to a rehabilitation decision, but it is a clinician's discretionary referral (with insurance and transport factors) and not an LVR-need reference standard [pages 5 to 7, 17]. No sensitivity or specificity is reported.
- Authors' conclusion on contrast: CS "is associated more strongly and is more discriminative of balance and mobility difficulties than visual acuity" [page 18]; they say a central-field-loss-specific mobility questionnaire may be needed [pages 2 to 3, 20].
- Field: no visual field in the sense of glaucoma (no MD, no Esterman). Field data are California Central Visual Field Test scotoma sizes [page 4].
- Glaucoma-specific? The instrument was developed for glaucoma/peripheral loss; this study contains no glaucoma participants.

## 4. APEDS-VFQ: Gothwal and Bagga 2012 (ID 5)

Sample: 351 visually impaired adults referred to an Indian vision rehabilitation centre, mean age 43.3 (SD 17.8); better-eye acuity mean 0.83 (SD 0.42) logMAR; 60 (17%) had acuity of 20/60 or better and these "had restricted visual fields (less than 20 degrees in better eye)" [pages 2, 3]. Glaucoma is not identified as a subgroup. Visual field was not measured as a variable.

Items (16; framing "How much difficulty do you have in...") [page 2]. Field, peripheral, glare, contrast and mobility content:
- 6 "Seeing objects in bright light due to glare"; 7 "Seeing objects in poorly lit surroundings"; 8 "Adjusting from darkness to bright light"; 9 "Adjusting from bright light to darkness".
- 12 "Noticing objects off to the side, when you are walking and looking straight ahead" (the peripheral item); 13 "Noticing objects on the floor near you, when you are walking around the house and looking straight ahead"; 14 "Reaching for an object, because it is farther or closer than you thought"; 15 "Climbing up or down steps because they were closer or further away than you thought"; 16 "Estimating the distance of a vehicle (a bus coming towards you) while crossing the road"; 11 "Recognizing traffic signals/lights".
- The authors grouped the 16 items into four subsets: resolution, illumination, contrast sensitivity, peripheral vision [page 3]. The text I read does not say which item belongs to which subset, so item-to-subset mapping is NR.

Evidence:
- Two factors: reading and contrast-sensitivity items loaded with logMAR acuity on one factor; peripheral-vision and illumination-dependent items loaded on the other (eigenvalues 2.4 and 1.2) [pages 6 to 7]. The authors read the second dimension as "disrupted by contrast sensitivity and visual field loss" [page 9], but they did not measure contrast sensitivity or field, so this is interpretation, not a test.
- The only correlation: Rasch-scaled score vs presenting binocular VA, r = 0.28, p < 0.0001 [page 7] (the Figure 4 caption says 0.29 [page 9]); n not stated for the pair, Rasch sample 351. The low correlation with acuity is taken as support for two dimensions [page 9].
- No correlation with field, contrast, falls or mobility performance. Not validated as a screening item.

## 5. NEI VFQ-25: Selivanova 2019 (ID 72), Stelmack 2002 (ID 65), Murro 2017 (ID 84), and what the included texts say about its field and driving items

What the included papers actually show:
- Stelmack 2002 (n = 77 legally blind veterans at a blind rehabilitation centre and 51 partially sighted veterans, mean acuity 1.00 and 0.54 logMAR; glaucoma 11.7% and 21.6%) [page 7]. The three NEI VFQ-25 driving items (questions 15, 16 and A10) were dropped from analysis because "very few of the patients were allowed to drive" [page 9]. The authors say that only three items concern mobility: "going down steps, stairs, or curbs in dim light or at night (item 9); noticing objects off to the side while you are walking along (item 10); and taking part in active sports or other outdoor activities (item A7)" [page 17]. None changed in difficulty after rehabilitation, even though the centre teaches mobility, and the authors wrote "It is possible that the wrong questions were asked" [page 17]. No field or contrast statistic. The study is about responsiveness to rehabilitation, not screening.
- Selivanova 2019 (n = 109 with pre and post data; mean age 68.5; glaucoma 13 [11.9%]; field loss central 61.5%, central and peripheral 26.6%, peripheral only 2.75%, hemianopia 4.59%, none 4.59%) [pages 3, 4]. The driving subscale of the NEI VFQ-25 was "not used" [page 2]. The Rasch visual-functioning scale used items 2, 4 to 14 and 19 (item 4 later deleted) [page 3]; item wording NR. The predictors of change: baseline contrast sensitivity (Pelli-Robson) OR about 0.97 to 0.99 per unit, all CIs spanning 1 (for example NEI functional 0.99, 0.97 to 1.02) and field loss type [page 5]. This is a change-after-rehabilitation outcome, not a screening result.
- Murro 2017 (n = 16 Stargardt patients, 31 eyes; mean age 34; mean better-eye ETDRS 0.84 logMAR) [pages 1, 2]. Pelli-Robson contrast sensitivity vs NEI VFQ-25 composite: Pearson r = 0.66 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.87) [page 4]; VA letter score r = 0.64 (0.37 to 0.90); MP1 mean sensitivity r = 0.72 (0.55 to 0.89) [page 4]; correlation of the peripheral-vision subscale with the composite r = 0.76 [page 4]. Driving not scored because no one had a licence [page 2]. Peripheral vision was not impaired in all but one patient [page 6], so this is a central-loss sample, not an informative test of the peripheral item.
- Secondary mention only: Massof 2007 cites DeCarlo et al. reporting that current drivers scored higher on the NEI VFQ-25 than non-drivers after correcting for acuity [page 1]. I did not see the primary paper.
- Verdict: the included papers do not support the NEI VFQ-25 peripheral-vision item (item 10) or the night/dim-light item (item 9) as validated screens for LVR need; in two of three included uses the driving items were dropped for lack of drivers.

## 6. Activity Inventory: Massof 2007 (ID 95, driving goal and 21 driving tasks) and Tabrett and Latham 2011 (ID 46)

Massof 2007: 851 consecutive low-vision clinic patients, age 18 to 101, median 75; binocular VA median 20/60, mean 0.54 logMAR (SD 0.45); mean log CS 0.78 (SD 0.56) (Pelli-Robson, measurable in 81%); 12% glaucoma [page 1]. Driving-goal importance and difficulty were rated first; the 21 driving tasks were rated only by those who gave a nonzero difficulty rating (N = 405) [page 3]. Visual field was not measured.
- Driving tasks (Table 4) [page 5], including: navigate in parking lots with ramps; park in the appropriate space; see lane markings; read road signs; merge into traffic; change lanes; maintain distance from the car in front; identify traffic signals; see merging cars; distinguish when a car is stopped on the shoulder; see traffic on interstate; stay in the lane; drive at night; see pedestrians; stop at right place at an intersection; see traffic on city streets and rural roads; read speedometer. Hardest: navigating parking ramps, parking, seeing lane markings, reading signs [page 1]. "Drive at night": item measure -0.23 logits, error 0.05, count 359, infit MnSq 1.11 [page 5] (Table 4 columns are scrambled; verify alignment). "See lane markings" misfit the model (infit z 6.7) [page 5].
- Perceived driving ability (Rasch person measure) correlated with logMAR acuity r = -0.28 (p < 0.001; regression slope -0.49) and with log CS r = 0.35 (p < 0.001; slope 0.83) [pages 1, 6]; pairs NR. Spearman -0.69 with the single overall driving-difficulty rating (p < 0.001) [page 5].
- By overall driving difficulty rating: "not difficult" mean logMAR 0.37 (SD 0.49), mean log CS 1.23 (SD 0.29); "impossible" mean logMAR 0.83 (SD 0.62), log CS 0.87 (SD 0.50); MANOVA P < 0.0001 for both [page 3]. Of 501 who rated driving as at least slightly important, 37% said driving was impossible [page 3]. This is a single global item ("how difficult is driving") with a clear contrast and acuity gradient, but no accuracy statistic.
- Importance: those who rated driving not important had worse acuity (0.88 logMAR) and contrast (0.83 log CS) than those who rated it extremely important (0.62; 1.03); MANOVA P = 0.003 [page 1]. So non-importance can hide need, which matters for a screening item.
- Against screening: authors say there is wide scatter, "other variables besides visual acuity and CS factor into perceived driving ability" [page 7].

Tabrett and Latham 2011: 100 visually impaired adults (39 men), median age 81 (IQR 73 to 86); primary diagnosis macular dysfunction 54 (right eye), optic neuropathy including glaucoma 11, diabetic retinopathy 7, retinal 5, cataract 2, others (per Table 2; right-eye column) [page 3]. Binocular HFA 30-2 SITA-Fast (central 10 degrees and outer ring 10 to 30 degrees mean threshold) and Pelli-Robson binocular CS [page 3]. Activity Inventory (235 tasks, 30 goals, about 35 minutes) [page 2]; the mobility domain wording is NR in this text. Spearman correlations of the AI mobility domain person measure (higher = more ability) with clinical measures (Table 6, re-aligned from a scrambled table) [page 5]:
- Outer ring 10 to 30 degrees mean threshold r = 0.61 (strongest of any vision measure for mobility); central 10 degrees r = 0.49; contrast sensitivity r = 0.47; distance VA r = -0.27; near reading acuity r = -0.30 [page 5]. p-values are given as significance symbols only (Table 6 key: P < 0.01 without symbol, * P < 0.05, ns P > 0.05); the authors note that with 156 comparisons about eight would be significant by chance at P = 0.05 [page 5].
- Mobility stepwise regression (visual function only): outer-ring field beta 0.54 (p < 0.001) and CS beta 0.20 (p < 0.05); total R2 = 0.43 [page 6]. The separate psychosocial regression for mobility (depression, adjustment) gave R2 = 0.32 [page 6]. Acuity was not retained for the mobility domain [page 6].
- This is the strongest direct field-to-mobility self-report evidence in the included texts after Bibby 2007, and it is in a mixed low-vision sample (11 optic neuropathy including glaucoma), not a glaucoma sample. The AI is long (about 35 minutes), not a screening item.

## 7. NVQ-10: Wu 2016 (ID 63)

Sample: 100 people with bilateral intermediate AMD, BCVA 20/40 or better (mean BCVA -0.03 SD 0.09 logMAR), age 50 or older (mean 69.8, SD 7.4), glaucoma excluded; 13% not currently drivers [pages 1, 3]. Not a glaucoma sample; deliberately near-normal acuity, which resembles the "preserved acuity" scenario but with central macular disease [page 4].

Instrument: 10-item Night Vision Questionnaire derived from four NEI VFQ-25 items and six items from a night vision symptom list; items 1 to 4 rate difficulty (none, a little, moderate, extreme, stopped because of eyesight) and items 5 to 10 rate how bothered the person is; items 1 to 3 can be unscorable if the person answers "not currently driving" [page 2]. Full item wording is in online supplementary table S1 and is NR in the text file. The text names only item 5, "vision at night", and item 6, "reading in dim light" [page 3]. Items 1 to 3 are therefore driving-related (by the "not currently driving" option), exact wording NR.

Statistics (univariate linear regression of the Rasch person measure, higher = worse night vision symptoms; n = 100) [page 3]:
- Low luminance deficit (LLVA minus BCVA, letters): beta = 0.067 (95% CI 0.005 to 0.130), p = 0.034 (the only significant predictor).
- BCVA beta = -0.001 (-0.086 to 0.084), p = 0.984; LLVA beta = -0.043 (-0.093 to 0.007), p = 0.090.
- Microperimetry (MAIA, central 6 degree grid): mean sensitivity beta = -0.145 (-0.366 to 0.077), p = 0.197; central 1 degree sensitivity beta = -0.107 (-0.280 to 0.066), p = 0.222 [pages 2, 3].
- Quartiles: median LLD 15.5 letters (IQR 12 to 19) in the fourth quartile vs 12.0 letters (IQR 9 to 15) in the first quartile, p = 0.019 [page 4].
- No peripheral field test, no contrast sensitivity test, no glare test. Not validated as a screening item. The paper reports no accuracy statistic.

## 8. IVI: Selivanova 2019 (ID 72); Gothwal and Bharani 2015 noted only

- IVI mobility and independence subscale items (by number only; wording NR in the text): items 2, 4, 10 to 13 and 16 to 20 [page 3]. Reading: 1, 3, 5 to 9, 14, 15. Well-being: 21 to 28 [page 3]. Item wording would have to come from the IVI development papers, which are not in the folder as text (cited as Lamoureux 2006, 2007) [page 8].
- Change after rehabilitation, mobility subscale: +0.38 logits (Rasch), paired p = .002, Cohen d = 0.31 [page 4].
- Field loss: adjusted odds ratio for an increase in IVI mobility score, hemianopia and peripheral loss vs no field loss 0.82 (95% CI 0.05 to 12.2) (not significant); for IVI well-being 0.008 (0.001 to 0.54) [page 5]. Only eight participants were in the hemianopia/peripheral group [page 6]. Contrast sensitivity (Pelli-Robson) OR for the IVI mobility change 0.98 (0.95 to 1.01) [page 5]. The Table 3 title says "linear regression" but the text says logistic regression and gives odds ratios [pages 4, 5]; flag.
- The authors note the IVI "was designed for patients with poor central vision" and may not detect mobility-related change in people with poor peripheral but good central vision, citing a glaucoma instrument-validity paper (Lamoureux 2007) [page 6].
- Gothwal and Bharani 2015 (not on the assigned list; keyword scan only) uses the IVI mobility subscale as an outcome (effect size 0.45) [page 1] and gives no field or contrast correlation that I found.
- Not validated as a screening item. Outcome (responsiveness) data only.

## 9. VA LV VFQ-48: Szlyk 2004 (ID 74) and Di Maggio 2020 (ID 105)

Items (identical numbering in both) [Szlyk page 4; Di Maggio page 3] "Is it difficult to...": 18 Find public restrooms; 19 Get around indoors in places you know; 20 Get around outdoors in places you know; 21 Get around in unfamiliar places; 22 Go out at night; 23 Go down steps in dim light (Di Maggio: "Get down steps in dim light"); 24 Adjust to bright light; 25 Cross streets at traffic lights; 26 Use public transportation; 27 Get around in a crowd; 28 Avoid bumping into things; 17 Find something on a crowded shelf; 30 Recognize persons from across the room; 31 Read street signs and store names. The authors describe the 48 items as covering navigation, resolution, peripheral detection, finding and general function [Szlyk page 3]. There is no driving item in the item list [Szlyk page 4].

- Szlyk 2004 (n = 117 veterans and private-sector rehabilitation patients, mean age 72; diagnoses and field not tabulated) [page 3]. No correlation with field, contrast or acuity is reported. In the Rasch training-interest analysis, an 18-item "Mobility" dimension includes Go out at night, Go down steps in dim light, Adjust to bright light, Get around in unfamiliar places, Avoid bumping, Get around in a crowd, Read street signs [page 12]; "Go out at night" was one of nine tasks for which participants most often obtained help from others [page 12]. The authors say interest in mobility vs reading training tracked loss of peripheral vs central vision, but add this "is beyond the scope" of the article and give no statistic [page 14].
- Di Maggio 2020 (Italian version; 131 patients, mean VA 0.91 logMAR, SD 0.42; AMD 58, myopic macular degeneration 27, diabetic retinopathy 21, glaucoma 14, other 21) [pages 1, 2]. Spearman correlation of the mobility subscale with contrast sensitivity (Pelli-Robson; n = 76 with CS data) is the smallest in the table, r = 0.18; mobility subscale vs VA r = 0.24; vs reading acuity -0.34; vs reading speed -0.39; overall score vs CS r = 0.29 [page 4]. Column and row alignment of Table 2 is uncertain in the text file, and the text says the non-significant value is "reading acuity with mobility subscale", which does not match the number I aligned to that cell; verify against the PDF before using. p-values for individual cells NR [page 4]. No visual field measure. Glaucoma subgroup results NR.
- Not validated as a screening item.

## 10. Subramanian and Dickinson 2006 (ID 97): Spatial Localization Questionnaire (SLQ)

Sample: 42 visually impaired adults, mean age 73; 32 bilateral ARM, 4 optic atrophy, 2 ARM with glaucoma, 2 diabetic retinopathy; binocular acuity 0.47 (SD 0.30) logMAR [page 3]. Visual fields were not measured (the authors thought perimetry unreliable with central scotomas) [pages 2, 5].
- SLQ: 26 to 28 items [page 3]; items about space and distance, including "Crossing the road", "Changes in ground level", "Judging the next stair", "Avoiding obstacles", "Walking in a straight line" [page 4].
- Correlation with the SLQ Rasch score (binocular): contrast sensitivity (Pelli-Robson) r = 0.53, p < 0.01; VA (logMAR) r = -0.38, p = 0.01 [page 6]; n = 42 for both [page 6]; no CI. Contrast sensitivity and vernier acuity together explained 42% of variance (r = 0.64, p < 0.0001) [pages 1, 4]. The positive sign is as printed (the higher the Rasch ability, the better the contrast sensitivity).
- No glaucoma-specific result. Not validated as a screening item.

## 11. Gaps across all of the above

- No reference standard of LVR need (referral, eligibility, rehabilitation outcome or expert assessment) was used to calculate sensitivity or specificity for any item in any of the papers listed here.
- Item-level data on field and contrast are absent. Rasch difficulty of individual items is reported (Bibby, Massof, Gothwal), but that says how hard the task is, not whether the item identifies people who need rehabilitation.
- Only Sumi 2003 (147 glaucoma patients) studies glaucoma directly against a perimetric measure, and it uses monocular HFA 30-2 with a Japanese-specific questionnaire without a driving or glare item and without contrast sensitivity.
- Contrast sensitivity vs self-reported function (Agathos, Murro, Massof, Tabrett, Subramanian, Di Maggio) comes from mostly macular disease samples.
- No included paper measures Esterman or an integrated binocular field against any item. The nearest are Bibby (kinetic binocular field, % of sphere) and Tabrett (binocular HFA 30-2 outer ring).
