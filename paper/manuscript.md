# QUESTIONNAIRES TO IDENTIFY GLAUCOMA PATIENTS FOR LOW VISION REHABILITATION

Two questions flag acuity loss in under a minute, but glaucoma also needs a field item.

**AT A GLANCE**

- A single question about reading a posted fast-food menu identified acuity of 20/40 or worse with sensitivity 0.89 and specificity 0.81, and a question about reading keyboard letters identified acuity of 20/200 or worse with sensitivity 0.89 and specificity 0.71, in one research cohort in which the items were both chosen and tested [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28].
- Both items are keyed to acuity; no self-report item in this review has been tested in a glaucoma clinic or against a clinician's judgement that the patient needs rehabilitation, and the only direct test of items against a field defect found sensitivities of 0.20 to 0.50 [@doi:10.1093/ageing/afx202].
- A technician can ask the two items plus a short set of proposed field, night and glare items and combine the answers with the last acuity and visual field; the add-on items and the referral rule are proposals to be piloted, not validated tests.

A familiar scene in a glaucoma clinic: a patient with advanced open-angle glaucoma reads 20/30 with each eye, has stopped driving at night, has given up the newspaper and bumps into people in the supermarket. Nobody has mentioned low vision rehabilitation (LVR), because nobody has asked. Glaucoma is among the most common causes of irreversible visual impairment, and LVR is the main support for patients whose loss cannot be reversed [@doi:10.3390/life16010012]. Yet the referral gap is wide. In a retrospective study at one academic center, most patients with severe open-angle glaucoma qualified as having low vision but only a small minority [VERIFY] were referred for an LVR evaluation, and referral tracked acuity rather than field loss [@doi:10.3390/life16010012]. Among American Glaucoma Society members, the specialists who referred most were those who followed the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) Preferred Practice Pattern (PPP) recommendations [@doi:10.1097/IJG.0000000000000600]. The Vision Rehabilitation PPP asks ophthalmologists to consider rehabilitation for any patient whose vision loss affects function, whatever the acuity [@doi:10.1016/j.ophtha.2022.10.033] [VERIFY: exact PPP referral criteria].

The practical question for a clinic that wants to close the gap is who should be asked, what, and by whom. A technician measures acuity and runs the field; a screening question that fits into that work-up costs nothing. This article reviews the evidence on self-report questionnaires and single screening items that identify adults who may need LVR, examines a two-question acuity screen derived from a 100-item survey [@doi:10.1167/tvst.13.9.20], and proposes a technician work-up protocol for a glaucoma or LVR clinic. It is a systematic search with narrative synthesis, not a meta-analysis.

## HOW GLAUCOMA DISABLES BEYOND ACUITY

Glaucoma spares the acuity chart until late. It takes the peripheral and then the paracentral field, contrast sensitivity and tolerance of glare and dim light first, and those losses drive the disability patients report. In 147 Japanese glaucoma patients who completed a 30-item visual disability questionnaire, the total disability index correlated with better-eye mean deviation at r = -0.64; the walking and going-out domains, which hold items such as "Do you fail to see people or cars approaching you from the side?", correlated with mean deviation at r = -0.61 to -0.63 [VERIFY: domain values read from a column-scrambled table] [@doi:10.1016/S0161-6420(02)01742-6]. In older adults with glaucoma, self-rated reading ability fell with field loss, and patients read less often than glaucoma suspects did [@doi:10.1167/iovs.14-14138]. In 100 low vision patients who completed the Activity Inventory, the mobility measure tracked the outer 10 to 30 degrees of the Humphrey field (rho = 0.61) far better than acuity (rho = -0.27) [@doi:10.1167/iovs.10-7055], and in a 35-item mobility questionnaire the Rasch measure correlated with the binocular kinetic field at r = -0.762 but with high-contrast acuity at only r = 0.41 [@doi:10.1111/j.1444-0938.2007.00120.x].

The legal definitions reflect this. In Canada, as in the United States, legal blindness is a best-corrected acuity of 20/200 or worse in the better eye or a visual field of 20 degrees or less, so a glaucoma patient can be legally blind with normal acuity [@healthlinkbc-legal-blindness; @doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. Any screen that keys only to acuity will, by construction, miss that patient. This is the central tension of the article: the best-validated brief items are acuity items, and the patients a glaucoma clinic most needs to find are often not acuity-impaired.

## FULL-LENGTH QUESTIONNAIRES

The questionnaires used to measure LVR need and outcome are mostly long (Table 1). The National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire (NEI VFQ-25) has 25 items in 12 subscales plus an optional 14-item appendix [@doi:10.1001/archopht.119.7.1050; @pmid:12202503]. The Veterans Affairs Low Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire (VA LV VFQ-48) asks four questions about each of 48 activities by trained telephone interviewers and takes 25 to 35 minutes [@doi:10.1167/iovs.05-1319]. The Activity Inventory is an adaptive interview of 50 goals and hundreds of tasks scored by Rasch analysis [@doi:10.1167/iovs.06-0566]. The Impact of Vision Impairment questionnaire (IVI) has 28 items in three Rasch-scored subscales [@doi:10.1097/OPX.0000000000001327]. The Low Vision Quality-of-Life Questionnaire (LVQOL) is the shortest of the general instruments at 25 summed items and 5 to 10 minutes [@doi:10.1016/S0002-9394(00)00610-3].

Three instruments were built with field loss in mind. The Glaucoma Quality of Life-15 (GQL-15) has peripheral-vision and dark-adaptation-and-glare factors by design [@doi:10.1097/00061198-200304000-00009], and its nine-item Rasch-derived short form, the Glaucoma Activity Limitation-9 (GAL-9), was chosen in one maculopathy clinic as an in-consultation screen because of its mobility scenarios and short length [@doi:10.1016/j.ajo.2025.08.051]. The Andhra Pradesh Eye Disease Study questionnaire (APEDS-VFQ) groups its 16 items by functional demand, including contrast, illumination and peripheral vision [@doi:10.3109/09286586.2012.712192]. None of the three has accuracy data against LVR need, and no GQL-15 study met our criteria.

How well validated are they? We rated every measurement-property result with the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) criteria and found nothing better than "doubtful" quality for any property of any instrument, mainly because no study pre-specified hypotheses or tested unidimensionality [@doi:10.1007/s11136-017-1765-4]. The results rated sufficient rest on single studies: LVQOL test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation 0.72) and responsiveness (6.8-point improvement after rehabilitation) [@doi:10.1016/S0002-9394(00)00610-3]; VA LV VFQ-48 responsiveness (effect size 1.9 after inpatient rehabilitation) [@doi:10.1167/iovs.05-1319]; and IVI responsiveness (reading subscale effect size 0.50) [@doi:10.1097/OPX.0000000000001327]. Correlations with acuity were moderate at best: 0.50 for the LVQOL [@doi:10.1016/S0002-9394(00)00610-3], 0.28 for the APEDS-VFQ [@doi:10.3109/09286586.2012.712192], and -0.014 for the NEI VFQ-25 composite in 184 LVR clients [@doi:10.1097/OPX.0b013e31812f5f24]. Where a field or contrast measure was taken, the questionnaire tracked it at least as well as acuity [@doi:10.1167/iovs.10-7055; @doi:10.1016/S0161-6420(02)01742-6; @doi:10.1111/j.1444-0938.2007.00120.x].

These instruments measure outcome, not need, and only the LVQOL, GAL-9 and GQL-15 are short enough for a technician work-up (Table 2).

## WHERE THE TWO QUESTIONS CAME FROM

The Legge group built a 100-item survey of yes-or-no questions about recognizing everyday objects at fixed distances, calibrated each item with item-response theory, and showed that the ability estimated from the whole survey correlated with measured acuity at r = -0.73 in 385 adults recruited across the acuity range at four US sites [@doi:10.1167/tvst.13.9.20]. Because item difficulty maps linearly onto logMAR, each item is in effect a one-question acuity test at a set threshold.

In the companion paper, the same 385 participants were used to ask which single items, pairs and triplets best classified low vision, defined as 0.3 logMAR (20/40) or worse, and acuity-defined legal blindness, 1.0 logMAR (20/200) or worse, against binocular habitual-correction acuity [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. The cohort had a mean age of 55.7 years, 57.4% were women, 16.9% had glaucoma, 19.2% had macular degeneration, and 68.6% had acuity worse than 0.3 logMAR [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. The two winners were Q97, "In a fast-food restaurant, can you usually read the posted menus?", for low vision, and Q30, "When seated in front of a computer, are you able to read the letters on the keyboard keys?", for legal blindness; a "No" answer is a positive screen [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28].

## HOW ACCURATE ARE THEY?

The menu item had sensitivity 0.89 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.85-0.92) and specificity 0.81 (95% CI, 0.73-0.87) for acuity of 0.3 logMAR or worse; the keyboard item had sensitivity 0.89 (95% CI, 0.80-0.94) and specificity 0.71 (95% CI, 0.66-0.76) for acuity of 1.0 logMAR or worse (Table 3, Figure 2) [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. The paper's area-under-the-curve (AUC) values of 0.85 and 0.80 are the mean of sensitivity and specificity at a single cut point, not areas under a curve, as the authors state; the confidence intervals are ours, from reconstructed 2 x 2 tables [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28].

Adding a second item helped a little. The best pair for low vision, the menu item plus a newspaper item, reached sensitivity 0.86 and specificity 0.88, and the best pair for legal blindness, the keyboard item plus a street-sign item, reached 0.88 and 0.77; a third item added nothing [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. A pair is positive only on "No" to both, so it always trades sensitivity for specificity. The whole 100-item survey, scored as predicted acuity, was less accurate than its single best item (sensitivity 0.94, specificity 0.66 for low vision) [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28].

Three caveats. First, the menu and keyboard items were never evaluated together: each appears only in the table for its own threshold, so a clinic that asks both is running two one-item tests, and the pair statistics belong to other pairs [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. Second, the items were selected by ranking all 100 items by accuracy in the same 385 people that supply the reported accuracy, with no hold-out or external sample; picking the best of 100 noisy estimates inflates the winner by an unknown amount, so every figure here is a derivation-sample estimate that should be expected to fall on replication [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28; @doi:10.7326/0003-4819-155-8-201110180-00009]. Third, predictive values depend on prevalence: the paper's positive predictive value of 0.93 for low vision comes from a sample in which 68.6% were acuity-impaired and will not transfer to a glaucoma clinic where most patients read 20/40 or better [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. On the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool, the study is at high risk of bias in the index-test domain and unclear in the other three, with high applicability concerns (Figure 4) [@doi:10.7326/0003-4819-155-8-201110180-00009]. We rate the certainty of the two accuracy estimates as low, and a "Yes" to either question is never proof that rehabilitation is not needed.

## OTHER BRIEF SCREENERS

Few comparators exist, none better founded (Table 3). In a Quebec community sample aged 65 and over, a positive answer to either of two Statistics Canada survey questions, trouble reading ordinary newsprint or trouble seeing a face at 12 feet with usual correction, had sensitivity 0.83 (95% CI, 0.52-0.98) and specificity 0.83 (95% CI, 0.71-0.92) for presenting acuity of 6/12 or worse; but only 65 of 144 eligible people were examined and verification depended on the answers, so the authors warn that the estimates cannot be applied to an unselected population [@doi:10.1097/00006324-200207000-00009]. The seven-item Visual Impairment as a Risk for Falls Questionnaire, developed in 89 older patients from enriched optometry clinics, was sensitive (0.88; 95% CI, 0.77-0.95) but unspecific (0.48; 95% CI, 0.30-0.67) for any charted impairment, and its three field-oriented items reached sensitivities of only 0.20 to 0.50 against a peripheral field defect [@doi:10.1093/ageing/afx202]. A Spanish LVQOL total score at a cut-off of 105 or 106 separated 170 visually impaired patients from 195 healthy volunteers with sensitivity 0.954 and specificity 0.918, an easier task than finding, among clinic patients, those who need help [@doi:10.1016/j.optom.2021.01.004].

Two relevant papers could not be retrieved in time: a screening tool for reduced habitual vision, defined to include field defects, in hospital rehabilitation inpatients [@doi:10.1097/OPX.0000000000002236], and the concordance between self-reported visual difficulty and measured impairment in the National Health and Aging Trends Study [@doi:10.1016/j.ophtha.2024.06.009]. Across every brief screener we found, specificity was the stable property; sensitivity was high only where the reference was acuity and the sample enriched (Figure 3).

## WILL IT WORK FOR GLAUCOMA?

For the patients it was designed to find, probably; for the patients a glaucoma clinic most needs to find, not on its own. The authors say so themselves: their items "do not detect legal blindness due to visual-field loss alone" and "may misclassify individuals with preserved acuity but severely constricted visual fields," and they name field-oriented items as future work [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. No included study tested any questionnaire or item against a clinician's judgement that the patient needs LVR; every accuracy figure in this review is against measured acuity or a charted composite. So "screen positive" means "acuity-impaired," not "needs rehabilitation."

Further assumptions travel with the items. They were answered inside the 100-item survey, by a mode the paper does not state, and the authors warn that performance "might differ if administered independently" [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. They assume familiarity with fast-food menus and keyboards and were tested in English only; the authors ask for cultural and linguistic adaptation before use elsewhere [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. Self-report of peripheral loss is poor: half or more of the patients with a field defect in the falls-risk study answered "No" to the questions aimed at it [@doi:10.1093/ageing/afx202].

Which items could be added for glaucoma? Nothing in our set has item-level accuracy against any reference, so every candidate is a pilot item, not a recommendation. The strongest evidence is domain-level, from the one glaucoma sample with perimetry: the walking and going-out domains of the Japanese questionnaire, which hold the side-approach item and "Do you feel uneasy going out at night because of your visual problems?", correlated with mean deviation at r = -0.61 and r = -0.63 [VERIFY: scrambled table] [@doi:10.1016/S0161-6420(02)01742-6]. The GAL-9 offers English equivalents for side, night and dim-light scenarios, and its total score correlated with contrast sensitivity at rho = -0.62 (95% CI, -0.72 to -0.49), but in 125 maculopathy patients with glaucoma excluded [@doi:10.1016/j.ajo.2025.08.051]. A glare item is the weakest proposal: "walking in high glare areas" was the hardest of 35 mobility items in 30 low vision patients, and no included paper tested glare against an item [@doi:10.1111/j.1444-0938.2007.00120.x]. A global mobility question, "Do you have mobility problems because of your vision?", was answered yes by 18.0%, 31.0% and 71.4% of patients with mild, moderate and severe contrast loss [@doi:10.1016/j.ajo.2025.08.051].

## PUTTING IT INTO PRACTICE

The Box is what we propose a technician should do while the patient is in the chair. The two acuity items carry the only accuracy evidence and take under a minute. The add-on items cover what the acuity items cannot see and are worded as closely as possible to the items that showed the strongest field and contrast signal, though none has been tested as a screen. The chart supplies the last acuity and field. The referral rule is deliberately inclusive, because an unnecessary LVR consultation costs little and a missed one costs years of avoidable disability. Everything in the Box except the two single-item accuracies is untested, so a clinic adopting it should treat the first months as a pilot: record the answers, the field and acuity, and the eventual LVR disposition, and compare them.

## HOW WE SEARCHED

We searched PubMed and Scopus on 4 October 2026 (2000 onward, English or French) for adult studies of self-report questionnaires or screening items evaluated against measured vision, a full-length instrument or clinician-judged LVR need; the strings are in the supplement. The searches identified 166 records (PubMed, 78; Scopus, 88), and 8 further records came from sources known to the authors. After 39 duplicates were removed, 135 records were screened and 81 excluded; 54 reports were sought, 6 could not be retrieved, 48 were assessed and 17 excluded, leaving 31 included reports (Figure 1), plus the survey development paper, which shares its cohort with the screening paper and counts with it as one dataset. One reviewer screened; a second will check a 20% sample. Data extraction, QUADAS-2 and COSMIN assessments were done per study and tabulated without pooling, following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis guideline, because no comparison had more than one independent accuracy dataset and thresholds and reference standards differed in every study [@doi:10.1136/bmj.l6890; @doi:10.7326/0003-4819-155-8-201110180-00009; @doi:10.1007/s11136-017-1765-4]. Certainty was rated with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach for test accuracy, without a pooled estimate.

**Use of artificial intelligence.** We used Anthropic large language models (Claude Fable 5.1, `claude-fable-5-1`, and Claude Sonnet 5.5, `claude-sonnet-5-5`; October 2026) to test the search strings, produce advisory screening suggestions recorded separately from the reviewer's decisions, convert PDFs to text, produce first-pass data extraction and draft QUADAS-2 and COSMIN assessments, write and run the analysis scripts, and draft this text. ## FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The study the field needs is straightforward: give the two acuity items and a short set of field, night and glare items to consecutive glaucoma clinic patients, independently of any longer survey, and compare the answers with the same visit's acuity, the better-eye mean deviation or an Esterman binocular field, contrast sensitivity, and a masked LVR clinician's judgement of need. That would supply the four things missing from every study here: external validation, a glaucoma population, a field reference and a need reference. Until then, the two items in the Box are the best-evidenced brief screen available, and the add-on items are a reasoned guess.

## CONCLUSION

A glaucoma clinic can screen for LVR need during the technician work-up with two yes-or-no questions that have derivation-sample sensitivity near 0.9 for acuity loss [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28], provided it also asks about the side, the night and the glare, and reads the last field. The questions find the patients who cannot see the chart; the clinic still has to find the patients who can.

**BOX. Proposed technician screen for low vision rehabilitation need in a glaucoma clinic (a proposal to pilot; only step 1 has accuracy evidence).**

**Step 1. Ask the two acuity items, verbatim** [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]**:**

1. "In a fast-food restaurant, can you usually read the posted menus?" Yes / No
2. "When seated in front of a computer, are you able to read the letters on the keyboard keys?" Yes / No

A "No" to item 1 is a positive screen for acuity of 20/40 or worse (derivation-sample sensitivity 0.89, specificity 0.81); a "No" to item 2 is a positive screen for acuity of 20/200 or worse (sensitivity 0.89, specificity 0.71) [@doi:10.1167/iovs.67.1.28]. The two items have not been tested together. Skip or adapt the items for patients unfamiliar with fast-food menus or keyboards, and record that the item was adapted.

**Step 2. Ask the proposed glaucoma add-on items (untested as screening items; wording adapted from the cited instruments):**

3. "Do you fail to see people or cars approaching you from the side?" Yes / No [@doi:10.1016/S0161-6420(02)01742-6; @doi:10.1016/j.ajo.2025.08.051]
4. "Do you feel uneasy going out at night, or in dim light, because of your vision?" Yes / No [@doi:10.1016/S0161-6420(02)01742-6; @doi:10.1016/j.ajo.2025.08.051]
5. "Is walking in bright glare difficult because of your vision?" Yes / No [@doi:10.1111/j.1444-0938.2007.00120.x]
6. "Do you have mobility problems because of your vision?" Yes / No [@doi:10.1016/j.ajo.2025.08.051]

**Step 3. Pull from the chart:** the last better-eye corrected acuity; the last visual field (better-eye mean deviation, and whether the central 10 degrees or the inferior field is involved); contrast sensitivity if measured.

**Referral rule (proposed, untested):** offer an LVR evaluation when any of the following holds: "No" to item 1 or item 2; "Yes" to any of items 3 to 6 together with a documented glaucomatous field defect in the better eye [VERIFY: field threshold to be set by the clinic]; better-eye acuity of 20/40 or worse [VERIFY: align with the AAO PPP referral criteria]; or a field of 20 degrees or less in the better eye, the legal-blindness criterion [@healthlinkbc-legal-blindness]. Record all six answers in the chart whether or not a referral follows, so that the pilot can be audited.

**Time:** the two acuity items take under a minute (the source study did not time them); the six items and the chart check should fit within the technician history [untested].

**What this box cannot claim:** the single-item accuracies come from one enriched research cohort in which the items were both chosen and tested; nothing here has been tested in a glaucoma clinic or against a clinician's judgement of rehabilitation need.
