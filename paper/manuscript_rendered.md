# QUESTIONNAIRES TO IDENTIFY GLAUCOMA PATIENTS FOR LOW VISION REHABILITATION

Two questions flag acuity loss in under a minute, but glaucoma also needs a field item.

BY [AUTHOR 1], [DEGREE]; [AUTHOR 2], [DEGREE]; AND [AUTHOR 3], [DEGREE]

A familiar scene in a glaucoma clinic: a patient with advanced open-angle glaucoma reads 20/30 with each eye, has stopped driving at night, has given up the newspaper and bumps into people in the supermarket. Nobody has mentioned low vision rehabilitation (LVR), because nobody has asked. Glaucoma is among the most common causes of irreversible visual impairment, and LVR is the main support for patients whose loss cannot be reversed.^1^ Yet the referral gap is wide. Of 522 patients with severe open-angle glaucoma at one academic center, 88% qualified as having low vision but 14 (2.7%) were referred for an LVR evaluation, and referral was associated with acuity, not with field loss [VERIFY: abstract values; full text not retrieved].^1^ Among American Glaucoma Society members, following the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) Preferred Practice Pattern (PPP) referral recommendations went with 2.5 times the odds of being a high referrer, yet only 22% followed them [VERIFY: abstract values; full text not retrieved].^2^ The Vision Rehabilitation PPP asks ophthalmologists to consider rehabilitation for any patient whose vision loss affects function, whatever the acuity [VERIFY: exact PPP referral criteria].^3^

The practical question for a clinic is who should be asked, what, and by whom. A technician measures acuity and runs the field; a screening question that fits into that work-up costs nothing. This article reviews the evidence on self-report questionnaires and single screening items that identify adults who may need LVR, examines a two-question acuity screen derived from a 100-item survey,^4^ and proposes a technician work-up protocol for a glaucoma or LVR clinic. It is a systematic search with narrative synthesis, not a meta-analysis.

## HOW GLAUCOMA DISABLES BEYOND ACUITY

Glaucoma spares the acuity chart until late. It takes the peripheral and then the paracentral field, contrast sensitivity and tolerance of glare and dim light first, and those losses drive the disability patients report. In 147 Japanese glaucoma patients who completed a 30-item visual disability questionnaire, the total disability index correlated with better-eye mean deviation at r = -0.64; the walking and going-out domains, which hold items such as "Do you fail to see people or cars approaching you from the side?", correlated with mean deviation at r = -0.61 and -0.63.^5^ In older adults with glaucoma, self-rated reading ability fell with field loss and with contrast loss, and patients read less often than glaucoma suspects did.^6^ In 100 low vision patients who completed the Activity Inventory, the mobility measure tracked the outer 10 to 30 degrees of the Humphrey field (rho = 0.61) far better than acuity (rho = -0.27),^7^ and in a 35-item mobility questionnaire the Rasch measure correlated with the binocular kinetic field at r = -0.762 but with high-contrast acuity at only r = 0.41.^8^

The legal definitions reflect this. In Canada, as in the United States, legal blindness is a best-corrected acuity of 20/200 or worse in the better eye or a visual field of 20 degrees or less, so a glaucoma patient can be legally blind with normal acuity.^9,10^

**Clinical Takeaways.** The disability glaucoma patients report tracks their field and contrast loss, not the chart, and a patient who reads 20/30 can be legally blind on field alone. Any screen keyed only to acuity will, by construction, miss that patient; that is the central tension of this article.

## FULL-LENGTH QUESTIONNAIRES

The questionnaires used to measure LVR need and outcome are mostly long (Table 1). The National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire (NEI VFQ-25) has 25 items in 12 subscales plus an optional 14-item appendix.^11,12^ The Veterans Affairs Low Vision Visual Functioning Questionnaire (VA LV VFQ-48) asks four questions about each of 48 activities, by trained telephone interviewers, in 25 to 35 minutes.^13^ The Activity Inventory is an adaptive interview of 50 goals, each with its own tasks, scored by Rasch analysis.^14^ The Impact of Vision Impairment questionnaire (IVI) has 28 items in three Rasch-scored subscales.^15^ The Low Vision Quality-of-Life Questionnaire (LVQOL) is the shortest of the general instruments at 25 summed items and 5 to 10 minutes.^16^

Three instruments were built with field loss in mind. The Glaucoma Quality of Life-15 (GQL-15) has peripheral-vision and dark-adaptation-and-glare factors by design,^17^ and its nine-item Rasch-derived short form, the Glaucoma Activity Limitation-9 (GAL-9), was chosen as an in-consultation screen in one maculopathy clinic for its mobility scenarios and brevity.^18^ The Andhra Pradesh Eye Disease Study questionnaire (APEDS-VFQ) groups its 16 items by functional demand, including contrast, illumination and peripheral vision.^19^ None of the three has accuracy data against LVR need, and no GQL-15 study met our criteria.

How well validated are they? We rated every measurement-property result with the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN) criteria and found no result of better than "doubtful" methodological quality for any property of any instrument, mainly because no study pre-specified hypotheses or tested unidimensionality.^20^ The few results rated sufficient each rest on one study: LVQOL test-retest reliability (intraclass correlation 0.72) and responsiveness,^16^ and the responsiveness of the VA LV VFQ-48^13^ and the IVI^15^ after rehabilitation. Correlations with acuity ranged widely, from 0.74 for the total disability index in the Japanese glaucoma sample^5^ and 0.50 for the LVQOL^16^ down to 0.28 for the APEDS-VFQ^19^ and -0.014 for the NEI VFQ-25 composite in 184 LVR clients.^21^

**Clinical Takeaways.** These instruments measure outcome, not need, and none has been tested as a screen; the validation evidence behind each is of low certainty at best. Only the LVQOL, GQL-15 and GAL-9 are short enough for a technician work-up (Table 2), and only the last two were built for field loss.

## WHERE THE TWO QUESTIONS CAME FROM

The Legge group built a 100-item survey of yes-or-no questions about recognizing everyday objects at fixed distances, calibrated each item with item-response theory, and showed that the ability estimated from the whole survey correlated with measured acuity at r = -0.73 in 385 adults recruited across the acuity range at four US sites.^4^ Because item difficulty maps linearly onto logMAR, each item is in effect a one-question acuity test at a set threshold.

In the companion paper, the same 385 participants were used to ask which single items, pairs and triplets best classified low vision, defined as 0.3 logMAR (20/40) or worse, and acuity-defined legal blindness, 1.0 logMAR (20/200) or worse, against binocular habitual-correction acuity.^10^ The cohort had a mean age of 55.7 years, 57.4% were women, 16.9% had glaucoma, 19.2% had macular degeneration, and 68.6% had acuity worse than 0.3 logMAR.^10^ The two winners were Q97, "In a fast-food restaurant, can you usually read the posted menus?", for low vision, and Q30, "When seated in front of a computer, are you able to read the letters on the keyboard keys?", for legal blindness; a "No" answer is a positive screen.^10^

## HOW ACCURATE ARE THEY?

The menu item had sensitivity 0.89 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.85–0.92) and specificity 0.81 (95% CI, 0.73–0.87) for acuity of 0.3 logMAR or worse; the keyboard item had sensitivity 0.89 (95% CI, 0.80–0.94) and specificity 0.71 (95% CI, 0.66–0.76) for acuity of 1.0 logMAR or worse (Table 3, Figure 2).^10^ The paper's area-under-the-curve (AUC) values of 0.85 and 0.80 are the mean of sensitivity and specificity at a single cut point, not areas under a curve, as the authors state; the confidence intervals are ours, from reconstructed 2 x 2 tables.^10^

Adding a second item helped a little. The best pair for low vision, the menu item plus a newspaper item, reached sensitivity 0.86 and specificity 0.88, and the best pair for legal blindness, the keyboard item plus a street-sign item, reached 0.88 and 0.77; a third item added nothing.^10^ A pair is positive only on "No" to both, so it trades sensitivity for specificity. The whole 100-item survey, scored as predicted acuity, was less accurate than its single best item (sensitivity 0.94, specificity 0.66 for low vision).^10^

Three caveats. First, the menu and keyboard items were never evaluated together, so a clinic that asks both is running two one-item tests.^10^ Second, the items were selected by ranking all 100 items by accuracy in the same 385 people that supply the reported accuracy, with no hold-out or external sample; picking the best of 100 noisy estimates inflates the winner, so every figure here is a derivation-sample estimate that should fall on replication.^10,22^ Third, predictive values depend on prevalence: the positive predictive value of 0.93 that the paper reports for one-item low vision screening comes from a sample in which 68.6% were acuity-impaired.^10^ In a glaucoma clinic where one patient in ten reads 20/40 or worse, the same sensitivity and specificity would give a positive predictive value near 0.34 (our arithmetic). On the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies 2 (QUADAS-2) tool, the study is at high risk of bias in the index-test domain and unclear in the other three, with high applicability concerns (Figure 4).^22^ We rate the certainty of the two accuracy estimates as low.

**Clinical Takeaways.** A "No" to the menu question flags acuity of 20/40 or worse, and a "No" to the keyboard question flags 20/200 or worse, each with derivation-sample sensitivity near 0.9. Expect lower figures on replication and many false positives where most patients see well, and treat a "Yes" as no proof that rehabilitation is unneeded.

## OTHER BRIEF SCREENERS

Comparators are few, and none is better founded (Table 3). Global self-rating questions from population surveys are specific but insensitive. The American Community Survey question, "Are you blind, or do you have serious difficulty seeing, even when wearing glasses?", had sensitivity 0.70 and specificity 0.88 for legal blindness but sensitivity 0.43 for low vision in 438 clinic patients, as Wu and colleagues summarize;^10^ in the 2022 National Health and Aging Trends Study, self-reported visual difficulty had sensitivity 0.16 and specificity 0.96 for objective impairment of distance acuity, near acuity or contrast in 4,999 older adults [VERIFY: abstract values; full text not retrieved].^23^ Task-specific items do better. In a Quebec community sample aged 65 and over, a positive answer to either of two Statistics Canada survey questions, trouble reading ordinary newsprint or trouble seeing a face at 12 feet with usual correction, had sensitivity 0.83 (95% CI, 0.52–0.98) and specificity 0.83 (95% CI, 0.71–0.92) for presenting acuity of 6/12 or worse; but only 65 of 144 eligible people were examined and verification depended on the answers, so the authors warn against generalizing the estimates.^24^ The seven-item Visual Impairment as a Risk for Falls Questionnaire, developed in 89 older patients from enriched optometry clinics, was sensitive (0.88; 95% CI, 0.77–0.95) but unspecific (0.48; 95% CI, 0.30–0.67) for any charted impairment, and its three field-oriented items had sensitivities of only 0.20 to 0.50 against a peripheral field defect.^25^ Among 112 inpatients of hospital rehabilitation units, 48.7% of whom had reduced habitual vision defined to include any field defect, three self-report questions (happiness with vision, difficulty reading a newspaper, difficulty distinguishing facial expressions) reached sensitivity 0.74 [VERIFY: abstract values; full text not retrieved].^26^ A Spanish LVQOL total score at a cut-off of 105 or 106 separated 170 visually impaired patients from 195 healthy volunteers with sensitivity 0.954 and specificity 0.918, an easier task than finding, among clinic patients, those who need help.^27^ Outside eye care, in 461 stroke survivors the symptom item "reading problems" had sensitivity 0.84 and specificity 0.75 against a specialist eye examination, as re-tabulated by the European Stroke Organisation guideline.^28^

Across every brief screener we found, specificity was the stable property; sensitivity was high only where the reference was acuity and the items were derived in, or the sample enriched with, acuity-impaired people (Figure 2).

## WILL IT WORK FOR GLAUCOMA?

For the patients it was designed to find, probably; for the patients a glaucoma clinic most needs to find, not on its own. The authors say so themselves: their items "do not detect legal blindness due to visual-field loss alone" and "may misclassify individuals with preserved acuity but severely constricted visual fields," and they name field-oriented items as future work.^10^ No included study tested any questionnaire or item against a clinician's judgement that the patient needs LVR; every accuracy figure in this review is against measured acuity, a charted composite or, once, a field defect (Figure 3). So "screen positive" means "acuity-impaired," not "needs rehabilitation."

Further assumptions travel with the items. They were answered inside the 100-item survey, by a mode the paper does not state, and the authors warn that performance "might differ if administered independently"; they assume familiarity with fast-food menus and keyboards, were tested in English only, and need cultural and linguistic adaptation before use elsewhere.^10^ Self-report of peripheral loss is poor: half or more of the patients with a field defect in the falls-risk study answered "No" to the questions aimed at it.^25^

Which items could be added for glaucoma? Nothing in our set has item-level accuracy against any reference, so every candidate is a pilot item, not a recommendation. The strongest evidence is domain-level, from the one glaucoma sample with perimetry: the walking and going-out domains of the Japanese questionnaire, which hold the side-approach item and "Do you feel uneasy going out at night because of your visual problems?", correlated with mean deviation at r = -0.61 and r = -0.63.^5^ The GAL-9 offers English equivalents for side, night and dim-light scenarios, and its total score correlated with contrast sensitivity at rho = -0.62 (95% CI, -0.72 to -0.49), but in 125 maculopathy patients with glaucoma excluded.^18^ A glare item is the weakest proposal: "walking in high glare areas" was the hardest of 35 mobility items in 30 low vision patients, and no included paper tested glare against an item.^8^ A global mobility question, "Do you have mobility problems because of your vision?", drew a yes from 18.0%, 31.0% and 71.4% of patients with mild, moderate and severe contrast loss.^18^

**Clinical Takeaways.** The two questions will find glaucoma patients whose acuity has fallen and will miss those whose loss is field, contrast or night vision alone. Ask the side, night and glare items as pilot questions, and audit the answers against the field and the referral.

## PUTTING IT INTO PRACTICE

The Box is what we propose a technician should do while the patient is in the chair. The two acuity items carry the only accuracy evidence and take under a minute. The add-on items cover what the acuity items cannot see and are worded after the items with the strongest field and contrast signal, though none has been tested as a screen. The referral rule is deliberately inclusive, because an unnecessary LVR consultation costs little and a missed one costs years of avoidable disability. Everything in the Box except the two single-item accuracies is untested, so a clinic adopting it should treat the first months as a pilot: record the answers, the field and acuity, and the eventual LVR disposition, and compare them.

## FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The study the field needs is straightforward: give the two acuity items and a short set of field, night and glare items to consecutive glaucoma clinic patients, independently of any longer survey, and compare the answers with the same visit's acuity, the better-eye mean deviation or an Esterman binocular field, contrast sensitivity, and a masked LVR clinician's judgement of need. That would supply the four things missing from every study here: external validation, a glaucoma population, a field reference and a need reference. Until then, the two items in the Box are the best-evidenced brief screen available, and the add-on items are a reasoned guess.

## CONCLUSION

A glaucoma clinic can screen for LVR need during the technician work-up with two yes-or-no questions that have derivation-sample sensitivity near 0.9 for acuity loss,^10^ provided it also asks about the side, the night and the glare, and reads the last field. The questions find the patients who cannot see the chart; the clinic still has to find the patients who can.

**BOX. Proposed technician screen for low vision rehabilitation need in a glaucoma clinic (a proposal to pilot; only step 1 has accuracy evidence).**

**Step 1. Ask the two acuity items, verbatim**^10^**:**

1. "In a fast-food restaurant, can you usually read the posted menus?" Yes / No
2. "When seated in front of a computer, are you able to read the letters on the keyboard keys?" Yes / No

A "No" to item 1 is a positive screen for acuity of 20/40 or worse (derivation-sample sensitivity 0.89, specificity 0.81); a "No" to item 2 is a positive screen for acuity of 20/200 or worse (sensitivity 0.89, specificity 0.71).^10^ The two items have not been tested together. Skip or adapt the items for patients unfamiliar with fast-food menus or keyboards, and record that the item was adapted.

**Step 2. Ask the proposed glaucoma add-on items (untested as screening items; wording adapted from the cited instruments):**

3. "Do you fail to see people or cars approaching you from the side?" Yes / No^5,18^
4. "Do you feel uneasy going out at night, or in dim light, because of your vision?" Yes / No^5,18^
5. "Is walking in bright glare difficult because of your vision?" Yes / No^8^
6. "Do you have mobility problems because of your vision?" Yes / No^18^

**Step 3. Pull from the chart:** the last better-eye corrected acuity; the last visual field (better-eye mean deviation, and whether the central 10 degrees or the inferior field is involved); contrast sensitivity if measured.

**Referral rule (proposed, untested):** offer an LVR evaluation when any of the following holds: "No" to item 1 or item 2; "Yes" to any of items 3 to 6 together with a documented glaucomatous field defect in the better eye [VERIFY: field threshold to be set by the clinic]; better-eye acuity of 20/40 or worse [VERIFY: align with the AAO PPP referral criteria]; or a field of 20 degrees or less in the better eye, the legal-blindness criterion.^9,10^ Record all six answers in the chart whether or not a referral follows, so that the pilot can be audited.

**Time:** the two acuity items take under a minute (the source study did not time them); the six items and the chart check should fit within the technician history [untested].

**What this box cannot claim:** the single-item accuracies come from one enriched research cohort in which the items were both chosen and tested; nothing here has been tested in a glaucoma clinic or against a clinician's judgement of rehabilitation need.
